models:
  - id: job-demands-resources-model
    name: "Job demands–resources (JD-R) model"
    by: "Evangelia Demerouti, Arnold Bakker, Friedhelm Nachreiner and Wilmar Schaufeli"
    year: "2001; 2007"
    idea: "Job demands drain energy and lead towards burnout; job resources such as control, support and feedback fuel engagement and soften the demands."
    use: "Diagnosing burnout or disengagement in a team, and choosing which demand to cut or which resource to add."
    watch: "So broad it can absorb almost any factor; it sorts causes well but rarely tells you which one matters most."
    cite: "Bakker, A. B. and Demerouti, E. (2007) The Job Demands-Resources model: state of the art. Journal of Managerial Psychology, 22(3)."
  - id: demand-control-support-model
    name: "Demand–control–support model"
    by: "Robert Karasek; support added by Jeffrey Johnson and Ellen Hall"
    year: "1979; 1988"
    idea: "Strain is highest where demands are high and control is low; support from colleagues and managers cushions it."
    use: "Redesigning claims, contact-centre, frontline and shift jobs that combine heavy targets with little say over the work."
    watch: "Demands and control each predict strain well; evidence that control buffers demands, the model's signature claim, is weaker."
    cite: "Karasek, R. A. (1979) Job demands, job decision latitude, and mental strain: implications for job redesign. Administrative Science Quarterly, 24(2)."
  - id: burnout-six-areas-of-worklife
    name: "Burnout and the six areas of worklife"
    by: "Christina Maslach, with Susan Jackson, Wilmar Schaufeli and Michael Leiter"
    year: "1981; 2001"
    idea: "Burnout is exhaustion, cynicism and a falling sense of effectiveness, caused by mismatches in workload, control, reward, community, fairness and values."
    use: "Showing leaders that burnout comes from the job and how it is managed, not from weak individuals."
    watch: "Not a medical diagnosis: WHO's ICD-11 calls it an occupational phenomenon. Never use burnout scores to label individuals."
    cite: "Maslach, C., Schaufeli, W. B. and Leiter, M. P. (2001) Job burnout. Annual Review of Psychology, 52."
  - id: psychosocial-risk-management
    name: "Psychosocial risk management"
    by: "UK Health and Safety Executive; ISO; World Health Organization"
    year: "2004; 2021; 2022"
    idea: "Treat work-related stressors such as demands, control, support, relationships, role and change as hazards to assess and control at source."
    use: "Turning a wellbeing plan from perks into prevention, with owners and actions, as for any safety risk."
    watch: "Risk surveys can become tick-box audits; prevention needs managers with the time and authority to change the work."
    cite: "ISO (2021) ISO 45003: Psychological health and safety at work. Guidelines for managing psychosocial risks; HSE (2004) Management Standards for work-related stress."
  - id: employee-experience-design
    name: "Employee experience (EX) design"
    by: "Jacob Morgan; Josh Plaskoff"
    year: "2017"
    idea: "Design the culture, technology and physical spaces people meet across their journey, concentrating on the moments that shape trust most."
    use: "Choosing the few moments, such as day one or a first illness, where better design changes how people feel about the employer."
    watch: "Becomes 'experience theatre' when journey maps and survey scores improve but the job and the manager stay the same."
    cite: "Morgan, J. (2017) The Employee Experience Advantage. Wiley; Plaskoff, J. (2017) Employee experience: the new human resource management approach. Strategic HR Review."
  - id: intersectionality-crenshaw
    name: "Intersectionality"
    by: "Kimberlé Crenshaw"

    use: "Cutting HR data by combinations, for example women with disabilities, before declaring a process fair."
    watch: "Cross-cuts produce small groups: numbers become unstable and people identifiable, so protect privacy and avoid over-reading."
    cite: "Crenshaw, K. (1989) Demarginalizing the intersection of race and sex. University of Chicago Legal Forum, 1989(1)."

  - id: wi-jdr-resources
    type: knowledge
    q: "A claims team's daily file target can't be cut this quarter. Using the job demands–resources model, which change is most likely to reduce burnout?"
    options:
      - "A meditation app for everyone in the team"
      - "More say over how and when files are handled, plus a weekly check-in with a trained team lead"
      - "A bigger incentive for meeting the target"
      - "A wellness day once a quarter"
    answer: 1
    feedback:
      - "Individual tools can help some people cope, but they don't change the balance of demands and resources in the job."
      - "Correct. Control and support are job resources: they buffer the demand you can't cut and feed engagement."
      - "A bigger incentive raises the stakes of the same demand and can add pressure."
      - "A day off gives brief recovery, but the strain returns on the next working day."
    why: "When you can't reduce a demand, add job resources such as control, support and feedback. They buffer strain and build engagement."
  - id: wi-primary-prevention
    type: knowledge
    q: "Which of these is a primary intervention, one that prevents harm at source?"
    options:
      - "An employee assistance programme (EAP) helpline"
      - "Resilience training for team leads"
      - "Re-setting file targets to match today's more complex claims"
      - "A phased return-to-work plan after sick leave"
    answer: 2
    feedback:
      - "An EAP is support for people once strain has started, so it is secondary or tertiary."
      - "Training helps people cope with the demands. Useful, but it is secondary, not prevention at source."
      - "Correct. Changing the work removes the hazard for everyone, which is primary prevention."
      - "Return-to-work support is tertiary: it helps someone recover after harm."
    why: "Primary interventions change the work, secondary ones help people cope, and tertiary ones treat and support return. Good programmes start with primary."

## Look at the work before the worker

The Hyderabad centre's two proposals are easy to approve and easy to photograph. But look at where the problem sits. Sick leave and exits cluster in the teams whose file targets were set when claims were simpler. That pattern points at the job, not at the people doing it.

The **job demands–resources (JD-R) model** gives HR one frame for wellbeing and engagement together. Every job has *demands*: the sustained effort it asks for, such as workload, time pressure, difficult customers and night shifts. Every job also has *resources*: what helps people do the work and grow, such as control over how they work, a supportive manager, feedback and training. The model sees two processes running side by side.

Two older models sharpen the picture:

- **Karasek's demand–control model** says strain peaks when demands are high and control is low. Johnson and Hall added that support from colleagues and managers cushions it. Claims processing, contact centres and shift work often score badly on all three.
- **Maslach's research on burnout** describes exhaustion, cynicism and a falling sense of effectiveness. It traces burnout to six mismatches between person and job: workload, control, reward, community, fairness and values. The World Health Organization's ICD-11 describes burnout as an occupational phenomenon, not a medical condition. In other words, it belongs to the job.

So the HR business partner's first question isn't "which app?". It is: which demands have grown, which resources have shrunk, and in which teams? Cut sick leave, exits and survey scores by team, target, shift and team lead before you spend anything.

## Prevent at source, then support, then treat

Most Indian wellbeing budgets go on what is easiest to buy: insurance top-ups, apps, yoga sessions and webinars. These help some people, but they sit at the wrong end of the problem.

**Psychosocial risk management** treats stressors at work as hazards, like a wet floor or an unguarded machine. The UK Health and Safety Executive's Management Standards (2004) name six areas to assess: demands, control, support, relationships, role and change. ISO 45003 (2021) gives a management-system version, and the WHO's 2022 guidelines on mental health at work recommend organisational interventions and manager training alongside individual support. Interventions sit at three levels.

</Figure>

The evidence favours the base. Randomised trials of US workplace wellness programmes found little effect on health, medical costs or productivity (Jones, Molitor and Reif, 2019; Song and Baicker, 2019). A large UK survey found that individual offers such as mindfulness, resilience training and apps made no measurable difference, while changes to how work was organised did (Fleming, 2024). It is a single cross-sectional study, so treat it as a pointer, not proof.

### Mental health at work

Mental health needs its own plan, because stigma keeps people quiet, and use of employee assistance programmes (EAPs) is often low in India as a result. Three practices help:

- **Train line managers** to notice changes, start a private conversation and agree adjustments, without trying to diagnose.
- **Make adjustments ordinary:** flexible start times, a break from on-call rotas, a temporary change to targets.
- **Check your group health cover.** The Mental Healthcare Act 2017 requires insurers to cover mental illness on the same basis as physical illness. Mental illness is also one of the disabilities the RPwD Act lists, so your equal opportunity policy should cover it.

<Callout type="note" title="At strategic level (SHRM-SCP, CIPD Level 7)">
Treat psychosocial risk like any other hazard on the risk register: a named owner, controls and a measure the board sees. The largest listed companies already disclose wellbeing spending as a share of revenue under BRSR Core. The sharper question for a board is whether that money went to prevention or to perks.
</Callout>

## Design the moments that matter

At the Hyderabad centre, exit comments keep naming the same few moments: a first week without system access, the first month the full target applies, and coming back from maternity leave to a rotating night roster. That is employee experience (EX) in practice. People judge an employer less by its average day than by a handful of moments when they needed it.

EX design borrows from service design. Jacob Morgan describes three environments to design together: culture, technology and physical space. In practice, HR picks the moments with the highest stakes for trust and redesigns them with the people who live them.

The [employee experience chapter](/handbooks/hr-professional/employee-experience/) lists what good looks like at each stage. Designing the moments well takes four habits:

1. **Choose by evidence.** Start where exit and pulse comments cluster, and where a bad moment costs most trust.
2. **Design with employees.** Walk the journey with five recent joiners or returners before you change anything.
3. **Listen continuously.** Combine an annual census, short pulses, lifecycle surveys at joining and exit, and open comments.
4. **Close the loop.** Tell people what changed because of what they said, within weeks, not at next year's town hall.

Continuous and passive listening raises privacy questions. The DPDP Act's main duties apply from May 2027, so work out the lawful basis for each use now, and don't analyse employees' messages for "sentiment" without telling them and taking advice.

## Inclusion: three cases and many identities

The CEO's women's leadership programme assumes the gap lies in women's skills. Before agreeing, look at who gets promoted and what the rules for team-lead roles require. Inclusion work starts with the system, not with the people it filters out.

CIPD's units 5OS05 and 7OS04 ask for three cases, and a strong argument uses all of them:

Three ideas turn the cases into practice:

- **Intersectionality** (Crenshaw, 1989). Overlapping identities create disadvantage that single-axis data hides. Women and people with disabilities may each be promoted at fair rates while women with disabilities are not.
- **Diversity paradigms** (Thomas and Ely, 1996). Firms treat diversity as fairness and compliance, as access to markets, or as a source of learning. Only the learning paradigm reliably changes how work and decisions are done.
- **Belonging plus uniqueness** (Shore and colleagues, 2011). People feel included when they belong and their differences are valued. Belonging that requires fitting in is assimilation.

The professional bodies now use different words for this work, and you need both vocabularies for the exams.

## Measure inclusion, not just headcount

Headcount by gender is where most Indian dashboards stop. A useful one tracks four things:

- **Representation** by level, function and location.
- **Flows:** hiring, promotion and exit rates for each group. Flows show where people are filtered out.
- **Pay:** gaps in the same grade, and for listed companies the BRSR Core metric of wages paid to women as a share of total wages.
- **Experience:** an inclusion index built from survey items on belonging and uniqueness.

Then cut the key numbers by combinations, with privacy safeguards for small groups. The [pay equity analyser](/toolkit/pay-equity-analyzer/) helps with the pay step.

<Worked title="Worked example: an inclusion dashboard for the claims centre">

All figures are an illustrative example for the 800 claims processors and 14 team leads.

**Step 1: representation.** Women are 55% of processors (440 of 800) but 14% of team leads (2 of 14). That is a gap worth explaining, not yet a cause.

**Step 2: promotion flows, with the four-fifths check.** Count only people eligible for team lead (two years' service, rated "meets" or above), and promotions over the last two years.

| Group | Eligible | Promoted | Rate |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Women | 180 | 2 | 1.1% |
| Men | 150 | 9 | 6.0% |

The ratio is 1.1 ÷ 6.0 = **0.19**. The four-fifths rule, a US rule of thumb that also works as a first screen elsewhere, flags any ratio below 0.80 for investigation. This is far below it.

**Step 3: pay share.** Women's gross wages are ₹15.8 crore of ₹33.5 crore, or **47.2%**, against 52.7% of headcount. Women are concentrated in lower-paid roles and receive less night-shift allowance.

**Step 4: cut by combination.** Exit rates over 12 months look similar by gender (30% for women, 28% for men). Cut by gender and shift together:

| Processors | Staff | Exits | Rate |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Women, day shifts | 320 | 80 | 25% |
| Women, rotating nights | 120 | 54 | **45%** |
| Men, day shifts | 160 | 40 | 25% |
| Men, rotating nights | 200 | 60 | 30% |

The single-axis view hid a hot spot: women on rotating nights leave at almost twice the day-shift rate.

**Step 5: experience.** On a 1–5 scale, belonging is similar (women 3.9, men 4.0), but "I can raise a different view without it counting against me" is 3.1 for women and 3.6 for men. That is the assimilation pattern Shore describes.

**Step 6: read it.** Team-lead roles require rotating nights, which most women returning from maternity leave can't take on. The barrier sits in the role design and roster, not in women's leadership skills. The fix is day-shift team-lead roles, written promotion criteria and a say in shifts on return. Then track the promotion ratio and night-shift exits every quarter.

**Privacy rule.** Report any group smaller than 10 only in aggregate. Here, the 18 employees with disabilities are too few to cut further without identifying people, so review their accommodations one by one instead.

</Worked>

## Where experts disagree

These debates run through CIPD 7OS04 and 7OS06 essays and XLRI case discussions.

- **Wellbeing-washing.** Critics argue that apps, fruit bowls and resilience workshops shift responsibility for structural problems (workload, pay, poor managers) onto individuals. The trial evidence on wellness programmes supports them. Defenders reply that individual support still helps people already struggling.
- **Is engagement new?** Engagement overlaps heavily with satisfaction and commitment (Macey and Schneider, 2008). Guest (2014) called it a management fad built on unitarist assumptions that can intensify work. CIPD increasingly frames the goal as "good work".
- **The business case for diversity.** Consultancy studies linking diversity to profit are correlational, and one prominent series failed to replicate on public data. Ely and Thomas (2020) warn that relying on the business case can backfire. Others reply that leaders won't fund what they can't link to results.
- **Training versus accountability.** Diversity training builds knowledge more than it changes behaviour (Bezrukova and colleagues, 2016). Accountability structures, task forces and mentoring do better (Kalev, Dobbin and Kelly, 2006).
- **Merit or equity.** SHRM's language of merit and access appeals in a US legal climate hostile to preferences. Critics say dropping "equity" hides structural barriers. The paradox-of-meritocracy research suggests that firms stressing merit can show more bias (Castilla and Benard, 2010).

A good answer names the model, shows where it fits, states its limit, and brings in a second source of evidence to cover the gap.

## In India

- **Disability.** The Rights of Persons with Disabilities (RPwD) Act 2016 recognises 21 disabilities, including mental illness. Every establishment, private ones included, must notify an equal opportunity policy (section 21). The 4% reservation applies to government establishments; private employers whose workforce is at least 5% persons with benchmark disabilities can get incentives (section 35). The Supreme Court has treated reasonable accommodation as part of the right to equality (*Vikash Kumar*, 2021). The ESI wage limit for employees with a disability is <Fact id="esi.wageCeilingPwd" />.
- **Transgender persons.** The Transgender Persons (Protection of Rights) Act 2019 bars discrimination in recruitment, promotion and other employment matters. Every establishment must designate a complaint officer, and the 2020 Rules require an equal opportunity policy, facilities such as unisex toilets, and confidentiality of gender identity.
- **POSH.** Every workplace with 10 or more employees needs an Internal Committee. The [POSH compliance chapter](/handbooks/labour-law/posh-compliance/) covers the committee, deadlines and reports; this module doesn't repeat them.
- **Women at work.** Maternity benefit is <Fact id="maternity.weeks" /> for the first two children. Night shifts for women need their written consent and the safeguards the rules require, and some state permissions add conditions such as transport and a working Internal Committee.
- **Reporting.** For the largest listed companies, BRSR Core people metrics need assurance or assessment. They include wellbeing spending as a share of revenue, wages paid to women as a share of total wages, and POSH complaints. [Module 18](/learn/global-hr-and-esg/) covers this in detail.
- **What goes unmeasured.** Few private firms measure caste or religion, yet a correspondence study found that CVs with Dalit and Muslim names received fewer interview calls than identical CVs with upper-caste Hindu names (Thorat and Attewell, 2007).
