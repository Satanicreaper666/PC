models:
  - id: management-by-objectives
    name: "Management by objectives (MBO)"
    by: "Peter Drucker; developed by George Odiorne"
    year: "1954; 1965"
    idea: "Managers and staff agree objectives together, and performance is judged by results against them."
    use: "Setting KRAs and annual objectives that tie each person's work to business goals."
    watch: "Can shrink into a yearly form-filling ritual, and it says little about how results were achieved."
    cite: "Drucker, P. F. (1954) The Practice of Management. Harper & Row."
  - id: objectives-and-key-results
    name: "Objectives and key results (OKRs)"
    by: "Andy Grove at Intel; popularised by John Doerr"
    year: "1970s; 2018"
    idea: "A few ambitious objectives, each with two to five measurable key results, set and reviewed every quarter and shared openly."
    use: "Aligning teams on a few priorities when work changes fast, as in tech firms and GCCs."
    watch: "Often becomes a renamed KPI list or a pay formula, which kills both the stretch and the honesty."
    cite: "Grove, A. S. (1983) High Output Management. Random House; Doerr, J. (2018) Measure What Matters. Portfolio."
  - id: bars-rating-scales
    name: "Behaviourally anchored rating scales (BARS)"
    by: "Patricia Cain Smith and Lorne Kendall"
    year: "1963"
    idea: "Each point on a rating scale is described by a concrete example of behaviour, so every rater judges against the same picture."
    use: "Rating behaviours or competencies where 'good' means different things to different managers."
    watch: "Slow and costly to build and keep current; anchors can miss what good looks like in new roles."
    cite: "Smith, P. C. and Kendall, L. M. (1963) Retranslation of expectations. Journal of Applied Psychology, 47(2)."
  - id: idiosyncratic-rater-effect
    name: "Idiosyncratic rater effect"
    by: "Steven Scullen, Michael Mount and Maynard Goff"
    year: "2000"
    idea: "More of the variation in performance ratings reflects the rater's own tendencies than the performance of the person being rated."
    use: "Making the case for calibration, several raters and evidence-based reviews."
    watch: "Measured on multisource ratings of managers; how large the effect is varies by setting."
    cite: "Scullen, S. E., Mount, M. K. and Goff, M. (2000) Understanding the latent structure of job performance ratings. Journal of Applied Psychology, 85(6)."
  - id: feedback-intervention-theory
    name: "Feedback intervention theory"
    by: "Avraham Kluger and Angelo DeNisi"
    year: "1996"
    idea: "Feedback improves performance on average, but often backfires when it draws attention to the person's self-image rather than the task."
    use: "Training managers to give feedback about the work and the next step, not about the person."
    watch: "Explains when feedback backfires better than it predicts how any single conversation will go."
    cite: "Kluger, A. N. and DeNisi, A. (1996) The effects of feedback interventions on performance. Psychological Bulletin, 119(2)."
  - id: split-roles-appraisal
    name: "Split roles in appraisal"
    by: "Herbert Meyer, Emanuel Kay and John French (at General Electric)"
    year: "1965"
    idea: "When one meeting both decides pay and coaches development, criticism makes people defensive and little improves."
    use: "Separating the development conversation from the increment decision."
    watch: "The two can't be fully separated: people know their rating feeds their pay."
    cite: "Meyer, H. H., Kay, E. and French, J. R. P. (1965) Split roles in performance appraisal. Harvard Business Review, 43(1)."
  - id: continuous-performance-management
    name: "Continuous performance management"
    by: "Marcus Buckingham and Ashley Goodall (Deloitte); Peter Cappelli and Anna Tavis"
    year: "2015; 2016"
    idea: "Replace the annual review with frequent check-ins, forward-looking coaching and fewer or simpler ratings."
    use: "Redesigning a cycle in which annual reviews come too late to change anything."
    watch: "Pay and promotion still need differentiation, so dropped ratings can return as hidden 'shadow ratings'."
    cite: "Buckingham, M. and Goodall, A. (2015) Reinventing performance management. Harvard Business Review, 93(4)."
  - id: grow-coaching-model
    name: "GROW coaching model"
    by: "Graham Alexander, Alan Fine and John Whitmore"
    year: "1980s; 1992"
    idea: "A coaching conversation moves through Goal, Reality, Options and Will (the way forward), with the coachee doing most of the thinking."
    use: "Structuring check-ins and coaching conversations so they end in a clear commitment."
    watch: "A process, not a theory. It works only if the manager listens and asks rather than tells."
    cite: "Whitmore, J. (1992) Coaching for Performance. Nicholas Brealey."

    options:
      - "Objective: be excellent at payroll. Key results: do our best every month and keep everyone happy."
      - "Objective: make pay day trouble-free. Key results: errors on under 0.5% of payslips; queries closed in 2 days; joiners paid in their first cycle."
      - "Objective: process payroll. Key results: run payroll on the 30th; file returns; answer emails."
      - "Objective: cut payroll errors this quarter. Key result: zero errors, with each team member's increment tied to it."
    answer: 1
    feedback:
      - "Neither part is measurable, so no one can tell at quarter-end whether it was met."
      - "Correct. One clear objective with a few measurable, stretching results that would show it was achieved."
      - "These are routine activities, not results. OKRs describe outcomes that need to change."
      - "Tying a key result mechanically to pay invites sandbagging: people set goals they know they can hit."
    why: "An OKR pairs a short, motivating objective with two to five measurable key results. Keep it apart from routine tasks and from any pay formula."
  - id: pm-halo
    type: knowledge
    q: "A manager rates an analyst 'outstanding' on accuracy, teamwork and punctuality, mainly because she gave an excellent presentation to the client. Which rating error is this?"
    options:
      - "Halo effect"
      - "Recency effect"
      - "Central tendency"
      - "Contrast effect"
    answer: 0
    feedback:
      - "Correct. One strong impression spills over into every dimension being rated."
      - "Recency is over-weighting the latest events. Here one trait is colouring the others."
      - "Central tendency is rating almost everyone in the middle of the scale."
      - "Contrast is rating someone by comparison with the person rated just before them."
    why: "Halo, and its negative twin the horns effect, shrink when each dimension is rated separately against behavioural anchors and evidence."
  - id: pm-calibration-purpose
    type: knowledge
    q: "What is the main purpose of a calibration meeting?"
    options:
      - "To force each team's ratings into the company's bell curve"
      - "To make ratings comparable across managers by testing them against evidence"
      - "To let senior leaders change ratings without asking the manager"
      - "To decide each person's increment percentage"
    answer: 1
    feedback:
      - "Forcing a curve is a quota, not calibration. It can push fair ratings down in strong teams."
      - "Correct. Calibration checks that 'exceeds' means the same thing in every team, using evidence."
      - "Changes made without the manager's evidence and reasons undermine trust and fairness."
      - "Pay is a later step that uses calibrated ratings as one input, alongside budget and position in the pay range."
    why: "Calibration exists because ratings vary with the rater. Its output is consistent, documented ratings, not a fixed distribution."
  - id: pm-feedback-backfire
    type: knowledge
    q: "What did Kluger and DeNisi's 1996 review of feedback research find?"
    options:
      - "Feedback improves performance in almost every case, if given often"
      - "Feedback helps on average, but lowered performance in over a third of cases"
      - "Feedback has no measurable average effect on performance at work"
      - "Feedback improves performance only when it is linked to pay"
    answer: 1
    feedback:
      - "That is the common assumption the review challenged."
      - "Correct. It backfired most when it turned attention to the person's self-image rather than the task and the next step."
      - "The average effect was positive, just very variable."
      - "The link to pay wasn't the deciding factor; where the feedback directed attention was."
    why: "Keep feedback about the work and what to do next, not about the person's worth."

    type: judgement
    q: "You are the HR business partner at a 300-person fintech in Pune. A team lead asks you to put an analyst on a 30-day improvement plan 'so we can exit her by March'. She has had no written feedback this year. What do you do?"
    options:
      - "Approve the plan, since it creates the paper trail the company needs"
      - "Ask the team lead to give clear written feedback and coaching first, find out what is behind the gap, use a genuine plan only if the gap persists, and check the legal route before any separation"
      - "Refuse, because improvement plans are always unfair"
      - "Offer her a separation package straight away to avoid a long process"
    answer: 1
    feedback:
      - "A plan used to paper an exit already decided is unfair, and weak evidence if the exit is challenged."
      - "Best. It gives her a real chance, finds the cause, keeps a fair record and protects the company legally."
      - "A fair plan with real support is a legitimate tool. The problem here is the missing feedback and the intent."
      - "Jumping to separation skips the feedback she never had, and can still be challenged."

  - id: pm-lenient-manager
    type: judgement
    q: "In a calibration meeting, one manager has rated all nine people in his team 'exceeds expectations'. Other managers are annoyed. As the HR business partner chairing the meeting, what is the best response?"
    options:
      - "Move five of his ratings down to fit the guidance range"
      - "Ask him to show the evidence for each rating against goals, compare with similar roles in other teams, and change only the ratings the evidence doesn't support, recording reasons"
      - "Accept his ratings, since he knows his team best"
      - "Ask the business head to decide"
    answer: 1
    feedback:
      - "A cut made to fit a range is arbitrary and unfair to anyone who really did exceed."

    type: written
    level: advanced
    q: "Critically evaluate whether an Indian IT services company should abandon forced-distribution ('bell curve') ratings. (About 200 words.)"
    why: "Model answer: Forced distribution was meant to stop rating inflation and make pay and exit decisions defensible. It does fix the share of top ratings, which helps a tight increment budget, and Indian IT firms adopted it widely. The evidence for it is weak, though. Simulations suggest the gains in workforce quality shrink after the first years while rivalry and gaming grow (Scullen, Bergey and Aiman-Smith, 2005). ## Look at the system before the form

The Hyderabad CEO's question sounds like a choice about a rating scale. It isn't. A rating is the last step of a year-long system, and most of what goes wrong with ratings starts earlier: vague goals, rare feedback, managers who rate differently, and one meeting that tries to coach someone and decide their pay at the same time.

Researchers separate *performance appraisal*, a once-a-year judgement, from *performance management*, a continuous cycle of planning, coaching, reviewing, rewarding and developing. Decades of work on better rating forms improved little; what changes performance is what managers do between the forms (Pulakos and O'Leary, 2011; DeNisi and Murphy, 2017).

For HR, this changes the question. Don't ask "which scale?" first. Ask what the system is mainly for: developing people, allocating pay and promotion, or documenting decisions. Then make each step serve that purpose. If the Hyderabad company keeps its curve but fixes nothing else, the same arguments will return next March.

## Goals that direct effort: KRAs, MBO and OKRs

Most Indian firms write goals as key result areas (KRAs) with key performance indicators (KPIs). The habit goes back to Peter Drucker's **management by objectives** (1954): agree objectives, then judge people by results. Its engine is **goal-setting theory**, which you met in [module 2](/learn/people-at-work/): specific, hard goals with feedback beat "do your best".

**OKRs** (objectives and key results) came from Andy Grove at Intel and spread through John Doerr to Google and much of the tech industry. They set a few priorities each quarter, each with two to five measurable key results, and keep them visible to everyone. They are meant to stretch, so missing some is normal.

Goals can also do harm. Narrow targets invite gaming and shortcuts, a risk Steven Kerr named in 1975 as rewarding one thing while hoping for another. Sales targets and call-handling times are the classic cases.

What this means for HR:

- Use KRAs for the stable core of a role and OKRs for the few things that must change this quarter.
- Write every goal so that a neutral person could tell at year-end whether it was met.
- Add a quality or behaviour measure to any target that could be gamed.
- Reset goals when priorities change. A goal set in April and never revisited is a common reason year-end ratings feel unfair.

## Rating performance, and the errors that creep in

Every rating method trades accuracy against cost and fairness.

| Method | How it works | Main risk |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Graphic rating scale | Rate each goal or competency from 1 to 5 | Words like "good" mean different things to different raters |
| Behaviourally anchored scale (BARS) | Each point is described by a real example of behaviour | Slow to build; needs updating as roles change |
| Ranking and forced distribution | Compare people with each other, or fill fixed shares per band | Pits colleagues against each other; unfair in small or strong teams |
| Results against goals (MBO, OKRs) | Judge what was delivered against what was agreed | Ignores how results were achieved; invites easy goals |
| 360-degree feedback | Views from manager, peers, reports and customers | Small average gains; can become a popularity vote if tied to pay |

Whatever the method, the rater gets in the way:

- **Halo:** one strong impression colours every dimension (Thorndike, 1920). Its opposite is the horns effect.
- **Leniency and severity:** a manager's whole team is pushed up or down.
- **Central tendency:** almost everyone lands in the middle.
- **Recency:** the last two months outweigh the other ten.
- **Similar-to-me:** people who think, speak or socialise like the rater do better.
- **Proximity:** in hybrid teams, people seen more often in the office can be rated higher for being visible.

Together these effects are large. In a big study of multisource ratings, more of the variation in ratings reflected the rater than the person being rated (Scullen, Mount and Goff, 2000). Raters also have goals of their own, such as keeping the team motivated or avoiding a hard conversation, so accuracy is rarely their only aim (Murphy and Cleveland, 1995).

What this means for HR:

- Rate each goal or competency separately, against anchors, not as one overall impression.
- Ask for evidence: outputs, examples, data, and views from more than one person.
- Train managers in the common errors, then check their rating patterns rather than trusting the training.

## Calibration: making ratings comparable

In a calibration meeting, managers who rate similar roles present their proposed ratings with evidence and agree what each rating level means in practice. HR chairs it, keeps the discussion on evidence, and watches for patterns. Guidance ranges, for example 15–25% "exceeds", tell the room where to look harder. They are not quotas: if the evidence shows a unit really did outperform, the record says so.

<Worked title="Worked example: calibrating a delivery unit's ratings">

A delivery unit has 30 engineers under three managers, and 12 of the 30 are women. The company's guidance ranges are 15–25% "exceeds", 60–75% "meets" and 5–15% "below". All numbers are an example.

**Step 1: compare each manager's proposed ratings with the guidance.**

| Team of 10 | Exceeds | Meets | Below |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A | 8 | 2 | 0 |
| B | 0 | 7 | 3 |
| C | 2 | 7 | 1 |
| **Unit** | **10** | **16** | **4** |

Across the unit of 30, that is 33% "exceeds", 53% "meets" and 13% "below". A looks lenient and B looks harsh. That tells the panel where to look, not what to decide.

**Step 2: test the outliers against evidence.**

- Four of A's eight "exceeds" met their key results only in part, and no one but A could point to anything more. They move to "meets". The other four have clear evidence and stay.
- Two of B's "meets" led a release delivered a quarter early, with written praise from the client. They move to "exceeds".
- One of B's "below" was moved across three projects and her goals were never reset. Another was on maternity leave for five months and was rated against full-year goals. Both are re-rated against goals pro-rated for the time worked, and both move to "meets". The third "below" has documented, repeated misses after feedback, and stays.
- C's ratings match the evidence and don't change.

**Step 3: check the result and look for patterns.**

| Rating | Before | After |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Exceeds | 10 (33%) | 8 (27%) |
| Meets | 16 (53%) | 20 (67%) |
| Below | 4 (13%) | 2 (7%) |

"Exceeds" is still slightly above the 25% guide. The panel records why (the early release) instead of pushing someone down to fit. Before calibration, women were 40% of the unit but held 2 of the 10 "exceeds" ratings (20%). After the evidence review they hold 3 of 8 (38%). Two of the four original "below" ratings had nothing to do with performance: one came from goals that were never reset, the other from leave.

**The lesson:** the guidance ranges found the problems, but the evidence fixed them. A forced curve would have cut A's ratings at random and left both unfair "below" ratings in B's team.

</Worked>

<Callout type="note" title="At strategic level (SHRM-SCP, CIPD Level 7)">
Senior HR owns the rules of calibration, not just the meeting. Decide who sits on the panel, what counts as evidence, how far guidance ranges bind, and what happens when a pattern by gender, location or leave appears. Report those patterns to leadership every cycle, as you would any other business risk.
</Callout>

## Feedback, check-ins and coaching

Most managers assume feedback always helps. The evidence is more interesting. Kluger and DeNisi (1996) reviewed decades of studies and found that feedback improved performance on average, but in over a third of cases it made performance worse. It backfired most when it turned attention to the person ("you're careless") rather than the task ("the reconciliation missed two accounts; here is a checklist"). Thirty years earlier, a study at General Electric had found that criticism given in the same meeting as the pay decision made people defensive (Meyer, Kay and French, 1965).

Both findings point the same way: talk often, about the work and the next step, and keep the pay conversation separate. In the 2010s several large firms redesigned around this idea:

- **Adobe** (2012) replaced its annual review and ranking with regular "check-ins" between managers and their teams.
- **Deloitte** (2015) moved to frequent check-ins plus a short quarterly "snapshot". Team leaders say what they would do about each person, such as whether they would give them the highest increase if it were their own money, or promote them today, instead of rating their skills. This was a direct answer to the rater effects above.
- **GE**, whose "vitality curve" made forced ranking famous and which had dropped it in the mid-2000s, began in 2015 to move staff from annual reviews to an app for frequent "touchpoints" on short-term priorities.

Cappelli and Tavis (2016) called this the performance management revolution. Whether it works is the debate below.

Check-ins help only if managers can coach. The **GROW** model, developed by Graham Alexander, Alan Fine and John Whitmore in the 1980s and popularised in Whitmore's *Coaching for Performance* (1992), keeps the employee doing most of the thinking.

What this means for HR:

- Make check-ins short and frequent, fortnightly or monthly, with a few notes kept by both sides.
- Train managers to describe the work, not the person, and to ask before they advise.
- Hold the development talk a few weeks away from the increment decision.

## Under-performance or misconduct: choosing the lawful route

A branch sales executive in Kochi misses his target three quarters running. Separately, an audit finds he filed false client-visit reports. These are two different problems, and each has its own route.

- **Under-performance** is about capability: the person can't yet do the job to the standard. The response is clear feedback, coaching and support, then a fair improvement plan with measurable goals and a review date.
- **Misconduct** is about conduct: the person broke a rule or a duty. The response is fact-finding, a show-cause notice or charge-sheet, an impartial enquiry and a proportionate penalty.

CIPD and UK law draw the same line, between capability and conduct.

In India the route also decides which law applies. The Industrial Relations Code's "worker" covers most non-managerial staff, and supervisors earning up to ₹18,000 a month. Ending a worker's employment for any reason other than disciplinary punishment is retrenchment, unless an exclusion applies. With a year's continuous service, that means written notice of <Fact id="notice.retrenchmentNoticeMonths" /> (or pay in lieu), compensation of <Fact id="notice.retrenchmentCompensationDays" /> for each completed year, notice to the government, and a payment of <Fact id="notice.reskillingFundDays" /> into the re-skilling fund. "Improvement plan, then exit" for a worker is a retrenchment, not a dismissal. Persistent poor performance can be treated as misconduct only where the standing orders or service rules define it that way and a fair enquiry proves it.

For managers and others outside the "worker" definition, the contract and the state Shops and Establishments Act decide notice, and some state Acts require notice or proof of misconduct before dismissal. The [termination chapter](/handbooks/labour-law/termination-grievance-and-disputes/) sets out each route, and the [performance improvement plan](/toolkit/performance-improvement-plan/) template gives you a fair structure.

<Callout type="warning" title="Don't use an improvement plan to paper an exit">
A plan written to fail is unfair, rarely survives a challenge, and for a worker it doesn't avoid the retrenchment rules. Use one only when the person has had clear feedback and could realistically succeed with support.
</Callout>

## Where experts disagree

Expect these in an XLRI case discussion, a CIPD Level 7 assignment or a SHRM-SCP judgement question.

- **Ratings: keep or kill?** In a 2016 debate in *Industrial and Organizational Psychology*, one side argued that ratings are so distorted they do more harm than good. The other replied that pay and promotion still need differentiation, so dropping ratings only hides the judgement in "shadow ratings" that people can't see or challenge (Adler et al., 2016). GE's early pilots reported that managers differentiated pay just as much without ratings, which suggests the conversation matters more than the number.
- **The bell curve.** Forced distribution stops everyone being rated above average. But simulations suggest its gains in workforce quality shrink quickly while rivalry and gaming grow (Scullen, Bergey and Aiman-Smith, 2005). Individual performance also tends to be heavy-tailed, with a few stars, rather than bell-shaped (O'Boyle and Aguinis, 2012). Defenders reply that without a curve, managers rate almost everyone highly.
- **The systems view.** W. Edwards Deming argued that most variation in performance comes from the system (processes, tools, management), so rating individuals blames people for what the system does. Most performance research still assumes individual differences matter a great deal.
- **360-degree feedback.** It adds viewpoints, but the average improvement after it is small (Smither, London and Reilly, 2005), and tying it to pay invites people to trade favourable reviews.

A good answer names the main purpose of the system (development, reward or documentation), weighs the evidence on both sides, and recommends a design for a stated context. "Guidance ranges plus calibration" is usually easier to defend than either a forced curve or no ratings at all.

## In India

- **Normalisation is common.** Rating curves are widespread in Indian IT and services. Deloitte India's 2026 Talent Outlook reported the share of top ratings falling from 10% to 7% while promotions rose from 12% to 14%: tighter ratings, with a risk of title inflation.
- **Worker or not, and the state layer.** Decide early whether the person is a "worker" under the Industrial Relations Code, because that decides whether a non-disciplinary exit is retrenchment. Where a state Shops and Establishments Act still applies, check its notice and dismissal rules. Karnataka, Goa and Delhi rules under the Codes were still in draft in early November 2026. See [termination, grievance and disputes](/handbooks/labour-law/termination-grievance-and-disputes/).
- **Standing orders and enquiries.** Standing orders are compulsory for establishments with 300 or more workers. Below that, many employers adopt service rules so that misconduct has a defined meaning. A domestic enquiry must follow natural justice: specific charges, an impartial enquiry officer, a chance to see the evidence and be heard, and a reasoned finding.
- **Leave and equal pay.** Rate people who took long leave against pro-rated goals. It is unlawful to dismiss a woman during or because of her maternity leave, and the Code on Wages bars gender discrimination in pay, so biased ratings that feed pay carry legal as well as fairness risk.
- **Monitoring data.** Productivity-tracking tools and algorithmic scores process personal data. The DPDP Act's core duties for employers start on 13 May 2027, so plan notices, purposes and limits now.
