## Ask what your old process predicted

Every hiring method is a bet that something you can see before the job predicts what you'll see in it. The Hyderabad company bet on an aptitude test and a 20-minute conversation. Three in ten of last year's batch had left or been let go within a year, so the bet didn't pay.

Before buying a faster tool, ask what the current process measures. An aptitude test measures general reasoning. A short unstructured interview mostly measures whether the interviewer liked the candidate. Neither looks at what a new engineer actually does in the first year: write and debug code, learn a client's systems, and work in a team to deadlines. A faster version of the same process would make the same mistakes faster.

For HR, the discipline is to define success first, measure it after a year, and trace failures back to the stage that caused them.

## Define the job before you search

**Job analysis** is the foundation of everything that follows, and it is what makes a selection decision defensible. Common methods:

- **Interviews and observation** with job holders and their managers.
- **Critical incidents:** managers describe real examples of very good and very poor performance, which become interview questions and rating anchors.
- **Task inventories and questionnaires,** such as the Position Analysis Questionnaire, with O*NET as a reference library of job descriptions.

The output is a short list of tasks and the competencies they need, split into knock-out criteria and desirable ones. Lyle and Signe Spencer's competency iceberg (1993) is a useful picture: some competencies are easy to see and train, while others sit deeper and are worth selecting for.

Then decide what you offer. An **employee value proposition (EVP)** is the honest answer to "why work here?": the work, the growth, the pay and the people. Ambler and Barrow argued in 1996 that employment can be managed like a brand. Wanous's **realistic job preview** adds a warning: tell candidates the hard parts too, such as night shifts, client pressure or a bench period, because people who join with accurate expectations leave less.

Choose sourcing channels by role. Referrals are fast and referred hires often stay longer, but they recruit people like your current staff. Benjamin Schneider's attraction–selection–attrition idea explains why firms drift towards sameness. See [writing job descriptions](/handbooks/recruitment/writing-job-descriptions/), [sourcing](/handbooks/recruitment/sourcing/) and [employer branding on a small budget](/handbooks/recruitment/employer-branding-on-a-small-budget/).

## How well do selection methods predict performance?

**Validity** is the correlation between scores at hiring and performance later. Zero means the method predicts no better than chance; one would be perfect. In selection research, around .40 is strong and around .20 is weak. A validity of .40 doesn't mean 40% of decisions are right; it measures how closely scores and later performance move together.

In 1998, Frank Schmidt and John Hunter combined 85 years of studies into one table, and it shaped HR textbooks for two decades. In 2022, Paul Sackett and colleagues showed that earlier reviews had over-corrected for **range restriction**, the fact that studies only see the performance of people who were hired. Correcting less aggressively, most estimates fell, and the ranking changed.

  />
</Figure>

What this means for HR:

- **Structured, job-specific methods lead.** Structured interviews and job-knowledge tests sit at the top, with scored biodata and work samples close behind.
- **Cognitive tests still help, but less than was claimed.** Because they can show larger gaps between groups, use them as one input, not as the only filter.
- **Unstructured interviews and years of experience predict poorly.** Neither should decide a hire.
- **Combine methods,** and check them against your own results, because validity varies widely from study to study.

<Callout type="note" title="At strategic level (SHRM-SCP, CIPD Level 7)">
Utility analysis turns validity into money: a more valid method, used on many hires in a role where performance varies a lot, can be worth far more than it costs. Weigh that against the diversity–validity dilemma, in which some valid methods widen group gaps. A strategic answer designs the whole system: which methods, in what order, with what checks on fairness.
</Callout>

## Measure the funnel: yield ratios and cost per hire

A hiring funnel narrows at every stage. The **yield ratio** of a stage is the share of people who move on to the next one. Yield ratios do two jobs. Worked backwards from the hires you need, they tell you how many applicants to attract. Compared over time and between groups, they show where the process leaks or where it may be unfair.

<Figure title="Example: the funnel for 60 experienced engineers" caption="Read from the widest stage down: each figure is the number who reached that stage, and its percentage of the stage above is that stage's yield ratio. Example numbers only." wide>

    stages={[

      { label: "Shortlisted after screening", value: 500, display: "500", note: "Knock-out criteria applied the same way" },
      { label: "Passed the work sample", value: 200, display: "200", note: "A 90-minute coding task" },
      { label: "Offered after the panel", value: 80, display: "80", note: "Structured interview, scored independently" },
      { label: "Joined", value: 60, display: "60", note: "After notice periods of up to 90 days" }
    ]}
  />
</Figure>

**Cost per hire** is total hiring costs for a period, external and internal, divided by the people who joined in that period. Counting your own team's time matters: leave it out and in-house hiring looks free.

<Worked title="Worked example: yield ratios and cost per hire for the Hyderabad engineers">

All figures are an illustrative example for the six months in which the company hired 60 experienced engineers.

**Step 1: yield ratios.** From the funnel: shortlist 500 ÷ 2,000 = 25%; work sample 200 ÷ 500 = 40%; offer 80 ÷ 200 = 40%; joining 60 ÷ 80 = 75%. Overall, 60 ÷ 2,000 = 3% of applicants joined.

**Step 2: plan the next round.** Next half-year needs 60 joiners again. Suppose faster offers and better contact during notice periods lift joining from 75% to 85%. Working backwards:

| Stage | People needed | Calculation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Offers | 71 | 60 ÷ 0.85 |
| Pass the work sample | 178 | 71 ÷ 0.40 |
| Shortlisted | 445 | 178 ÷ 0.40 |
| Applicants | 1,780 | 445 ÷ 0.25 |

Fixing a late stage saves effort at every stage above it: about 220 applications, 55 work samples and 22 panel interviews.

**Step 3: add up the costs.**

External costs, **₹38,80,000**:

- Agency fees: 12 hires × ₹1,50,000 = ₹18,00,000
- Referral bonuses: 20 hires × ₹50,000 = ₹10,00,000
- Job portals and advertising: ₹6,00,000
- Online assessment platform: ₹3,00,000
- Background checks: 60 × ₹3,000 = ₹1,80,000

Internal costs, **₹25,05,000**:

- Recruiters: 3 × ₹9 lakh CTC × 6 of 12 months × 80% of their time = ₹10,80,000
- Interviewers: 850 hours × ₹1,500 an hour = ₹12,75,000
- Applicant tracking system, share for the period: ₹1,50,000

Total hiring costs: **₹63,85,000**.

Interviewer hours: 500 work samples reviewed at 30 minutes each (250 hours), plus 200 panel interviews with two interviewers for 90 minutes (600 hours). The hourly cost is an annual CTC of ₹30 lakh ÷ about 2,000 working hours.

**Step 4: the result.** Cost per hire = ₹63,85,000 ÷ 60 = **₹1,06,417**. The 60 hires have an average CTC of ₹18 lakh, ₹10.8 crore in all, so the recruiting cost ratio is ₹63.85 lakh ÷ ₹10.8 crore, about **5.9%**.

**What to do with it.** Interviewer time is the largest hidden cost, so a sharper screen that sends fewer people to the panel saves real money. Before cutting agency or referral spend, compare each channel's one-year retention and performance, not just its cost. The [cost per hire calculator](/toolkit/cost-per-hire-calculator/) does this arithmetic for you.

</Worked>

## Structure the interview, and treat candidates fairly

Michael Campion and colleagues reviewed the research on interview structure in 1997. Structure has two halves: **what you ask** (job-based questions, the same for everyone) and **how you score** (anchored scales, independent ratings, trained interviewers). Most of the gain in validity comes from doing both.

<Figure title="Building a structured interview" caption="Work through the steps in order; skipping the anchors or scoring after discussion undoes most of the benefit.">
  <Steps
    items={[
      { title: "Start from the job analysis", body: "Pick the four to six competencies that matter most in the first year." },
      { title: "Write the questions", body: "Behavioural ('Tell me about a time you…') and situational ('What would you do if…'), the same for every candidate." },
      { title: "Anchor the scores", body: "Describe what a weak, adequate and strong answer sounds like for each question." },
      { title: "Brief the panel", body: "Agree who asks what, how to probe without leading, and which questions are never asked." },
      { title: "Score before you talk", body: "Each interviewer scores alone; then the panel discusses only the differences." },
      { title: "Check the results", body: "Compare scores with performance at 6 and 12 months, and pass rates across groups." }
    ]}
  />
</Figure>

**Assessment centres** go further. Trained assessors watch candidates across several exercises, such as an in-tray task, a group discussion, a role-play and a presentation, and rate each competency more than once before pooling their judgements. Its business use grew out of AT&T's management studies from 1956. It costs more than an interview, so keep it for managerial roles where several competencies matter. Many Indian employers run the same design as a "development centre" to guide promotions and development plans.

**Candidate experience** is part of selection, not an extra. Stephen Gilliland showed that candidates judge a process by rules of fairness: is it related to the job, do I get a chance to show what I can do, is everyone treated the same, am I told the outcome honestly and respectfully? In India, where 60- to 90-day notice periods and counter-offers are common, a process that feels slow or disrespectful also loses people between offer and joining. See [structured interviews](/handbooks/recruitment/structured-interviews/), [screening and shortlisting](/handbooks/recruitment/screening-and-shortlisting/) and the [interview evaluation form](/toolkit/interview-evaluation-form/).

## AI screening: test it before you trust it

AI now drafts job ads, ranks CVs, schedules interviews and scores video or voice interviews. Recruiting is the most common use of AI in HR in SHRM's 2026 survey. The promise is speed and consistency. The risk is that a tool learns the patterns in past decisions, including their biases, and repeats them at scale.

The warning signs are well documented:

- In a 2024 University of Washington study of more than three million comparisons, three large language models ranking résumés preferred white-associated names 85% of the time and female-associated names only 11% of the time.
- Amazon dropped an experimental CV-ranking tool after it learned to mark down CVs that mentioned women's activities (reported in 2018).
- In May 2025, a US court let an age-discrimination claim against an HR-technology vendor, *Mobley v. Workday*, proceed as a nationwide collective action. Vendors as well as employers can now be in the frame.

Regulation is catching up. The EU AI Act treats AI used to recruit or select people as **high-risk**; after amendments in 2026, the duties on employers using such systems apply from 2 December 2027. Indian capability centres whose tools affect people in the EU may be caught. India has no AI-specific law, but the DPDP Act and anti-discrimination laws apply to what a tool does.

Write the controls into the vendor contract: audit rights, limits on how candidate data is used, and a duty to share bias-test results.

## Where experts disagree

- **The numbers themselves.** Oh, Le and Roth (2023) argue that Sackett's corrections are too cautious and understate validity; Sackett and colleagues replied the same year. Treat it as a live debate about method. The practical message survives either way: structure everything, combine job-related methods, and never let an unstructured interview decide.
- **Validity against diversity.** Some of the more valid tools, cognitive tests in particular, can produce larger gaps between groups. Researchers call this the diversity–validity dilemma. Combining methods and weighting them with care can narrow the gap, usually at some cost in accuracy; there is no free answer.
- **Fit or sameness?** Hiring for "culture fit" can mean shared values, or it can mean people like us. Schneider's attraction–selection–attrition idea predicts the second. Many employers now ask whether a candidate adds something the team lacks.
- **Gut feel against structure.** Managers trust their own judgement of people and resist structured tools, even though the evidence favours structure. AI adds a new version of the argument: early field studies of AI-led interviews report better results, but most are working papers or vendor-funded, and bias and explainability remain open questions.

A good answer cites the evidence, shows how it applies to the role in the case, names the fairness trade-off, and proposes a design that balances them.

## In India

- **Appointment letters.** The OSH Code requires every employer to give every employee an appointment letter, in the format the rules prescribe. Staff already employed when the Code started on 21 November 2025 were to get one by about 20 February 2026. An offer letter doesn't replace it. Cover the designation, nature of employment (permanent or fixed-term), date of joining, wages and their components, and the social security that applies. State formats may differ. See [offers and negotiation](/handbooks/recruitment/offers-and-negotiation/), the [offer letter](/toolkit/offer-letter/) and the [appointment letter](/toolkit/appointment-letter/).
- **Candidate data under the DPDP Act.** Most of the Act's duties start in about May 2027. Until then, the IT Act's rules require written consent before collecting sensitive personal data such as health, biometric or financial information. It isn't settled that the Act's "employment" ground covers people who aren't yet employees, so give candidates a clear notice and take consent for the application, any talent pool and background checks. Collect only what you need, protect it, and delete it when the vacancy closes. Failing to protect personal data can attract a penalty of up to ₹250 crore. See [handling candidate data with care](/handbooks/recruitment/screening-and-shortlisting/).
- **Equal treatment.** The Code on Wages bars discrimination on the ground of sex when recruiting for the same or similar work. The Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act 2016 requires every establishment to have an equal opportunity policy; offer reasonable adjustments to tests and interviews as a matter of course. Never ask about caste, religion, marriage plans or pregnancy.
- **Offer to joining.** Long notice periods, counter-offers and notice buy-outs make the gap between offer and day one the riskiest stage in Indian hiring. Keep in touch during the notice period and track your joining rate by source. See [onboarding from offer to day 90](/handbooks/recruitment/onboarding-day-1-to-90/).
