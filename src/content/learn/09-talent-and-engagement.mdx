## Look beneath the attrition number

The Bengaluru GCC lost 22% of its engineers last year, about 396 of 1,800 people. That headline hides several different problems. Before anyone designs a fix, split the number four ways:

- **Voluntary or not:** did people choose to leave?
- **Regrettable or not:** were they people the organisation wanted to keep?
- **Avoidable or not:** could the employer have changed the outcome, or was it a spouse's transfer, higher studies or a move abroad?
- **Where:** which units, managers, roles and lengths of service?

Losing people the organisation can spare is *functional* turnover, and some of it is healthy. Losing people it wanted to keep is *dysfunctional*, and that is where retention work belongs.

The problem is now focused: 60 exits in three units, not 396 across the site. Exit interviews in India often say "better pay", partly because it is the easiest reason to give a former employer. Pay does matter, especially in a hot market. But when exits cluster in a few units, the manager and the work inside those units are the first suspects.

For HR, this means reporting attrition every quarter as regrettable and avoidable exits, by unit and manager, and treating a cluster as a diagnosis problem before it becomes a budget problem.

## Who counts as talent, and the 9-box

Leadership's proposal assumes that "talent" means the people in the top boxes. That is one answer to a question every talent strategy has to settle: who is talent management for?

- **Exclusive:** a small group of high performers and high potentials gets most of the investment. McKinsey's "war for talent" research (Chambers and colleagues, 1998; Michaels, Handfield-Jones and Axelrod, 2001) made this the default in many large firms.
- **Pivotal positions:** Huselid, Beatty and Becker (2005) argued for differentiating roles as well as people. Invest most where differences in performance move the strategy most, and make sure top performers sit there.
- **Inclusive:** everyone has strengths worth developing, and the job is to find and use them.

These approaches differ both in whom they cover and in whether "talent" means the people or their qualities (Gallardo-Gallardo, Dries and González-Cruz, 2013). Critics of the exclusive model, notably Jeffrey Pfeffer (2001), argue that star cultures neglect the systems that make most people effective.

The grid has well-known weaknesses:

- **Potential is a forecast,** and raters disagree about it. It has several parts, commonly described as aspiration, ability and engagement (Corporate Leadership Council, 2005), but managers often judge it as one impression borrowed from current performance (Silzer and Church, 2009).
- **Labels stick and can carry bias.** A working paper on a large retailer found women received lower potential ratings than men despite higher performance ratings, which explained much of the gap in promotions (Benson, Li and Shue, 2022).
- **High performers aren't always high potentials,** and a strong year in one role says little about readiness for a different one.

So calibrate potential the way you calibrate performance (see [module 7](/learn/performance-management/)). Ask for evidence of aspiration, ability and engagement, review the grid every year, and check who lands in each box by gender, location and working pattern. The [9-box talent mapper](/toolkit/nine-box-talent-mapper/) helps you run the review.

## Succession, careers and internal mobility

If the GCC's two strongest architects left tomorrow, who would step in? Succession planning answers that question before it is urgent.

- **Replacement planning** names an heir for each senior job. It is quick, but the charts go stale and the named heir is often not ready.
- **Succession management** builds pools for levels or families of roles, rates readiness (ready now, ready in one to two years, longer), and develops the pool with stretch roles, rotations and mentoring. Byham, Smith and Paese (2002) called these acceleration pools. Charan, Drotter and Noel's *leadership pipeline* (2001) describes what each step up demands.
- **Emergency cover** names, for every critical role, someone who could hold it for a few months while a permanent choice is made.

Succession is the organisation's view; careers are the employee's. Edgar Schein's **career anchors** (1978) remind us that some people want to manage while others want technical mastery, autonomy or security. **Dual ladders** let strong engineers grow in pay and status without managing people. Douglas Hall's **protean career** (1976) is self-directed and values-led, so people move on when internal routes are blocked. Internal talent marketplaces match people to projects and roles inside the firm.

One rule matters most in practice: a manager shouldn't be able to block an internal move indefinitely. Blocked internal moves are a common route to external attrition.

What this means for HR:

- Name critical roles from the strategy, and keep two or three candidates at different stages of readiness for each.
- Publish internal openings, and set a fair rule on how long a manager can hold someone back.
- Review succession pools every year, including who is in them by gender and location.

## Engagement: what it is and what India's data says

"Engagement" is used loosely for satisfaction, commitment, happiness and survey scores. Three research traditions give it sharper meaning:

- **William Kahn (1990)** described personal engagement as bringing your whole self to a role. It depends on three conditions: the work feels *meaningful*, it is *safe* to be yourself and speak up, and you have the physical and emotional *availability* to give.
- **Wilmar Schaufeli and colleagues (2002)** defined work engagement as a positive state of *vigour*, *dedication* and *absorption*, roughly the opposite of burnout, measured with the Utrecht Work Engagement Scale. The job demands–resources model links it to what a job provides, such as support, autonomy and feedback.
- **Gallup** measures the everyday conditions for engagement with twelve questions, such as knowing what is expected and having the chance to do your best every day. Its meta-analyses link team scores to productivity, safety and turnover (Harter, Schmidt and Hayes, 2002).

## Why people stay or leave, and what leaving costs

Classic theory says people leave when leaving looks both desirable and easy (March and Simon, 1958). Later research added two ideas that change what HR should do:

- **Shocks.** Many quits follow a jolt, such as a missed promotion, a reorganisation, a new manager or an unsolicited offer, rather than slowly building dissatisfaction (Lee and Mitchell's unfolding model, 1994).
- **Embeddedness.** People stay because of their *links* to colleagues and community, their *fit* with the job and the place, and what they would *sacrifice* by leaving (Mitchell and colleagues, 2001).

In India's tight tech and GCC markets, the pay jump on switching jobs is a real pull, so check the pay position (compa-ratio) of the people you most want to keep. But pay rarely explains exits that cluster under particular managers.

**Stay interviews** ask current employees what keeps them and what might make them leave, before a shock does. Managers hold them, ideally once a year with each person and soon after any shock. Four questions do most of the work:

1. What do you look forward to at work?
2. What would make you think about leaving?
3. What would you change about your role if you could?
4. How can I support you better?

The interview helps only if the manager acts on one or two answers and follows up. The [stay interview guide](/toolkit/stay-interview-guide/) has a full script.

All figures are an example. The typical leaver in the three units is an engineer on ₹24,00,000 a year (₹2,00,000 a month), with about 250 working days a year, so a day's value is ₹9,600.

**Step 1: cost one leaver.**

| Part, and how it is worked out | Cost |
| --- | --- |
| **Empty seat:** ₹9,600 × 45 working days × 50% of work not covered | ₹2,16,000 |
| **Hiring:** 8.33% agency fee on ₹24,00,000, plus ₹20,000 for tests and checks | ₹2,19,920 |
| **Interview and handover time:** 40 hours × ₹1,500 an hour | ₹60,000 |
| **Onboarding:** induction, laptop set-up and training | ₹60,000 |
| **Ramp-up:** ₹2,00,000 × 4 months × 50% output lost | ₹4,00,000 |
| **Cost per leaver** | **₹9,55,920** |

That is about 40% of a year's CTC, and it leaves out lost clients, delayed projects and the extra load on colleagues, so it understates the true cost.

**Step 2: cost the problem.** 60 avoidable, regrettable exits × ₹9,55,920 = **₹5,73,55,200, about ₹5.7 crore a year.**

**Step 3: compare the options.**

- **Leadership's bonus:** suppose 270 engineers (15% of 1,800) sit in the top three boxes. A 15% bonus on an average CTC of ₹24,00,000 costs 270 × ₹3,60,000 = **₹9,72,00,000, about ₹9.7 crore a year**, more than all 60 exits cost, and most of it goes to people who weren't leaving.
- **A targeted fix:** coach the two managers and reopen internal moves (about ₹30,00,000), and correct the pay of 80 strong engineers below a compa-ratio of 0.90 by an average of 8% (80 × ₹24,00,000 × 8% = ₹1,53,60,000). Total: **about ₹1.8 crore.**

**Step 4: find the break-even.** ₹1,83,60,000 ÷ ₹9,55,920 = about 19. The targeted fix pays for itself if it prevents 19 of the 60 exits, roughly a third.

**The lesson:** the number doesn't decide for you, but it changes the conversation. It turns "should we pay a bonus?" into "which fix prevents the most regrettable exits for the money?"

## Where experts disagree

- **Exclusive or inclusive talent management?** Supporters of the war for talent say scarce capability drives results. Critics such as Pfeffer (2001) and Malcolm Gladwell (2002) pointed to Enron's star culture, and Groysberg, Lee and Nanda (2008) found that stars' performance often falls after they move, because it relied on their old team and resources. The "A positions" view sits between the two.
- **Is engagement anything new?** Critics argue it repackages older ideas such as job satisfaction, involvement and commitment, and that commercial survey indexes mix the conditions for engagement with engagement itself. Macey and Schneider (2008) tried to separate its meanings as a trait, a state and a behaviour. Defenders reply that vigour and absorption predict outcomes beyond satisfaction.
- **Should you tell people they are high potential?** Telling them can motivate and retain them; not telling them avoids fixing a fragile forecast and demotivating the rest. There is no settled answer, which is why transparent criteria matter.
- **Pay or manager?** Much Western research finds managers and growth matter more than pay for quitting. In India's hot tech markets, pay jumps drive many exits. Both can be true: an outside offer pulls hardest on people whose manager has already made leaving easy.

A good answer defines its terms (whose talent, which engagement), uses evidence from more than one tradition, and recommends a design that fits the organisation's strategy and labour market.

## In India

- **Attrition benchmarks.** Aon's October 2026 survey put attrition at about 18% in 2026, up from 16.2% in 2025. EY's *Future of Pay 2026* reported 16.4% for 2025, and 14.1% in GCCs. Compare against your own sector and role family, not a national average.
- **Notice periods and buy-outs.** Notice periods of 60 to 90 days are common in IT and GCCs, and new employers often buy them out. A long notice period gives time for an honest conversation, but holding people to it rarely changes a decision.
- **Non-competes and counter-offers.** Post-employment non-compete clauses are generally void under section 27 of the Indian Contract Act, 1872, so retention has to be earned rather than contracted. Counter-offers should fit the pay structure; see [offers and negotiation](/handbooks/recruitment/offers-and-negotiation/).
- **Start with managers.** India's engaged share fell from 33% to 23% between 2022 and 2025, and manager engagement fell fastest, so manager selection and support are the first lever.
- **Employee data.** Attrition-prediction models and engagement analytics use personal data, and the DPDP Act's core duties apply from 13 May 2027. Tell people what you collect and why, and report survey results only for groups large enough to protect anonymity.
