models:
  - id: triple-test-industry
    name: "Triple test for 'industry'"
    by: "Supreme Court of India (seven judges), Bangalore Water Supply v. A. Rajappa"
    year: "1978"
    idea: "An activity is an industry if it is systematic, organised through employer–worker cooperation, and produces goods or services for human wants, whether or not for profit."
    use: "Deciding whether a dispute pending under the old Act, involving a hospital, school, club or trust, is an industrial dispute."
    watch: "Since Jai Bir Singh (2026) it governs only disputes pending under the repealed Act; new disputes turn on the IR Code's own text."
    cite: "Bangalore Water Supply & Sewerage Board v. A. Rajappa (1978) 2 SCC 213; State of U.P. v. Jai Bir Singh (nine judges, 20 August 2026)."
  - id: dominant-duty-test
    name: "Dominant-duty test for 'worker'"
    by: "Supreme Court of India, H.R. Adyanthaya v. Sandoz (India) and S.K. Maini"
    year: "1994"
    idea: "Classify a person by the main duties they actually do, not their title; only people doing the kinds of work the law lists are workers."
    use: "Deciding who gets worker protections such as overtime, standing orders and retrenchment compensation."
    watch: "The IR Code keeps a pay cut-off for supervisors, and titles such as 'executive' or 'manager' still mislead."
    cite: "H.R. Adyanthaya v. Sandoz (India) Ltd (1994) 5 SCC 737 (Constitution Bench)."
  - id: economic-reality-test
    name: "Economic reality test"
    by: "Supreme Court of India, Hussainbhai v. Alath Factory Thozhilali Union"
    year: "1978"
    idea: "Where workers produce for a business and depend on it for their living, its owner is their real employer, even if a contractor stands in between."
    use: "Testing whether contract or platform arrangements hide a real employment relationship."
    watch: "Fact-heavy; later rulings such as Balwant Rai Saluja (2014) put more weight on effective control and supervision."
    cite: "Hussainbhai v. Alath Factory Thozhilali Union (1978) 4 SCC 257."
  - id: effective-control-test
    name: "Effective and absolute control test"
    by: "Supreme Court of India, Balwant Rai Saluja v. Air India"
    year: "2014"
    idea: "A principal employer is the employer of contract workers only if it exercises effective and absolute control and supervision over them."
    use: "Designing and auditing outsourced canteen, security and facility contracts."
    watch: "A high bar for workers to meet; read it with the sham-contract test, which looks past the paperwork."
    cite: "Balwant Rai Saluja v. Air India Ltd (2014) 9 SCC 407."
  - id: sham-contract-test
    name: "Sham-contract test (no automatic absorption)"
    by: "Supreme Court of India (Constitution Bench), Steel Authority of India v. National Union Waterfront Workers"
    year: "2001"
    idea: "Abolishing contract labour doesn't automatically make the workers permanent, but if the contract is a sham, they are the principal employer's own employees."
    use: "Assessing regularisation claims by long-serving contract workers doing core work."
    watch: "Workers must prove a sham case by case; later rulings such as Jaggo (2024) push back on long 'temporary' engagement by the State."
    cite: "Steel Authority of India Ltd v. National Union Waterfront Workers (2001) 7 SCC 1."
  - id: reasonable-accommodation-rpwd
    name: "Reasonable accommodation"
    by: "Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act 2016; Supreme Court of India, Vikash Kumar v. UPSC"
    year: "2016; 2021"
    idea: "Equality requires necessary and proportionate adjustments for persons with disabilities, unless they impose a disproportionate or undue burden."
    use: "Responding to a request for adjusted duties, hours, equipment or assessments."
    watch: "There is no numeric test for 'disproportionate'; decide case by case and record your reasoning."
    cite: "Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act 2016; Vikash Kumar v. UPSC (2021) 5 SCC 370."
  - id: budd-efficiency-equity-voice
    name: "Efficiency, equity and voice"
    by: "John W. Budd"
    year: "2004"
    idea: "Employment relationships, and the laws that govern them, should balance efficiency, equity (fair treatment) and voice (a say in decisions)."
    use: "Evaluating whether a law or policy, such as the IR Code's 300-worker thresholds, strikes a fair balance."
    watch: "It names the goals but not how to weigh them when they conflict."
    cite: "Budd, J. W. (2004) Employment with a Human Face: Balancing Efficiency, Equity, and Voice. Cornell University Press."

    type: written
    level: advanced
    q: "Assess whether raising the threshold for prior government permission for lay-off, retrenchment and closure from 100 to 300 workers strikes a fair balance between employers and workers. (About 200 words.)"
    why: "Model answer: The change, in Chapter X of the IR Code, applies to factories, mines and plantations, and states can set a higher figure. Employers argue that the old 100-worker threshold discouraged formal hiring, because firms stayed small or used contract labour to avoid needing permission to adjust. Budd's framework of efficiency, equity and voice helps judge the balance. Efficiency may improve if mid-sized plants hire more formally, but evidence on whether the old rule really held back hiring is contested, so the gain is uncertain. Equity depends on what replaces permission: retrenched workers still receive notice, compensation of 15 days' average pay per year of service and a re-skilling fund payment, which is modest for long-serving workers in a weak labour market. A balanced conclusion is that the higher threshold is defensible only alongside early consultation, prompt payment and real re-skilling support. HR can provide much of that through policy even where the law does not require it, and should measure whether it does."

## Start with the map: 29 laws, four Codes and what sits outside

The CFO's instinct is half right. On 21 November 2025 four Labour Codes replaced 29 central labour laws, so each area now has one central text with shared definitions. But the Codes didn't replace everything, and they work through rules that each government makes.

Several laws HR uses every week sit outside the Codes and still apply in full:

- the POSH Act 2013;
- the Digital Personal Data Protection (DPDP) Act 2023 and its 2025 Rules;
- the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (RPwD) Act 2016 and the Transgender Persons (Protection of Rights) Act 2019;
- the Apprentices Act 1961;
- each state's Shops and Establishments Act, professional tax and labour welfare fund laws;
- the Income-tax Act 2025 for salary TDS, in force since 1 April 2026.

Labour is on the Concurrent List, so states legislate too. Where a state law and a Central Code genuinely conflict, the Central law generally prevails unless the state law has the President's assent; where both can be obeyed, follow the stricter or more beneficial rule. The [four Labour Codes chapter](/handbooks/labour-law/the-four-labour-codes/) lists every Act each Code replaced.

## How to read a Code: appropriate government, rules and the gap

A Code sets duties and some key numbers. Most of the working detail (forms, registers, procedures and often the numbers) sits in rules made by the **appropriate government**. Reading a Code therefore means working down three layers.

Ask three questions, in order:

1. **Which government is appropriate?** The Centre for its own sphere, such as mines, major ports, banking and insurance, and central public sector undertakings. Under the Social Security Code the Centre is also appropriate for an establishment with branches in more than one state. For wages, IR and OSH matters at an ordinary private office or factory, it is the state.
2. **Are that government's rules final?** The Central Rules under all four Codes were notified on 8 May 2026 (some sources say 9 May). Karnataka, Goa and Delhi had published drafts but not final rules by early November 2026, and sources disagree about some Karnataka rules, so check the state gazette.
3. **What fills the gap?** Draft rules aren't enforceable. Until a state finalises its rules, its old rules and forms continue where they fit the Codes, as the government said when the Codes started. Duties that come straight from a Code apply regardless.

Add a fourth check for laws outside the Codes: state Shops and Establishments Acts were not repealed, so a Karnataka office must also meet the Karnataka Act as amended from 4 September 2026. The amendment exempts some establishments registered under the OSH Code; whether that exemption covers the whole Act or only registration is not settled.

## The ten obligations every HR leader must know

Most of the Codes' day-to-day duties fall into ten groups. Underlined numbers come from the site's checked statutory data and show their legal source when you point at them; the handbook chapters give the detail.

  <KeyNumbers

      { value: "10", label: "employees: ESI, gratuity, maternity benefit and a POSH Internal Committee" },
      { value: "20", label: "employees for EPF; workers for a grievance redressal committee" },
      { value: "50", label: "employees for a crèche; contract workers for contract-labour rules" },
      { value: "300", label: "workers: standing orders, and permission to lay off, retrench or close" }
    ]}
  />
</Figure>

1. **Pay at least the minimum wage, on time, to everyone.** Minimum wages and timely payment now cover every employee in every establishment, with no wage ceiling. Monthly wages are due <Fact id="wages.monthlyPayDay" />. See [wages and pay](/handbooks/labour-law/wages-and-pay/).
2. **Use the single definition of wages.** Basic pay, DA and retaining allowance are wages; if excluded items such as HRA and conveyance exceed <Fact id="wages.halfRule" /> of total pay, the excess is added back. That base drives PF, gratuity, bonus, overtime and leave encashment.
3. **Settle exits fast.** Pay wages due within <Fact id="wages.separationPayDays" /> of a resignation, removal, dismissal or retrenchment, and gratuity within <Fact id="gratuity.paymentDays" /> of it becoming payable.
4. **Give every employee an appointment letter** in the prescribed format, on joining. See [contracts and fixed-term employment](/handbooks/labour-law/contracts-and-fixed-term-employment/).
5. **Enrol people in social security.** EPF applies from <Fact id="epf.coverageEmployees" /> and is mandatory on wages up to <Fact id="epf.wageCeiling" /> since 17 September 2026; ESI applies from <Fact id="esi.coverageEmployees" /> for employees earning up to <Fact id="esi.wageCeiling" />. Deposit both by the 15th of the next month. See [social security](/handbooks/labour-law/social-security-and-benefits/).
6. **Pay gratuity** of <Fact id="gratuity.daysPerYear" />' wages per completed year after <Fact id="gratuity.minYears" /> of service, or after <Fact id="gratuity.fixedTermMinYears" /> for fixed-term staff, up to <Fact id="gratuity.ceiling" />.
7. **Keep to hours, overtime and leave limits:** <Fact id="osh.dailyHours" /> a day and <Fact id="osh.weeklyHours" /> a week; overtime only with consent, at <Fact id="osh.overtimeMultiple" />; annual leave at <Fact id="leave.accrualDays" /> once a worker has worked <Fact id="leave.eligibilityDays" /> in the year. See [leave and working hours](/handbooks/labour-law/leave-and-working-hours/).
8. **Protect women at work:** <Fact id="maternity.weeks" /> of maternity benefit after <Fact id="maternity.minDaysWorked" /> of work in the year before delivery, and <Fact id="maternity.weeksAdoption" /> for adoptive mothers whatever the child's age since a March 2026 Supreme Court ruling; a crèche at 50 or more employees; night work only with written consent and safeguards; equal pay for the same or similar work, which now also protects transgender persons.
9. **Treat fixed-term and contract workers lawfully.** Fixed-term staff get the same hours, wages, allowances and benefits as permanent workers doing the same or similar work. Contract-labour rules apply at 50 or more contract workers, contract labour in core activities is restricted, and the principal employer pays if a contractor defaults on wages.
10. **Run the IR Code's voice and exit machinery:** a grievance redressal committee at 20 or more workers, standing orders at 300, 21 days' notice before changing wages, hours or leave, and on retrenchment <Fact id="notice.retrenchmentNoticeMonths" />'s notice, <Fact id="notice.retrenchmentCompensationDays" /> per completed year and <Fact id="notice.reskillingFundDays" /> to the re-skilling fund. See [termination, grievances and disputes](/handbooks/labour-law/termination-grievance-and-disputes/).

<Callout type="note" title="At strategic level (SHRM-SCP, CIPD Level 7)">
The ten obligations are a floor, not a strategy. Senior HR leaders decide how to meet them across locations with one standard: for example, applying the strictest state's overtime cap everywhere, or paying all leavers within two working days whatever the debate about scope. A single high standard is often cheaper to run than ten local ones, and easier to defend.
</Callout>

## Landmark judgments, and whether they survive the Codes

The newest case shows how courts treat old precedent under new Codes. In *State of U.P. v. Jai Bir Singh* (20 August 2026), nine judges wrote five opinions. They agreed on two points: the 1978 *Bangalore Water Supply* test still governs disputes pending under the repealed Industrial Disputes Act, and new disputes must be decided on the IR Code's own definition of 'industry', with older cases only persuasive. Four judges sketched a refined test, but it doesn't bind. Employers such as hospitals, schools and trusts should watch how tribunals now read the Code's exclusions for charitable and sovereign activity.

When you read an older judgment, ask one question: did the Code keep the words (the principle probably survives), write the principle in (codified), or change the words (look for newer rulings)?

## POSH, data protection and disability: the laws beside the Codes

Three laws outside the Codes carry some of HR's biggest risks.

**POSH Act 2013.** Every workplace with 10 or more employees needs an Internal Committee, presided over by a senior woman, with an external member and at least half its members women; each office of a multi-state employer needs its own. Complaints are due within three months (extendable by three), the inquiry must finish within 90 days, and the employer acts on the report within 60 days. In *Aureliano Fernandes v. State of Goa* (2023) the Supreme Court held that a rushed inquiry that denied the respondent a fair defence breached natural justice, and its follow-up orders pushed states to check committees district by district. Since 14 July 2025, companies' board reports must state POSH complaints received, disposed of and pending. See [POSH compliance](/handbooks/labour-law/posh-compliance/).

**DPDP Act 2023 and Rules 2025.** The Rules were notified on 13 November 2025 with an 18-month runway: consent-manager rules start around 13 November 2026 and the main duties around 13 May 2027. From then an employer may process employee data on consent or a 'legitimate use', which includes employment purposes. It must give clear notices when it asks for consent, keep reasonable security safeguards, report breaches to the Data Protection Board and to the people affected, and erase data once its purpose ends, unless another law, such as labour-register rules, requires it to be kept. Penalties reach ₹250 crore for failing to protect data. Start the employee-data inventory now.

**RPwD Act 2016.** It covers 21 specified disabilities, and a benchmark disability is 40% or more. Every establishment, private ones included, must have an equal opportunity policy, and equality includes **reasonable accommodation**: necessary, proportionate adjustments unless they impose a disproportionate burden (*Vikash Kumar v. UPSC*, 2021). Government establishments may not dispense with or demote an employee who acquires a disability in service. The Transgender Persons Act 2019 adds a ban on discrimination in employment and a duty to designate a complaint officer.

## Build the compliance year

The CFO wants one calendar. Give him one, built on the rule map, with five kinds of entry:

- **Monthly:** wages before the 7th; salary TDS deposited by the 7th; EPF and ESI by the 15th; state professional tax (Karnataka by the 20th).
- **Quarterly and half-yearly:** TDS statements, overtime caps (apply the strictest at each office), labour welfare fund where it is half-yearly, and minimum-wage and DA revisions, usually from April and October.
- **Annual:** statutory bonus within eight months of the year end, the leave-year close, POSH annual reports and the annual returns your rules require.
- **Event-driven:** appointment letters on joining, exit payments, 21 days' notice of change, retrenchment payments and accident reports.
- **Continuous:** registers (the Central Rules require five years), notices and records.

Give every line a status (notified, pending, transitional or in force), an owner and the evidence you will keep, and set an internal deadline two or three working days before each legal one.

## Where experts disagree

- **Flexibility or protection?** Higher thresholds for standing orders and retrenchment permission, and strike notice in every establishment, give employers flexibility. Unions see weaker protection, and ten central unions struck against the Codes in February 2026. Evidence on whether the old 100-worker rule held back formal hiring is contested.
- **Fixed-term work.** Parity and one-year gratuity are real gains for fixed-term staff, but critics fear fixed-term contracts will replace permanent jobs; employers reply that they replace informal contract labour instead.
- **Inspector or facilitator?** Web-based, randomised inspection and a duty to advise may cut rent-seeking, or may weaken enforcement where compliance is already poor.
- **Simpler, or just different?** One central framework promised simplicity. A year on, the patchwork of draft and final state rules means multi-state employers face more complexity during the transition, not less.

A good answer separates what the Code says from how it is being implemented, and uses Budd's efficiency, equity and voice to weigh the trade-offs.

## In India: practice beyond the statute book

- **Enforcement has changed shape.** Inspections are increasingly web-based and randomised, employers often get a notice to comply before prosecution, and many first offences that carry only a fine can be compounded. See [penalties](/handbooks/labour-law/penalties/).
- **Courts are in transition too.** Old Labour Courts and Tribunals keep hearing cases until the new Industrial Tribunals are set up, and disputes pending under the Industrial Disputes Act stay with them.
- **State law keeps moving.** Karnataka's Shops Act changes from 4 September 2026 include registration valid until closure, a service certificate within 7 days of a request and a ban on keeping employees' original documents. See [state rules](/handbooks/labour-law/state-specific-rules/).
- **Gig and platform work.** The Social Security Code recognises gig and platform workers, and a National Social Security Board was set up in October 2026, but no national scheme or aggregator contribution rate had been notified. Karnataka's own 2025 Act already charges aggregators a welfare fee on payouts.
- **What's still pending** is tracked in [what's next](/handbooks/labour-law/whats-next/). Review it every quarter.
