## Look beneath the problem first

Absence, attrition and low effort are what you can see. The causes sit lower down: how people get to work, how their supervisor treats them, whether the reward feels fair, whether the job gives them anything back. HR's first job is diagnosis, because a fix aimed at the wrong cause wastes money and trust.

In the Pune plant, the visible problem is 14% night-shift absence. A bigger bonus only works if money is what's missing. Before agreeing to it, ask what else could be keeping people away.

Each cause below the line matches one of the models in this module. Most of what you need to diagnose a motivation problem comes from five questions:

1. Are basic needs met? (needs theories)
2. Can people do what's asked, and do they trust the reward? (expectancy)
3. Does it feel fair? (equity)
4. Is the work itself worth doing? (job design)
5. Is the team working, or still fighting? (team stages)

## Needs: what people want from work

**Maslow** proposed that people satisfy basic needs before higher ones. It's the most famous model in management and the least supported by evidence in its strict form. It still works as a prompt: you can't build engagement on top of unsafe transport or late salaries.

## Expectancy and fairness: the sums in people's heads

**Vroom's expectancy theory** says effort depends on three beliefs, multiplied together. If any one of them is near zero, effort is near zero too.

<Figure title="Expectancy theory" caption="All three must be high for effort to be high: a zero anywhere makes the whole product zero.">
  <Formula
    parts={[
      { value: "Expectancy", label: "can I do it?" },
      { op: "×" },
      { value: "Instrumentality", label: "will doing it get me the reward?" },
      { op: "×" },
      { value: "Valence", label: "do I value the reward?" }
    ]}
    result={{ value: "Motivational force", label: "the effort people choose to give" }}
  />
</Figure>

<Worked title="Worked example: will doubling the bonus work?">

These are illustrative scores from 0 to 1 for a typical night-shift operator. They are judgements from the diagnosis, not measurements.

| Belief | Today | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Expectancy: "I can attend every shift" | 0.4 | The last bus leaves at 5:30 a.m. on Fridays, before shift end |
| Instrumentality: "Attending gets me the bonus" | 0.5 | One late swipe cancels the whole month's bonus |
| Valence: "I want ₹2,000" | 0.3 | Many earn more from a second job |
| **Motivational force** | **0.06** | 0.4 × 0.5 × 0.3 |

**Option 1: double the bonus.** Valence might rise to 0.5, giving 0.4 × 0.5 × 0.5 = **0.10**. The cost doubles and effort barely moves.

**Option 2: fix transport and the bonus rule.** Add a Friday bus (expectancy 0.9). Prorate the bonus instead of cancelling it for one late swipe (instrumentality 0.9). That gives 0.9 × 0.9 × 0.3 = **0.24**, four times today's figure, without raising the bonus at all.

The lesson: when any one of the three beliefs is low, raising the reward is the weakest lever.

</Worked>

**Adams' equity theory** adds comparison. People weigh what they put in and get out against others. In many Indian plants, contract workers do similar work to permanent staff for less pay and fewer benefits. If the attendance bonus applies only to permanent staff, contract workers on the same line may see it as one more unfairness. Feeling under-rewarded leads people to cut effort, push for more, or leave.

## Goals and the needs that keep people going

**Goal-setting theory** (Locke and Latham) is among the best-supported findings in work psychology. Specific, challenging goals, with feedback and real commitment, raise performance more than "do your best". **Self-determination theory** (Deci and Ryan) explains what sustains effort when no one is watching. People need *autonomy* (some say in how they work), *competence* (getting better at something) and *relatedness* (belonging to a group that matters to them).

For HR this has practical consequences:

- Write targets and KRAs as specific outcomes with dates.
- Give people regular feedback against them.
- Leave room for how the work gets done.
- Treat recognition from peers and leaders as part of reward, not an extra.

<Callout type="note" title="At strategic level (SHRM-SCP, CIPD Level 7)">
Senior HR is judged on systems, not single fixes. Ask whether your performance, reward and job-design systems together support autonomy, competence and belonging, or whether one undoes the others. A forced-ranking appraisal, for example, can undercut the teamwork your engagement programme is trying to build.
</Callout>

## Designing jobs people want to do

Hackman and Oldham's **job characteristics model** gives five levers. *Skill variety*, *task identity* (doing a whole piece of work) and *task significance* make work feel meaningful. *Autonomy* creates ownership. *Feedback* lets people know how they're doing. Their "motivating potential score" multiplies autonomy and feedback, so a job that lacks either scores close to zero.

Cheap redesigns often work best:

- Show operators the quality-pass rate for their machine each morning.
- Let experienced operators train new joiners.
- Rotate people across lines so they build more skills.

## Teams: from forming to performing

**Tuckman's** stages describe how groups mature. The manager's role changes at each stage: give direction while forming, surface and settle conflict while storming, agree norms while norming, and step back while performing. When a team adjourns, recognise the work and capture the lessons.

## Where experts disagree

Expect these debates in any XLRI case discussion or CIPD Level 7 essay.

- **Needs models.** Popular hierarchies are weakly supported; studies don't find people moving neatly up Maslow's levels. Herzberg's results rest on how people tell stories about good and bad days, so many researchers treat the two-factor split as a useful lens rather than a law.
- **Rewards and interest.** Self-determination researchers argue that contingent rewards can weaken interest in work people already enjoy. Others reply that well-designed incentives raise performance on routine, measurable tasks. The answer depends on the task: rewards help with routine output and can backfire on creative work.
- **Goals.** Goal-setting has a strong evidence base, but over-narrow targets can encourage gaming, quality shortcuts and unethical behaviour. Sales targets and call-centre handling-time metrics are the classic cases.

A good answer names the model, shows where it fits the case, states its limit, and brings in a second model to cover the gap.

## In India

- **Shift work and safety.** Under the OSH Code, women can work night shifts only with their written consent and the safeguards the rules require. Transport is an obvious one. A transport gap is therefore a compliance issue as well as a motivation issue. See [leave and working hours](/handbooks/labour-law/leave-and-working-hours/).
- **Contract and permanent workers.** Similar work with different pay and benefits is a common source of resentment on Indian shop floors. Check who an incentive covers before you announce it.
- **Hierarchy and recognition.** In many Indian workplaces people don't challenge a supervisor openly, so problems surface as absence or exits rather than complaints. Skip-level conversations and anonymous pulse surveys bring them out earlier.
- **Family and social context.** Second jobs, study and family duties shape attendance, especially on night shifts. Flexible shift-swaps can cost less than incentives.
