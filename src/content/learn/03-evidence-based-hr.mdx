## Decide with four sources of evidence

At the Tamil Nadu NBFC, the business head is working from one source of evidence: his own reading of the market. He may be right. But a 15% rise for 600 relationship managers is a large, permanent cost, and it deserves a better test than one person's conviction.

Evidence-based practice means deciding with the best available evidence from several sources, each judged for how far it can be trusted. CIPD builds on the work of the Center for Evidence-Based Management (CEBMa), by Eric Barends, Denise Rousseau and Rob Briner, and names four sources.

| Source | In the NBFC case | Typical weakness |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Scientific research**: peer-reviewed studies, ideally systematic reviews | Research on why new hires leave in their first year | May come from a different context |
| **Organisational data**: HRMS, payroll, exit and survey data | Exits by tenure, branch and manager | Messy; shows patterns more than causes |
| **Practitioner expertise**: judgement from similar situations | Area managers' view of the rival's hiring | Bias and overconfidence |
| **Stakeholders' values and concerns**: what the people affected care about | What relationship managers say about targets | Loud voices drown out quiet ones |

No single source is enough. Data without judgement misreads context; judgement without data repeats old beliefs. CIPD works through six steps, and they loop: checking the result raises the next question.

At the Appraise step, weigh what you find. A systematic review usually counts for more than one study, and one well-designed study for more than a consultant's survey of its clients. But the best design depends on the question: for "why are people leaving?", good interviews may beat any number (Petticrew and Roberts, 2003). The business head's view is practitioner expertise: one input, not the answer.

## Ask an answerable question, then design the study

"Why is attrition up?" is a fair worry but a poor question. It doesn't say about whom, compared with what, or what result would count. CEBMa suggests a five-part check called PICOC.

Then choose the design that can answer it:

- **Existing records** (HRMS, payroll, exits) are fast and cheap, and show patterns. On their own they rarely show causes.
- **A survey** reaches many people. Use established scales where they exist, one idea per item and neutral wording. "My manager gives me clear targets and support" asks two things at once.
- **Interviews and focus groups** explain why. Code the answers into themes (thematic analysis; Braun and Clarke, 2006) rather than quoting the most vivid one.
- **A pilot with a comparison group** is the strongest practical test of cause: make the change in some branches, and compare them with similar branches that wait.

Two checks catch most design mistakes. **Sampling:** in a large workforce, a random sample of about 400 gives a margin of error of roughly ±5 percentage points at 95% confidence. A sample of volunteers can be biased however large it is. **Ethics:** tell people what you collect and why, keep responses anonymous, report no group smaller than five, and keep data only as long as you need it.

## Read the statistics an HR leader needs

You rarely need to run the statistics yourself. You do need to read them well enough to spot a weak argument, including your own.

### Mean, median and spread

The mean adds everything up and divides; the median is the middle value. Pay is skewed by a few high earners, so the median describes the typical employee better. In a branch where nine relationship managers earn ₹3.5–4.5 lakh and the branch head earns ₹18 lakh, the mean is pulled up to about ₹5.4 lakh while the median stays at about ₹4 lakh. Look at the spread too: two teams with the same average engagement score can be very different places to work.

### Correlation is a lead, not a verdict

A correlation (r, from −1 to +1) shows how closely two things move together. Squared, it gives the share of variation explained, so r = 0.3 explains only 9%. A correlation can't tell you which way cause runs, or what else lies behind it. If branches with new managers lose more people, the managers may be the cause. Or new managers may have been sent to the hardest branches, a **confounder** that drives both.

### Regression in one picture

Regression estimates the effect of each factor while holding the others constant. Software finds the numbers; your job is to read them.

### Significance, size and trust

A p-value is the chance of seeing a difference at least this large if there were really none. Below 0.05 is the usual convention for "significant", but that says nothing about whether the difference matters. With thousands of records, tiny gaps become significant, so read the **effect size** as well. Jacob Cohen's rough rule calls a standardised difference (d) of 0.2 small, 0.5 medium and 0.8 large.

Two more tests apply to any measure. **Reliability** is consistency: a stable team should get similar scores if it takes a survey twice. For a multi-item scale, a Cronbach's alpha of about 0.7 is the usual floor. **Validity** is accuracy: does the score measure what it claims to? An "engagement" score that mostly tracks liking for one's manager can be reliable without being valid.

Three rules keep these honest:

- Use the same base for every rate, usually average headcount for the period, and say when you have annualised a part-year rate.
- Split turnover into voluntary and involuntary, and regretted and not regretted, because each has different causes.
- Fix the start and end points of time to fill, list what goes into cost per hire, and agree the parts of quality of hire (it has no standard formula) before you report it.

### When people metrics are audited: BRSR Core

SEBI requires India's 1,000 largest listed companies by market value to publish a Business Responsibility and Sustainability Report (BRSR). A subset of it, BRSR Core, must be independently checked. Three of its nine attributes are about people:

| BRSR Core attribute | People metrics (paraphrased) |
| --- | --- |
| Employee wellbeing and safety | Spending on employee and worker wellbeing as % of total revenue; lost-time injury frequency rate (LTIFR) per million person-hours; fatalities; permanent disabilities |
| Gender diversity | Gross wages paid to women as % of total wages; POSH complaints: number, as % of women employees and workers, and number upheld |
| Inclusive development | Wages paid to people employed in smaller towns as % of total wage cost |

Checking was phased in from 2023–24 and reaches the top 1,000 listed companies in 2026–27. Since March 2025, companies may choose an independent assessment instead of assurance. Either way, these people metrics are now audit-grade data. They need written definitions, a named owner and an audit trail.

## Make the numbers change a decision

Most HR dashboards change nothing. John Boudreau and Peter Ramstad's **LAMP** model explains why: measurement improves decisions only when four parts work together.

| Part | The question it answers | What failure looks like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Logic | What is the causal story linking people to business results? | Measuring whatever is easy to count |
| Analytics | Which methods give valid conclusions? | Reading cause into a correlation |
| Measures | Are the data accurate, complete, timely and consistent? | Three attrition rates in one meeting |
| Process | How do findings reach the people who decide, in time? | A good analysis nobody acts on |

Process is where **data storytelling** comes in. Leaders remember a story, not a table: what is happening, what it costs, why, what to do, and how you'll know it worked. Lead with the decision you need, use one chart per point, and say plainly what the data can't tell you.

<Worked title="Worked example: diagnosing the NBFC's attrition spike">

All figures are an Example for the fictional NBFC. Follow the steps in order.

**1. Ask.** Would a 15% rise for relationship managers cut voluntary exits over the next two quarters, compared with a cheaper fix aimed at the main cause?

**2. Check the measure.** The HRMS shows 108 resignations in six months, an annualised 36% on an average headcount of 600. Checking them against payroll shows that 12 were transfers to the gold-loan business, coded as resignations. The true figure is 96, or 32% annualised: still high, but a smaller spike than it looked.

**3. Slice the data.** Split the 96 exits by length of service, and by whether the branch got a new manager in the last six months. Rates are annualised by doubling the six-month figure.

| Annualised attrition (Example) | 14 new-manager branches | 26 other branches |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Under 12 months' service | 80% (28 of 70) | 47% (26 of 110) |
| 12 months or more | 20% (14 of 140) | 20% (28 of 280) |
| All | 40% (42 of 210) | 28% (54 of 390) |

Across all 40 branches, new joiners are leaving at 60% (54 of 180) and experienced relationship managers at 20% (42 of 420), below last year's 24%. The spike is among new joiners, and it is worst under new branch managers.

**4. Test the pay story.** The median compa-ratio of leavers is 0.96; of stayers, 0.95. Pay position does not separate the two groups. Medians are used because a few senior relationship managers on high pay would distort the means.

**5. Bring in the other three sources.** Sixty exit interviews, coded into themes, point to early target pressure with no ramp-up period (26), the manager (19) and better pay elsewhere (15). Pay is the easiest reason to give, so read that theme with care. Area managers confirm the rival is hiring, but mostly experienced staff. Research on newcomer adjustment links role clarity and feeling accepted in the first months to lower turnover (Bauer and colleagues, 2007).

**6. Look for a confounder.** Five of the 14 new-manager branches are also new branches in tougher markets. But new-joiner exits run at about 75% a year in the nine established branches with new managers too, so the manager change still stands out. This is strong evidence, not proof.

**The recommendation.** A 15% rise for all 600 would cost about ₹3.8 crore a year (600 × average CTC of ₹4.2 lakh × 15%) and targets a cause the data doesn't support. Instead, introduce a 90-day ramp-up with lower first-quarter targets, a buddy for every new joiner and coaching for the 14 new branch managers, at about ₹35 lakh. Run it in seven of those branches and use the other seven as the comparison group. Track new-joiner attrition every month for two quarters.

## Where experts disagree

Expect these debates in a CIPD 5CO02 or 7CO04 assignment and in XLRI research-methods discussions.

- **Is "evidence-based" neutral?** Critics argue that medicine-style hierarchies of evidence undervalue context and qualitative knowledge, and that "evidence-based" can become a slogan that hides whose interests are served (Learmonth and Harding, 2006; Morrell, 2008). The four-source model answers this partly by giving stakeholders' concerns a formal place.
- **How much can HR analytics deliver?** Reviews find that most HR analytics is still reporting rather than analysis (Marler and Boudreau, 2017), and that HR often lacks the skills, data and theory to answer strategic questions (Angrave and colleagues, 2016). HR professionals' beliefs also often differ from research findings (Rynes, Colbert and Brown, 2002). Supporters reply that simple, well-framed diagnosis, like the worked example, already changes decisions.
- **Metrics get gamed.** Goodhart's and Campbell's laws warn that once a measure becomes a target, it stops measuring well. Push time to fill down hard enough and quality of hire falls.
- **Prediction and privacy.** Individual "flight-risk" scores built from email or badge data raise questions of consent, accuracy and fairness, and can damage trust even when they are lawful.

A good answer weighs all four sources, names the limits of each, and says what further evidence would change the recommendation.

## In India

- **Audit-grade people data.** BRSR Core makes some people metrics independently checked data for large listed companies (see the table above), and their suppliers may be asked for the same.
- **Data protection.** The Digital Personal Data Protection (DPDP) Act, 2023 and its 2025 Rules cover employee data, and the main duties for employers apply from May 2027. Say what you collect and why, use it only for that purpose, keep it secure and set retention periods. See [people analytics and data literacy](/handbooks/hr-professional/people-analytics-and-data-literacy/).
- **Say who you counted.** Many workplaces mix permanent, fixed-term, contract and gig workers, often on different systems. State whether each metric includes contract and fixed-term workers; BRSR and the Labour Codes both separate employees from workers.
- **Surveys need extra care.** Where people fear being identified, keep the anonymity promise. Offer surveys in the languages people work in, and back-translate items so that a tested scale stays valid.
- **Benchmarks are context, not a diagnosis.** All-India salary and attrition surveys average across very different sectors. Compare with your own sector and role, and above all with your own trend.
