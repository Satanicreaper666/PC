## Start with a reward strategy

The Pune CFO is asking a structural question, but there is an earlier one: what is this company's pay for, and where does it want to sit against the market? Without answers, every restructure and every equity fix becomes a one-off negotiation.

Most compensation courses, XLRI's included, organise reward around four policy choices from Milkovich and Newman's **pay model**:

- **Internal alignment:** how jobs compare inside the firm.
- **External competitiveness:** how pay compares with the market.
- **Employee contributions:** what earns more, such as performance, skills or seniority.
- **Administration:** budgets, communication and governance.

The choice leaders notice first is market position.

**Efficiency-wage theory** explains why leading can pay for itself: above-market pay can raise effort and loyalty and reduce quits (Akerlof, 1982; Shapiro and Stiglitz, 1984). Few firms take one position for everything. A common pattern is to lead for scarce skills, match for most roles, and lag where security or purpose makes up the difference. EY's *Future of Pay 2026* reported premiums of 30–40% for scarce AI skills in India.

Pay is only part of what people weigh. The **total rewards** view (WorldatWork; Armstrong and Brown) adds benefits, development and the work environment.

What this means for HR: write the philosophy on one page. Name the market you compare with, your target position for each job family, the pay mix, what earns more, and how pay will be explained to employees. Every later decision in this module should trace back to it.

## Valuing jobs: job evaluation, grades and market pricing

### Sizing the job, not the person

Internal fairness starts with sizing jobs. Methods range from ranking whole jobs and writing grade descriptions to **point-factor** schemes. The best known is the Hay Guide Chart-Profile method, usually dated to 1951, which scores know-how, problem solving and accountability. Mercer and WTW offer similar factor-based systems. The points decide the grade, and the grade carries a pay range.

### Grades or broadbands

Traditional structures have many narrow grades. **Broadbands** collapse several grades into a few wide bands, so people can grow in pay through skills and scope, not only through promotion.

### Pricing jobs against the market

Salary surveys report market pay by job and level, usually as percentiles: the 25th, the median (50th) and the 75th. Match jobs on content, not titles, and age the data to a common date. Set each range's midpoint at your target market position, then read each person's place in the range with the **compa-ratio**.

Two related ideas help:

- **Range penetration** shows how far someone is between the minimum and maximum. With a range of ₹9,00,000 to ₹15,00,000, pay of ₹9,60,000 is (₹9,60,000 − ₹9,00,000) ÷ ₹6,00,000 = 10% of the way in.
- **Pay compression** happens when new hires come in at or above experienced staff, as the Pune engineers discovered.

A merit matrix, which gives larger increases to strong performers who sit low in their range, slowly corrects position in range. The [salary band designer](/toolkit/salary-band-designer/) and the [increment budget allocator](/toolkit/increment-budget-allocator/) do the arithmetic.

## Paying for performance: incentives, benefits and executive pay

Variable pay works over two time horizons:

- **Short-term:** annual bonus, sales commission, team gainsharing and profit sharing.
- **Long-term:** ESOPs, restricted stock units (RSUs), stock appreciation rights and phantom stock, mostly for senior staff and in technology firms.

In EY's 2026 survey, variable pay in India averaged 16.1% of fixed pay. Does it work? The evidence is clearer than the debate suggests:

- Financial incentives raise the quantity of output more reliably than its quality (Jenkins, Mitra, Gupta and Shaw, 1998).
- At Safelite Glass, a switch to piece rates raised output by about 44%, and roughly half of that came from attracting and keeping more productive people (Lazear, 2000). Pay systems shape who joins and stays, not just effort.
- Tangible rewards for tasks people already enjoy can weaken their interest, though the effect depends on the task. [Module 2](/learn/people-at-work/) covers this and the expectancy test every incentive should pass.

Two economic theories explain senior pay. **Agency theory** (Jensen and Meckling, 1976) says that when owners can't watch effort, pay should follow outcomes, which shifts risk onto managers. **Tournament theory** (Lazear and Rosen, 1981) treats promotions as prizes, which explains steep pay jumps near the top and why such contests can damage cooperation. Bebchuk and Fried (2004) argue that executives often shape their own pay through boards they influence. In India, a public company's total managerial remuneration is capped at 11% of net profits unless shareholders approve more, and a listed company must disclose each director's pay as a ratio of the median employee's pay (Companies Act, 2013, section 197).

Benefits are either statutory (PF, ESI, gratuity, maternity benefit, statutory bonus) or voluntary (health and life cover, NPS, extra leave, wellbeing support). Voluntary benefits are where employers can differentiate, and different groups value them very differently, so ask employees before you spend.

What this means for HR: before approving any incentive, check that people can influence the measure, add quality and conduct measures, set caps and clawbacks, and pay on time.

## Pay equity and transparency

Back in Pune, two women engineers earn 12% less than a man hired last year into the same grade. The Code on Wages bars gender discrimination in pay for the same or similar work, and an employer may not fix an inequality by cutting anyone's pay. HR's question is whether the gap has a legitimate, documented explanation.

A sound pay-equity review has four steps:

1. **Compare like with like:** the same grade or role, using total fixed pay.
2. **Explain each gap** with factors you can document, such as performance, relevant experience or a market premium recorded at the time of hiring.
3. **Correct unexplained gaps** to a costed timeline.
4. **Fix the cause:** give hiring managers pay ranges, stop basing offers on pay history, and calibrate ratings, because biased ratings make gaps look "explained".

The [pay equity analyser](/toolkit/pay-equity-analyzer/) works out both the overall gap and the like-for-like gap within roles.

Many Indian GCCs run hiring, payroll or reward for European entities, so the **EU Pay Transparency Directive** (2023/970) reaches their processes. It requires a pay range before interview and bans questions about pay history. It makes pay-secrecy clauses void and gives workers a right to information about pay levels by sex for work of equal value. Where an unexplained gap of 5% or more in a category of workers isn't fixed within six months, a joint pay assessment is required, and the burden of proof shifts to the employer.

## Designing a CTC under the Labour Codes

CTC is a market convention, not a legal term. The law works with "wages" (the Labour Codes) and with "salary" and "perquisites" (income tax), so HR has to translate between CTC, gross pay and take-home pay.

Since 21 November 2025, "wages" means all remuneration minus a fixed list of exclusions, such as HRA, conveyance, reimbursement of job expenses, the employer's PF contribution, overtime and commission. If the excluded items come to more than <Fact id="wages.halfRule" /> of total remuneration, the excess is added back into wages. A general special allowance isn't excluded, so it already counts. The rule does not say basic pay must be half of CTC.

That wage figure drives most statutory costs:

- **PF:** <Fact id="epf.employerRate" /> from each side, on wages up to the <Fact id="epf.wageCeiling" /> ceiling if the employer caps contributions.
- **Gratuity:** <Fact id="gratuity.daysPerYear" /> of wages for each completed year, on a <Fact id="gratuity.divisor" />-day month, with no wage ceiling. A monthly provision of about 4.81% of wages covers it.
- **ESI and statutory bonus:** eligibility depends on wages up to <Fact id="esi.wageCeiling" /> for ESI and ₹21,000 a month for bonus, so a restructure can move people across the line.
- **Overtime and leave encashment** use the same base.

<Worked title="Worked example: restructuring a CTC so allowances stay within the rule">

A senior engineer in Pune has a CTC of ₹1,25,000 a month (₹15 lakh a year), set before the Codes. Treat the flexible-benefits bucket (fuel, driver, phone, travel) as excluded items: conveyance, travel and job-expense reimbursements. How each item is classified is open to interpretation, so check your own. Employer PF is left out of the 50% test here, because whether it belongs in "total remuneration" isn't settled. All figures are an example, monthly, in rupees.

**Step 1: run the 50% test on the old structure.** Basic is ₹36,400, HRA ₹18,200, flexible benefits ₹45,000 and special allowance ₹20,850, so gross pay is ₹1,20,450. The excluded items (HRA plus flexible benefits) come to ₹63,200, which is 52.5% of gross. Half of gross is ₹60,225, so ₹2,975 is added back:

- Wages = ₹36,400 basic + ₹20,850 special allowance + ₹2,975 added back = **₹60,225**.
- The old structure provided gratuity on basic only (₹1,750 a month). On the true wage base it should be about ₹2,895, so it was under-provided by about ₹1,145 a month.

**Step 2: restructure so the excluded items stay under half.**

| Monthly, ₹ | Old | New |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Basic | 36,400 | 40,000 |
| HRA (half of basic) | 18,200 | 20,000 |
| Flexible benefits | 45,000 | 35,600 |
| Special allowance | 20,850 | 22,400 |
| **Gross pay** | **1,20,450** | **1,18,000** |
| Excluded share of gross | 52.5% | 47.1% |
| Wages under the Codes | 60,225 | 62,400 |
| Employer PF | 1,800 | 3,000 |
| Gratuity provision | 1,750 | 3,000 |
| Group insurance | 1,000 | 1,000 |
| **CTC** | **1,25,000** | **1,25,000** |
| Employee PF deducted | 1,800 | 3,000 |
| **Take-home before income tax and PT** | **1,18,650** | **1,15,000** |

In the old column, wages include the ₹2,975 add-back, PF is 12% of the old ₹15,000 ceiling, and gratuity was provided on basic only. In the new column, PF is 12% of the ₹25,000 ceiling and gratuity is provided on the full ₹62,400 of wages.

**Step 3: see where the money went.** Take-home falls by ₹3,650 a month, about 3%. Of that, ₹2,400 goes into PF (₹1,200 from each side, because the ceiling rose on 17 September 2026) and ₹1,250 into the gratuity provision (because gratuity is now provided on the full wage base). The restructure itself added little: once the add-back applied, the old structure's true wage base was already ₹60,225.

**Step 4: decide who absorbs the cost.**

- **CTC-neutral**, as above: no change to the budget; take-home falls by ₹3,650.
- **Absorb:** keep gross pay at ₹1,20,450 by putting the extra ₹2,450 into special allowance. Take-home falls only by the employee's extra ₹1,200 of PF, and CTC rises by about ₹2,570 a month, roughly 2%.
- **Split by band:** absorb for lower bands, where ₹3,650 hurts most, and go CTC-neutral for senior bands, with a statement showing the higher PF and gratuity value.

**The lesson:** the 50% rule rarely saves or costs much on its own. The money moves because the wage base is wider and the PF ceiling is higher. Under the new tax regime most flexible-benefit items no longer save tax anyway, so a simpler structure costs employees little.

</Worked>

Before you change anyone's structure, give the notice workers are owed (21 days for a change in wages under the Industrial Relations Code), bargain where a union represents them, and rewrite offer letters and payslips to show CTC, gross pay and expected take-home pay. The [take-home salary calculator](/toolkit/take-home-salary-calculator/) models individual cases.

Treat the Codes' restructure as a reward-strategy decision, not a payroll task. Who absorbs the cost is a choice about the employment deal: it changes take-home pay, retirement savings and trust at once. Bring leadership the options costed by band, with the effect on low earners shown first.

## Where experts disagree

- **Do incentives work?** Kohn (1993) argued that incentive plans cannot work, because they punish, damage relationships and kill interest. The evidence says they raise quantity, with real risks to quality, cooperation and ethics (Jenkins et al., 1998). Crowding-out is real but conditional, and popular books overstate it.
- **Pay matters more than people admit.** People play pay down in surveys but respond strongly to it in practice (Rynes, Gerhart and Minette, 2004). In India's tight tech and GCC markets, the pay jump on switching jobs drives much attrition.
- **Job evaluation or market pricing?** Job evaluation can carry old gender bias in its factor weights; market pricing imports whatever bias the market holds. Pay-transparency law is pushing firms back towards documented, factor-based structures.
- **Total rewards or packaging?** CTC counts money employees can't spend today, such as gratuity provisions and employer PF. Total-reward statements that present statutory costs as generosity can feel like spin.
- **Executive pay.** The link between CEO pay and company performance is weak (Jensen and Murphy, 1990), and managerial power shapes pay (Bebchuk and Fried, 2004). Others credit markets and better governance.

A good answer states the reward objective, uses evidence on how people actually respond to pay, and recommends a design that fits the context and the law, with its risks named.

## In India

- **Three numbers, two sets of law.** CTC is a convention; "wages" (Labour Codes) and "salary" (Income-tax Act, 2025) are legal ideas. Under the new tax regime, splitting pay between basic and allowances doesn't change the tax; it changes PF, gratuity and, under the old regime, the HRA exemption. See [CTC design and perquisites](/handbooks/tds/ctc-design-and-perquisites/) and [wages and pay](/handbooks/labour-law/wages-and-pay/).
- **The 2026 PF ceiling.** From 17 September 2026 the EPF wage ceiling rose from <Fact id="epf.wageCeilingBefore" /> to <Fact id="epf.wageCeiling" />. Employers that pay PF only up to the ceiling pay up to ₹1,200 more a month per employee, and the employee's deduction rises by the same amount.
- **Variable pay and ESOPs.** The Labour Ministry's FAQs say annual performance incentives are not wages, but FAQs are guidance, not law, and the final rules dropped a clarification on bonus and ESOPs. Keep variable pay genuinely variable and documented in a plan.
- **The market.** Aon's October 2026 survey projected average increases of 9.1% for 2027 and found 65.4% of firms compliant with the Wage Code; 42.8% expected the Codes to raise payroll costs by at least 1%. With budgets flat, differentiation drives pay outcomes.
- **State rules.** Karnataka, Goa and Delhi rules under the Codes were still in draft in early November 2026, and states notify their own bonus limits for establishments in their sphere. Check before you finalise a structure.
