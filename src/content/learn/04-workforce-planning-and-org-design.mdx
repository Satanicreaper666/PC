## Start with the work, not the headcount

The founders' number, 300 people, answers a question nobody has asked yet. Before anyone argues about headcount, HR needs three things from the business plan: what work there will be, which skills it needs, and when. Headcount, structure and payroll follow from those.

In the Gurugram brand, doubling revenue won't double every team. Orders drive warehouse work, customer contacts drive support, and the product roadmap drives tech. Some work can be automated, some bought in for a season, some done by people the brand already has. A plan built from these drivers, team by team, is usually smaller, cheaper and easier to defend than a top-down number.

That discipline is workforce planning, and most planning cycles run through the same five steps.

The output is not one headcount number. It is a plan by role, skill, location and month, with a cost and the assumptions behind it. Agree those assumptions with finance and the business heads early, because they are what people will argue about later. The [headcount planning model](/toolkit/headcount-planning-model/) does the month-by-month arithmetic once you have them.

## Forecast demand and supply

**Demand** is the people the work will need. Three methods cover most situations:

- **Driver-based (ratio) forecasting.** Divide the volume of work by what one person can handle. It suits operational roles with a clear driver, such as orders, tickets, beds or branches.
- **Trend and regression.** Relate past headcount to revenue or volume and project it forward. It works when the business model is stable, and misleads when it is changing.
- **Judgement.** Ask managers bottom-up, set limits top-down, or run Delphi rounds, in which experts revise their estimates anonymously, for capabilities you have never built before.

**Supply** is the people you will have if you do nothing. Start with today's headcount, then subtract expected leavers: resignations, retirements and fixed-term contracts that end. Add internal moves in and out, such as promotions and transfers. Larger firms track these moves as yearly probabilities in a transition (Markov) matrix. Check external supply too: whether the skills exist in that city, at what pay, and who else is hiring them.

The gap between demand and supply is what you must close. Remember that hires are not the same as growth. Expected leavers are roughly average headcount × attrition rate, so a team with high attrition may hire twice as many people as it grows by.

## Close each gap: build, buy, borrow or bridge

The Gurugram brand has four very different gaps. It needs a marketplace lead within weeks, data analysts for the next three years, 45 extra people in the warehouse for the festive peak, and an HR person to open the Bengaluru site. One answer won't fit all four.

SHRM's body of knowledge names four ways to close a gap:

- **Build:** develop your own people through trainee schemes, academies, coaching and stretch roles. It is slower, builds loyalty and firm-specific skill, and needs a pipeline started early.
- **Buy:** hire from the market. It is fastest for scarce or senior skills, and most expensive: pay premiums, agency fees and the risk that a star's results don't travel with them.
- **Borrow:** use contractors, consultants, agencies or gig workers for a period. It is flexible and quick to stop, but the knowledge leaves with them, and Indian law limits contract labour in core work.
- **Bridge:** move existing people into new roles through redeployment, transfers and promotions, often after short reskilling. It is quick and keeps know-how, but every move leaves a role to backfill.

Some frameworks add **bind** (keep the people you have), **bounce** (exit roles you no longer need) or **bot** (automate the work).

For HR, the point is to compare options on the same terms: time to productivity, full cost over the period, risk and legal limits. Borrowing a core capability for years creates dependency and, in India, may breach the ban on contract labour in core activities. At strategic level, Lepak and Snell's HR architecture makes the same choice by how valuable and how unique each skill is; see [strategic HRM](/learn/strategic-hrm/).

## Plan in scenarios, then commit

Next year's demand depends on things nobody controls: a festive season, a marketplace changing its fees, a competitor's price cut. A single forecast hides that uncertainty and makes the plan look more certain than it is.

Shell's planners, led by Pierre Wack, made **scenario planning** famous in the 1970s. The method is simple:

1. Pick the two or three uncertainties that move demand most.
2. Build three plausible scenarios, such as low, base and high.
3. Find the **no-regret moves** you need in every scenario, and commit to them now.
4. Hold **contingent moves** for the high or low case, each with a **trigger point** that tells you when to switch.

Peter Cappelli makes the same argument from supply-chain thinking: long-range forecasts are usually wrong, so plan in ranges and make errors cheap to correct. Short fixed-term contracts, internal moves and trained contractors adjust more easily than early permanent hires.

**Step 2: hires are growth plus expected leavers.** Leavers are estimated as average headcount × attrition.

| Group | Growth + leavers = hires | Attrition |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Warehouse | 40 + 32 = **72** | 40% |
| Customer support | 5 + 10 = **15** | 30% |
| Tech and data | 8 + 4 = **12** | 15% |
| Marketing | 6 + 7 = **13** | 20% |
| Corporate | 4 + 5 = **9** | 12% |
| **Total** | **63 + 58 = 121** | |

The founders asked for 120 more people. That is roughly the number of **hires** the plan needs, but headcount grows by only 63, because 58 of those hires replace people who leave.

**Step 3: choose a B for each gap.**

- **Warehouse:** build through a two-week induction academy; borrow a licensed contractor for loading, housekeeping and security; hire the festive surge on fixed-term contracts.
- **Support:** buy entry-level agents; the chatbot is already in the demand figure.
- **Tech and data:** buy most roles; bridge two operations analysts who already work with the data, and count their backfills.
- **Marketing:** buy the marketplace lead; borrow a performance-marketing agency; build two associate brand managers.
- **Corporate:** bridge an HR executive to run the Bengaluru site; buy the rest.

**Step 4: set scenarios and triggers for the most volatile group.**

| Scenario | Associates | Orders a day, month 12 | Signal at month 6 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Low (revenue × 1.5) | 75 | 9,000 | Near 7,500 a day: slow hiring |
| Base (revenue × 2) | 100 | 12,000 | Near 9,000 a day: stay on plan |
| High (revenue × 2.5) | 125 | 15,000 | Near 10,500 a day: open contingent roles |

The warehouse system, the induction academy and the data hires are no-regret moves. A second Bengaluru shift and extra fixed-term staff are contingent.

**Step 5: check the payroll.** Using example average CTCs (₹3 lakh in the warehouse, ₹4 lakh in support, ₹18 lakh in tech, ₹12 lakh in marketing, ₹10 lakh in corporate), the annual payroll run-rate rises from ₹14.2 crore to about ₹18.2 crore, up 28%. After a 9% pay rise the increase is about 39%: inside the CFO's 40% limit, but only just. Three hundred people at today's average cost would add about 67%.

**The answer to the board:** 243 people at year end, 121 hires, payroll within the limit, and a written list of the assumptions and triggers that would change the plan.

</Worked>

## Choose a structure that fits the strategy

Today the brand is organised by function: heads of marketing, operations, tech and finance report to the founders. That worked with one category and one website. Now the marketing head sets priorities for the website and two marketplaces at once, operations plans stock by warehouse rather than by channel, and every trade-off goes to the founders. The structure, not the people, is creating the queue.

Alfred Chandler's study of large American firms found that **structure follows strategy**: as companies diversified, functional structures gave way to divisional ones. The main forms are these:

| Form | Groups people by | Strengths | Risks | Fits when |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Functional | Expertise: marketing, operations, finance | Deep skills, low cost, clear careers | Silos; every trade-off climbs to the top | One main product or market, fairly stable |
| Divisional | Product, region or customer | Focus and clear accountability for results | Duplicated functions; divisions compete | Several distinct businesses or markets |
| Matrix | Two dimensions at once, such as category and function | Balances market focus with expertise | Two bosses, conflict and slow decisions | Both dimensions really matter |
| Network | A small core, with partners doing much of the work | Flexible, low fixed cost, quick to scale | Less control; depends on partners | Work can be specified and bought in |

Many consumer firms use hybrids. A "front-back" design, for example, organises the customer-facing front by channel and the back by product or function. For the Gurugram brand, a light matrix is the likely answer: category leads accountable for results, working with functional teams that keep their expertise.

Most reorganisations redraw boxes and lines, then stop. Jay Galbraith's **Star Model** says the chart is only one of five levers, and the five must fit. A matrix with rewards that pay each function for its own targets will fight itself.

## Set spans and layers by the type of work

The brand's warehouse supervisors each manage 20 associates, while its marketing team has six managers with two reports each. Both could be right, or both wrong. It depends on the work.

A **span of control** is the number of people reporting directly to a manager. **Layers** are the levels between the CEO and the front line. The two are linked: narrow spans need more layers to cover the same people. For a 1,000-person firm, an average span of 4 needs about five layers below the CEO, a span of 8 about four, and a span of 15 about three.

Set target spans by role type, then count layers. Watch for managers with one or two reports, who are often senior specialists given a title, and for "player-coaches" carrying a full individual target, who have little time left for their people.

## Where experts disagree

- **How far ahead can you plan?** Classic manpower planning forecasts years ahead. Cappelli argues most long-range forecasts are wrong and favours short cycles and options. The answer depends on lead time: pilots, doctors and specialist engineers take years to develop, so their plans must look further ahead than plans for sales or support roles.
- **Fit or choice?** Contingency theorists argue that the environment and technology decide the best structure. John Child replied in 1972 that managers make real strategic choices. Restructuring is also overused: a reorganisation disrupts work for months, and its promised benefits often fail to arrive.
- **Flatter is not always better.** Removing layers can speed decisions, but flat structures can hide informal hierarchies, and the evidence on self-managing models such as holacracy is mixed. Popular span rules of thumb have little research behind them.
- **Who carries the flexibility?** Atkinson's flexible firm explains how employers buy flexibility; critics point out that the risk lands on peripheral workers. In India the point is sharp, because contract workers often do similar work to permanent staff for less pay.

A good answer names the model, applies it to the case, states its limit, and shows how evidence from the case changes the recommendation.

## In India

- **Fixed-term employment (Industrial Relations Code).** A fixed-term employee works on a written contract for a set period and must get the same hours, wages, allowances and benefits as a comparable permanent worker, with statutory benefits in proportion to service. Gratuity is payable after <Fact id="gratuity.fixedTermMinYears" /> of service under the contract. Expiry or non-renewal is not retrenchment, but ending a contract early for non-disciplinary reasons may be. Use it for genuinely time-bound work: rolling renewals for permanent work invite challenge. See [contracts and fixed-term employment](/handbooks/labour-law/contracts-and-fixed-term-employment/).
- **Contract labour (OSH Code).** The rules apply from 50 contract workers, up from 20. Contract labour is barred from **core activities** unless the work is normally done through contractors in that industry, doesn't need full-time workers for most of the day, or is a sudden rise in core work that must be finished in a set time. Support services such as security, canteen, housekeeping and loading are not core. The principal employer must pay wages if the contractor doesn't.
- **Planning a reduction.** Retrenching a worker with a year's service needs notice of <Fact id="notice.retrenchmentNoticeMonths" /> (or wages in lieu), compensation of <Fact id="notice.retrenchmentCompensationDays" /> for each completed year, and a payment of <Fact id="notice.reskillingFundDays" /> into the worker re-skilling fund. Industrial establishments with <Fact id="notice.layoffPermissionWorkers" /> or more need government permission first. See [termination, grievances and disputes](/handbooks/labour-law/termination-grievance-and-disputes/).
- **State rules.** The Codes are in force nationwide, but state rules lag. Karnataka, Goa and Delhi rules were still in draft in early November 2026, so older state rules and forms continue where they are consistent with the Codes. A plan that covers Gurugram and Bengaluru must check the rules in both states.
