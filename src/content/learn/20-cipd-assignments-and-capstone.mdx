## Read the marking before you write

The Ahmedabad HR business partner's referral is better news than it looks. CIPD's marking tells her exactly what failed: two criteria, not the whole piece. Rewriting everything would put at risk the five criteria she has already met. To see why, start with how CIPD assessment works.

- **CIPD sets every assessment.** Each brief is built around a realistic, employer-style scenario, and the assessment criteria (ACs) come from the unit specification. Briefs are refreshed from time to time, so check you are working to the current version.
- **Your centre marks it; CIPD checks the marking.** Your study centre marks your work against each criterion and checks its own marking. CIPD then moderates a sample before releasing results.
- **Every criterion must be met.** Marking is criterion-referenced: each AC is judged on its own, and one unmet AC means the work is referred back to you. Level 5 is pass or fail. At Level 7 each assessment is graded Pass, Merit or Distinction, though the diploma itself is pass or fail.
- **Most units are written,** as reports, briefing papers, essays, case analyses or reflective statements; providers describe a typical assessment as {CRED.cipdWords.display}. Units with "demonstrate" or "be able to" outcomes also need practical evidence, such as a live training session or a record of your CPD.
- **Referral is not failure.** You get feedback and a window to resubmit, set by your centre.
- **AI has rules.** Centres apply malpractice policies to AI. CIPD's own policy for apprenticeship assessments allows AI for research, ideas and proofreading if you disclose it, and bans submitting AI-written answers as your own. Expect your centre to take the same line.

## Level 5 and Level 7: one topic, two standards

The same topic can appear at both levels. What changes is what you must do with it. Level 5 asks you to analyse, interpret and evaluate; Level 7 asks you to critically evaluate complex information and handle problems with many interacting factors. The verbs show it: in four sampled Level 7 units, {CRED.cipdCritically.display} criteria begin "critically", against none of 39 in four Level 5 units.

Here is the difference in two sentences on the same point:

- **Level 5:** "Kotter's eight steps would help the hospital plan its change, because they give managers a clear sequence (Kotter, 1996)."
- **Level 7:** "Kotter's sequence suits planned, top-down change, but this hospital's change depends on nurses volunteering effort on wards where managers have little control, so an emergent view of change explains the risks better; the plan should combine Kotter's early steps with continuous ward-level adjustment."

The second sentence judges the model's fit to the context, names its limit and arrives at a position. That is what "critical" means at Level 7: not negative, but weighed.

<Callout type="note" title="At strategic level (SHRM-SCP, CIPD Level 7)">
Merit and Distinction at Level 7 reward the depth of critical evaluation and the quality of recommendations. The detailed descriptors sit in each brief, so read them before you plan. In practice, the strongest scripts make a few well-evidenced judgements rather than many thin ones.
</Callout>

## Command verbs: what each one asks for

Every criterion starts with a verb, and the verb sets the task. A frequent reason for referral, especially at Level 7, is answering a different verb from the one in the criterion: usually describing or explaining when the criterion asks you to evaluate.

## Map every criterion before you write

The most reliable way to pass is to plan the whole assessment as a map before you write a sentence. It takes an hour and saves days.

1. **Read the brief twice.** The first time for the story; the second time underlining every fact about the organisation: its size, sector, strategy, problems and people.
2. **List every criterion.** For each one, circle the verb and underline the content words.
3. **Build a criterion map:** one row per criterion, with the heading you will use, the theories and evidence you will draw on, the case facts you will apply and a word budget.
4. **Write to the map,** one signposted section per criterion, or per group of criteria if the brief asks for a report format.
5. **Check each section against its verb** before you submit, and cut description that isn't doing work.

Inside each section, build paragraphs that argue rather than describe. A reliable pattern, close to Toulmin's model of argument, has five moves:

- **Point:** the claim this paragraph makes, in one sentence.
- **Evidence:** the theory or research behind it, referenced.
- **Application:** what it means for the organisation in the brief.
- **Limitation:** where the evidence is weak, or doesn't fit this case.
- **So what:** the judgement you draw, which feeds your conclusion.

<Worked title="Worked example: turning one criterion into a structured answer">

The situation's HR business partner has a referred criterion. In our words, it asks her to **critically evaluate best-practice and best-fit approaches to linking people practice with performance, for the organisation in the brief**: a 1,200-person pharmaceutical distributor that competes on reliable, compliant delivery, with depot staff on shifts and a sales force on incentives. *(This criterion is paraphrased for teaching, not quoted from a CIPD brief.)*

**Step 1: unpack the verb.** "Critically evaluate" means weighing both approaches and the quality of the evidence for each, then arguing a position for this organisation. Describing both approaches will not pass.

**Step 2: unpack the content.**

- *Best practice:* one bundle of high-commitment practices improves performance anywhere (Pfeffer, 1998).
- *Best fit:* practices should match the business's competitive strategy (Schuler and Jackson, 1987).
- *Evidence linking HR practices to performance:* for example, Huselid's (1995) study of US firms.

**Step 3: find the case facts.** Reliability and compliance drive margins; errors at depots are costly; the sales force works to incentives.

**Step 4: plan the paragraphs and words** (example: this criterion carries about a fifth of a 3,000-word assessment).

| Paragraph | Its job | Evidence | Words |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | State your position in one sentence | – | 40 |
| 2 | Best practice: the claim and its evidence, applied to depots | Pfeffer (1998); Huselid (1995) | 140 |
| 3 | The limits of that evidence | Huselid's design; Boxall and Purcell (2022) | 110 |
| 4 | Best fit: the claim, applied to depots and to sales | Schuler and Jackson (1987) | 140 |
| 5 | The limits of fit: strategies shift; people want more than fit | Boxall and Purcell (2022) | 100 |
| 6 | Your judgement for this distributor | The case facts | 110 |
| | | **Total** | **640** |

**Step 5: write each paragraph to the pattern.** Here is paragraph 3 written two ways.

*Descriptive (referred):* "Best practice means using the same HR practices everywhere. Pfeffer (1998) lists seven practices, such as employment security and selective hiring. Many organisations use them successfully."

*Critical (passes):* "Huselid (1995) found that firms using more high-performance work practices had lower turnover and better financial results, which appears to support a universal bundle **[point and evidence]**. For the distributor, this would justify investing in selective hiring and training for depot supervisors, where errors are costly **[application]**. However, its design shows association rather than cause, and it covered US firms, few of which resemble an Indian distributor whose margins depend on regulatory compliance **[limitation]**. The case for a single bundle is therefore weaker here than its advocates suggest: it is better used as principles to adapt than as a checklist to install **[so what]**."

The critical version is barely longer. It is better because every sentence does a job the verb asks for, and the last sentence makes a judgement about this organisation.

</Worked>

## Harvard referencing, done properly

CIPD expects Harvard referencing. It has two halves, and both must match: a short **citation** in your text, and a full **reference list** at the end, in alphabetical order of author.

- **In the text:** "(Huselid, 1995)" at the end of a sentence, or "Boxall and Purcell (2022) argue that …" within it. Add a page number when you quote directly. For three or more authors, use the first author and "et al."
- **In the reference list:** a full entry that lets a reader find the source.

| Type of source | Reference-list entry |
| --- | --- |
| Book | Boxall, P. and Purcell, J. (2022) *Strategy and Human Resource Management*. 5th edn. London: Bloomsbury. |
| Journal article | Huselid, M.A. (1995) 'The impact of human resource management practices on turnover, productivity, and corporate financial performance', *Academy of Management Journal*, 38(3), pp. 635–672. |
| Report or web page | Organisation (year) *Title of the report*. Available at: web address (Accessed: date you read it). |
| Internal document | Organisation name (year) *Title of the document*. Internal document, unpublished. |

Small habits prevent most referencing problems:

- **Cite as you write,** not at the end. A free reference manager such as Zotero keeps the two halves in step.
- **Check every citation has an entry,** and every entry is cited.
- **Prefer the original source.** If you must cite something you found in another book, say so ("Pfeffer, 1998, cited in …"), and use this sparingly.
- **Never let an AI tool supply references.** Such tools can produce convincing citations to sources that do not exist. Find and read every source yourself.
- **Follow your centre's Harvard guide** for punctuation and italics, and be consistent.

## The 7CO04 research project

7CO04 *Business research in people practice* is the Level 7 unit that asks you to do research, not just read it. You plan a project that adds value to an organisation, justify your methods, analyse the data you collect, and make costed recommendations. The criteria cover:

- aims, objectives and research questions;
- a critical review of the literature;
- your research design and methods, with their limitations;
- ethics and data handling;
- analysis of numbers and of words, and how you present them;
- findings, conclusions and recommendations, with an action plan, costs and benefits;
- a critical reflection on how you did the research.

Choose a question your organisation genuinely needs answered, and small enough to finish: "Why do nurses leave in their first year?", "Has our new-manager programme changed team engagement?", or "What would a four-day week cost our support centre?" Three things make a 7CO04 project stand out: a literature review that compares studies instead of summarising them one by one; methods justified for *this* question; and recommendations an HR director could take to a budget meeting.

## Your capstone: an HR strategy, as a board deck

The capstone pulls the whole course together, as XLRI's capstone project does in the second year of the PGDM (HRM). It is the piece of work you can show an employer.

**The brief.** Write an evidence-based HR strategy for a real organisation and present it as a board deck.

- **The organisation:** your own, with permission and with confidential details removed, or a listed Indian company you can study from public sources such as its annual report and BRSR disclosures.
- **The task:** diagnose the business context and its main people issues; choose three priorities that serve the business strategy; set out a costed, phased plan for 12 to 24 months; define how you will measure success; and name the risks, including legal duties under the Labour Codes and the DPDP Act.
- **What you produce:** 10–12 slides, presented in 10 minutes, followed by 10 minutes of questions; a supporting note of about 1,500 words with Harvard references; and an appendix with your data.
- **The evidence:** draw on all four sources CIPD's evidence-based practice uses: research, organisational data, professional expertise and the views of the people affected.

**A slide outline that works.** Lead with the answer, as the pyramid principle advises:

1. The answer: three priorities, total cost, expected effect, the decision needed
2. The business context: strategy, market, key numbers
3. The people diagnosis: what the data shows
4. to 6. One slide per priority: what, why, and the evidence
7. The costed, phased plan, with owners
8. The measures: baselines, targets, leading and lagging indicators
9. Risks, legal duties and how you will manage them
10. The decision you need today

**How it is marked.** This rubric is ours, modelled on how CIPD grades Level 7 assessments; it is not a CIPD document.

## Where experts disagree

- **Criteria or compliance?** Clear criteria make marking fairer and tell learners what is expected, which helps people new to UK-style assessment. But Torrance (2007) argued that very explicit objectives, criteria and feedback can make learning about meeting the criteria rather than understanding the subject. Use the criteria to plan; write for a thoughtful reader.
- **Writing about practice, or doing it?** A written assignment shows you can argue from evidence, not that you can handle a difficult meeting. CIPD adds practical evidence for some units; SHRM tests judgement through scenarios; XLRI makes you defend a case in class. Each sees a different slice of competence.
- **What counts as evidence?** Some academics give peer-reviewed research the most weight; practitioners often trust their own organisation's data and experience more. CIPD's approach weighs four sources, and their quality, rather than ranking one above the rest.
- **AI and authorship.** Is an AI-assisted draft a shortcut or a new professional skill? CIPD's 2026 CPD policy requires reflections to be the member's own even where AI tools helped, and centres treat undisclosed AI writing as malpractice.

A strong Level 7 answer on assessment states the purpose of the assessment first, then judges each method against it, with evidence on both sides.

## In India

- **No local centre.** CIPD's centre finder listed {CRED.cipdIndiaCentres.display} in India in November 2026. Indian learners study with approved centres licensed for online delivery; check any provider on [CIPD's centre finder](https://findacentre.cipd.org/) before paying. Costs and routes are in the [credentials guide](/learn/credentials/).
- **UK law in the briefs.** Where a criterion asks about law, answer with UK law, because that is what is assessed. Ask your centre whether you may draw on Indian organisations elsewhere; many learners' best evidence comes from their own workplace.
- **Indian evidence counts.** Annual reports and BRSR disclosures, government statistics such as the Periodic Labour Force Survey, and Indian research all strengthen an argument about an Indian organisation, alongside CIPD's own research.
- **Research data and the DPDP Act.** Colleagues' survey answers and HR records are personal data. With the Act's main duties applying from about 13 May 2027, build in permission, anonymisation, secure storage and deletion from the start.
- **Time zones.** UK evening tutorials fall around midnight in India, so plan your study weeks around recordings and deadlines in UK time.
