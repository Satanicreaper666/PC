  - id: utaut-model
    name: "Unified theory of acceptance and use of technology (UTAUT)"
    by: "Viswanath Venkatesh, Michael Morris, Gordon Davis and Fred Davis"
    year: "2003"
    idea: "Use depends on expected performance, expected effort, social influence and the support available, shaped by age, gender, experience and choice."
    use: "Designing change for a new HR system or AI tool: managers as role models, training, and help at hand."
    watch: "Many variables; it predicts intentions in surveys better than behaviour in messy, mandatory rollouts."
    cite: "Venkatesh, V., Morris, M. G., Davis, G. B. and Davis, F. D. (2003) User acceptance of information technology: toward a unified view. MIS Quarterly, 27(3)."

  - id: ht-four-fifths
    type: knowledge
    q: "In a pilot, an AI tool shortlists 40% of men and 28% of women who apply. What does the four-fifths rule suggest?"
    options:
      - "No concern, because both groups were shortlisted"
      - "Possible adverse impact: 28 ÷ 40 = 0.70, below 0.80, so investigate before any live use"
      - "The tool is unlawful and must be scrapped"
      - "It is a US test, so the result can be ignored in India"
    answer: 1
    feedback:
      - "Shortlisting some women doesn't show that the rates are fair."
      - "Correct. A ratio below 0.80 is a red flag that calls for investigation of what drives the gap."
      - "The rule flags a gap to investigate; on its own it doesn't prove the tool is unlawful."
      - "Indian law bars gender discrimination in recruitment, and the ratio is a sensible first screen anywhere."
    why: "Divide the lower selection rate by the higher one. Below 0.80, find the features driving the gap before the tool touches real decisions."

    type: written
    level: advanced
    q: "Recommend and justify how an Indian GCC should govern AI tools used in hiring before the EU AI Act's high-risk duties apply in December 2027. (About 200 words.)"
    why: "Model answer: I recommend a risk-tiered governance framework, built now rather than in late 2027. First, create an AI register listing every hiring tool, its purpose, owner, vendor, data and the decisions it shapes. Tools that filter, rank or evaluate candidates are high-risk under Annex III of the EU AI Act, and the Act reaches the GCC wherever outputs are used for EU roles. Second, test before use: run each tool in shadow mode and apply an adverse-impact test such as the four-fifths rule. ## Start with the stack, not the tool

The Bengaluru centre already runs a core HR suite, an applicant tracking system (ATS), a learning platform and a dozen spreadsheets that fill the gaps. The vendor's screening tool would sit on top of the ATS. Whatever it promises, it can only be as good as the data and process beneath it.

An HR technology stack has layers, and data flows up through them.

Three choices shape every stack:

- **Suite or best-of-breed.** A single suite integrates more easily; specialist tools may do one job better but need integration work. AI features are pulling buyers back towards suites, with more risk of lock-in.
- **One record, one architecture.** Decide who owns each data field, and keep one job and skills architecture, or every tool will disagree.
- **Indian localisation.** Statutory payroll (PF, ESI, professional tax, labour welfare fund, TDS), Labour Code registers, multi-state rules, and mobile, local-language access for frontline staff are where imported systems most often fall short.

Buying is the easy part; use is the hard part. The **technology acceptance model** (Davis, 1989) says people adopt a system they find useful and easy, and **UTAUT** (Venkatesh and colleagues, 2003) adds social influence and support. That is why managers who visibly use a tool matter so much. For the business case, Lepak and Snell's three types help: *operational* gains (cheaper transactions), *relational* gains (better service) and *transformational* gains (better decisions). Expect the first and prove the third. The [digital agility chapter](/handbooks/hr-professional/digital-agility-and-ai/) has a buyer's checklist. Before you buy an analytics layer, see what your existing exports already tell you: the [people analytics tool](/toolkit/people-analytics/) works out headcount, attrition and tenure from a spreadsheet in your browser.

## Where AI earns its place, and where it needs a human

The centre's recruiters already use generative AI to draft job adverts and summarise interview notes. The vendor's tool is different in kind: it ranks and scores people. The line between helping a person write and shaping a decision about a person is the line to manage.

Two waves are arriving together. *Generative AI* drafts, summarises and answers questions. *Agentic AI* plans and carries out multi-step tasks across systems, with a person signing off at set points. The evidence on value is real but uneven:

- SHRM's *State of AI in HR 2026* found 39% of organisations using AI in HR, most often in recruiting. Most users reported efficiency gains, but about half saw no better decisions, and just under half had a formal AI policy.
- In a large customer-support operation, an AI assistant raised issues resolved per hour by about 14%, with the biggest gains for new agents (Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond). That speeds up onboarding, but if juniors skip the formative tasks, the pipeline of future experts can thin out.
- Microsoft's 2026 Work Trend Index attributes about two-thirds of AI's measured impact to organisational factors, such as culture, managers and work design, rather than to individual users.

## The rules are arriving on fixed dates

The vendor's tool would screen candidates for jobs in India and in the bank's EU offices. That brings several regimes into play at once, each with its own date.

What each regime asks of an employer:

- **EU AI Act.** Since February 2025 it has banned emotion recognition at work, except for medical or safety reasons. From 2 December 2027, AI used to recruit, select, promote, dismiss, allocate tasks or monitor workers is high-risk. Employers must follow the provider's instructions, assign trained human oversight, monitor the system, keep logs for at least six months, and inform workers' representatives and affected workers. The Act reaches organisations outside the EU when a system's output is used in the EU, so an Indian centre screening for EU roles is in scope. Fines reach €35 million or 7% of turnover for banned practices.
- **New York City Local Law 144.** Since July 2023, automated tools used to hire or promote for New York City jobs need an annual independent bias audit, a public summary and notice to candidates. A December 2025 state audit found enforcement weak, and the city agreed to tighten it.
- **Colorado.** The state's 2024 AI Act was delayed, stayed in court and then replaced by a narrower law from 1 January 2027. It requires notice, an explanation of adverse decisions within 30 days, a chance to correct data, and human review on request.
- **India.** There is no AI Act. MeitY's AI Governance Guidelines (November 2025) set principles only. The binding rules for HR are the DPDP Act and Rules, whose core duties start on 13 May 2027, and existing anti-discrimination law.

<Callout type="note" title="At strategic level (SHRM-SCP, CIPD Level 7)">
Design to the strictest regime you are exposed to. For most Indian GCCs that means EU rules for any work whose output is used in the EU, the DPDP Act for every Indian candidate and employee, and US state rules where the parent hires. One governance framework built to that standard costs less than three retrofits.
</Callout>

## Govern the risks before you buy

Most AI failures in HR are governance failures. Nobody tested the tool on the company's own data, nobody owned it, and nobody could explain a decision when a candidate asked.

| Risk | How it shows up in HR | Typical controls |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bias | Rankings that penalise career gaps, colleges or PIN codes | Adverse-impact tests, independent audit, human review, an alternative route |
| Privacy | Collecting too much; reusing HR data to train models | Purpose limits, retention schedule, processor contracts |
| Explainability | Rejected candidates can't find out why | Reason codes, an appeal route, decision records |
| Surveillance | Keystroke scores, webcam checks, emotion analytics | Proportionality test, consultation, transparency |
| Vendor opacity | A model changes without notice, or can't be audited | Audit rights, logs, change notice, re-testing |

The bias risk is not hypothetical. Across more than 3 million comparisons, three large language models preferred white-associated names 85% of the time (University of Washington, 2024). In *Mobley v. Workday*, a US court in May 2025 let a nationwide age-discrimination claim proceed against an HR-tech vendor, not only against employers.

<Worked title="Worked example: the business case and risk assessment for the screening tool">

All figures are an illustrative example.

**Step 1: the baseline.** The centre screens 48,000 applications a year (40,000 for India roles, 8,000 for EU roles). Recruiters spend about 4 minutes per CV, so 48,000 × 4 ÷ 60 = **3,200 hours**. At a loaded cost of ₹900 an hour, first screening costs **₹28.8 lakh** a year. Time-to-hire averages 50 days, of which first screening takes 7.

**Step 2: the costs.**

| Cost | Year 1 | Later years |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Licence | ₹40 lakh | ₹40 lakh |
| Integration and set-up | ₹12 lakh | – |
| Bias audit and legal review | ₹10 lakh | ₹6 lakh |
| Human review, 1 minute per CV | ₹7.2 lakh | ₹7.2 lakh |
| **Total** | **₹69.2 lakh** | **₹53.2 lakh** |

**Step 3: the benefits.** The hard saving is the 3,200 screening hours, **₹28.8 lakh**: less than the cost in every year. The case therefore rests on speed. The tool can only shorten the screening stage, from 7 days to about 2. That is 5 days faster, not the 25 days the vendor's "halve time-to-hire" implies. If finance agrees that an empty engineering seat costs ₹2,500 a day, then 600 hires × 5 days × ₹2,500 = **₹75 lakh**. Total benefit ₹103.8 lakh against ₹53.2 lakh of running cost, but only if that vacancy cost holds.

**Step 4: the risks.**

| Risk | Rating | Control before go-live |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bias against women or older candidates | High | Shadow pilot with adverse-impact test; yearly audit |
| Video scoring of "enthusiasm" from faces | High | Switch it off in the contract: the science is contested, and the EU bans emotion recognition in the workplace |
| EU high-risk duties for EU roles, 2 Dec 2027 | Certain | Oversight, logs for 6 months or more, candidate notice |
| DPDP duties for Indian candidates, May 2027 | Certain | Purpose limits, retention schedule, processor contract |
| No explanation for rejections | High | Reason codes and human review on request |

**Step 5: the pilot result.** In an 8-week shadow pilot on 5,000 applications, the tool shortlists 40% of men and 28% of women. The impact ratio is 28 ÷ 40 = **0.70**, below the 0.80 four-fifths threshold. The vendor traces it to a penalty on career gaps, which falls hardest on women returning after maternity leave.

**Step 6: the decision.** Don't sign this month. Sign only with audit results, an explanation of the main factors, emotion scoring removed, logs, change notice and DPDP-ready data terms. Go live only for roles where the re-tested ratio clears 0.80 for every group you can lawfully measure, with recruiter review of every rejection the tool recommends. A named owner reports the ratio each quarter.

## Skills, hybrid and gig work: what the evidence says

Vendors and conference speakers sell all three trends. The evidence is more modest, and more useful.

### Skills-based organisations

A skills *taxonomy* lists skills; an *ontology* adds how they relate and which roles need them. The WEF expects 39% of core skills to change by 2030. But the 2024 Harvard and Burning Glass study found that about 45% of US firms that dropped degree requirements went on hiring as before. The firms that really changed practice raised non-degree hiring, and those hires had 10-point higher retention than graduates. LinkedIn estimates that hiring on skills would widen India's talent pool 11.3 times for non-AI jobs and 22.1 times for AI jobs. Evidence on internal talent marketplaces comes mostly from vendor case studies. Start with a few high-volume or scarce roles, validate the assessments, and track quality of hire.

### Hybrid work

In a randomised trial at Trip.com, published in *Nature* in 2024, two home days a week cut quits by about a third, with no change in performance reviews or promotions over two years. Women, non-managers and long-distance commuters gained most, which matters in Bengaluru traffic. Office mandates are nonetheless tightening, in India and abroad. Build hybrid policy around team anchor days and output measures, and watch what mandates do to women's retention and to attrition by commute distance.

### Gig and platform work

The Code on Social Security recognises gig and platform workers for the first time. It sets aggregators' contribution to a social security fund at 1–2% of annual turnover, capped at 5% of what they pay these workers, with the exact rate still to be notified. Workers register on e-Shram so benefits follow them across platforms. Karnataka's 2025 Act adds a welfare fee, 14 days' notice with reasons before termination, and a ban on algorithms that discriminate. For HR, the issues are misclassification, overlapping levies, and algorithmic management: software that allocates, rates and deactivates people now falls under transparency and grievance duties.

## Where experts disagree

- **Augment or automate?** SHRM's "AI + HI" stance says AI should support human judgement. Task-based research (Autor and colleagues) shows technology reshapes jobs task by task, and Acemoglu and Restrepo (2019) argue that whether it displaces or empowers workers is a design choice.
- **Does AI pay?** Task-level studies show real gains. A widely cited MIT report claimed about 95% of enterprise generative-AI pilots had no measurable effect on profit; its method is disputed, but Deloitte finds technology-first approaches more likely to miss returns than human-centred ones.
- **Skills hype.** Vendors sell skills inference and talent marketplaces on case studies. Independent, peer-reviewed evidence on their accuracy is thin, and inferred skills are probabilities, not facts.
- **Hybrid versus mandates.** The trial evidence favours hybrid. Critics reply that one firm's engineers may not represent all work, and that mentoring and innovation effects are harder to measure.
- **Does technology hollow out HR?** One view says HR becomes a ticket queue; the other says automation frees HR for advice and design. Marler and Fisher (2013) found mostly efficiency gains so far.

A good answer weighs task-level evidence against organisation-level results, names who bears the risk, and proposes a measurable pilot instead of a verdict.

## In India

- **Data protection.** The DPDP Act's core duties start on 13 May 2027 (some firms count from 12 May). Section 7 lets employers process personal data for employment purposes without consent, but security safeguards, breach reporting, erasure and grievance handling still apply. Optional uses, such as wellness apps or training AI on employee data, are safer with consent. Penalties reach ₹250 crore for security failures. The [digital agility chapter](/handbooks/hr-professional/digital-agility-and-ai/) has a preparation checklist.
- **No AI Act.** MeitY's guidelines set seven principles and nothing specific to HR. Existing law still governs outcomes: the Code on Wages bars gender discrimination in recruitment for the same or similar work, the Transgender Persons Act bars discrimination in employment, and the RPwD Act requires every establishment to have an equal opportunity policy.
- **Gig workers.** The Code on Social Security's aggregator levy rate has not yet been notified. Karnataka's Act is in force and its welfare fee of 1% of payouts is notified; platforms' court challenge was pending in late 2026, and Telangana has a draft bill.
- **Always-on work.** The Right to Disconnect Bill 2025 is a private member's bill and has not passed. Long hours remain a matter of the Labour Codes, state Shops and Establishments Acts and company policy.
- **GCCs.** Indian centres that build or run HR AI for EU entities face the EU AI Act through where outputs are used, plus EU pay-transparency rules for reward work (see [module 18](/learn/global-hr-and-esg/)).
