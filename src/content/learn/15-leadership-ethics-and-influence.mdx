    name: "Contingency model of leadership"
    by: "Fred Fiedler"
    year: "1967"
    idea: "A leader's style, task- or relationship-motivated, is fairly fixed. Effectiveness depends on how favourable the situation is: relations, task structure and position power."
    use: "Deciding which leader suits a situation, or how to change the situation to suit the leader."
    watch: "The LPC scale is hard to interpret, and support is mixed for situations in the middle range."
    cite: "Fiedler, F. E. (1967) A Theory of Leadership Effectiveness. McGraw-Hill."
  - id: situational-leadership-model
    name: "Situational Leadership"
    by: "Paul Hersey and Ken Blanchard"
    year: "1969"
    idea: "Leaders should move between telling, selling, participating and delegating as a follower's ability and willingness for a task grow."
    use: "Coaching new managers to match direction and support to each person and task."
    watch: "Very popular in training but weakly supported by research (Thompson and Vecchio, 2009)."
    cite: "Hersey, P. and Blanchard, K. H. (1969) Life cycle theory of leadership. Training and Development Journal, 23(5)."
  - id: transformational-leadership
    name: "Transformational and transactional leadership"
    by: "James MacGregor Burns; Bernard Bass"
    year: "1978; 1985"
    idea: "Transactional leaders exchange rewards for performance. Transformational leaders also inspire, challenge thinking, attend to individuals and act as role models."
    use: "Designing leadership development and 360-degree feedback for leaders who must lead change."
    watch: "Definitions mix the style with its effects, and the measures are heavily criticised (van Knippenberg and Sitkin, 2013)."
    cite: "Bass, B. M. (1985) Leadership and Performance Beyond Expectations. Free Press."
  - id: lmx-theory
    name: "Leader–member exchange (LMX)"
    by: "Fred Dansereau, George Graen and William Haga; later Graen and Mary Uhl-Bien"
    year: "1975; 1995"
    idea: "Leaders form higher-quality relationships with some team members than others; those members get more trust, support and opportunity."
    use: "Explaining perceived favouritism and uneven access to good work, and helping managers widen strong relationships."
    watch: "Cause and effect are hard to separate: strong performers may earn good relationships rather than gain from them."
    cite: "Graen, G. B. and Uhl-Bien, M. (1995) Relationship-based approach to leadership. Leadership Quarterly, 6(2)."

    year: "1980"
    idea: "In Indian organisations, effective leaders pair a firm focus on the task with personal care and guidance that depends on the follower doing the work."
    use: "Adapting manager expectations and leadership development to hierarchical, relationship-oriented Indian workplaces."
    watch: "Can slide into paternalism and dependence if the leader never hands over control."
    cite: "Sinha, J. B. P. (1980) The Nurturant Task Leader. Concept Publishing."

## Influence without authority

The HR head in Gurugram can't overrule the CEO or the business head. If the flight-risk tool is to be stopped or reshaped, she has to persuade people who outrank her. That is the normal condition of HR work: most of its influence comes from expertise, relationships and trust, not from position.

French and Raven (1959) identified five bases of power, and Raven later added a sixth. Some come with the position; others come from the person.

HR has little positional power over line leaders, so expert, informational and referent power do most of the work. They have to be built in advance: you can't become the trusted expert in the week you need to disagree with the CEO.

How you use power matters as much as which power you have. Yukl and colleagues studied the tactics people use to influence bosses, peers and teams. Four tend to win commitment:

- **Rational persuasion:** evidence and logic.
- **Consultation:** asking for the other person's input into the plan.
- **Inspirational appeals:** linking the request to values the person holds.
- **Collaboration:** offering help to make the change work.

Pressure and legitimating tactics ("policy says so") are the least likely to win commitment. For the flight-risk tool, the strongest opening is rational persuasion plus consultation: accept that retention is a real problem, bring data on why engineers actually leave, and ask the business head to help design a better answer. The [communication and influence chapter](/handbooks/hr-professional/communication-and-influence/) sets out a step-by-step approach for sceptical managers.

## From traits to relationships: how leadership theory developed

Leadership theory has moved in waves. Each wave added a question rather than replacing the last.

<Figure title="How leadership theory developed" caption="Read the waves in order. Each asked a new question; none of the earlier ones disappeared, and all still appear in SHRM and CIPD material." wide>
  <Timeline
    items={[
      { when: "To the 1940s", label: "Traits", note: "who leads?" },
      { when: "1950s–60s", label: "Behaviours", note: "what do leaders do?" },
      { when: "1960s–70s", label: "Contingency", note: "when does a style work?" },
      { when: "1970s–80s", label: "Transformational", note: "how do leaders change what followers want?", accent: true },
      { when: "1970s onwards", label: "Servant, LMX, authentic", note: "service, relationships, integrity" }
    ]}
  />
</Figure>

- **Traits.** Meta-analyses (Judge and colleagues, 2002) find that traits such as extraversion and conscientiousness predict who emerges as a leader, but only modestly.
- **Behaviours.** The Ohio State and Michigan studies found two families of behaviour, structuring the task and showing consideration for people. Both matter.
- **Contingency.** Fiedler (1967) argued that a leader's style is fairly fixed, so match the leader to the situation or change the situation. Hersey and Blanchard (1969) argued the opposite: flex your style, from telling to delegating, as a follower becomes more ready for the task. Their model is popular in training but weakly supported by research.
- **Transformational leadership** (Burns; Bass) asked how leaders lift what followers want for themselves: by inspiring, challenging thinking, attending to individuals and setting an example.
- **Servant** leadership (Greenleaf) and **authentic** leadership (George; Avolio and Gardner) put the leader's purpose and integrity at the centre. **LMX** (Graen and colleagues) looked at relationships: leaders build closer ties with some team members, who then get more trust and better work.

Indian research adds a useful correction. J. B. P. Sinha's **nurturant-task leader** (1980) combines a firm focus on the task with personal care that depends on the follower doing the work. It fits hierarchical, relationship-oriented workplaces better than some imported models.

For HR the practical uses are clear. Use contingency thinking when you coach managers. Watch LMX patterns for hidden favouritism, which is also an inclusion issue. Be sceptical of any programme that promises to make every manager "transformational" in two days.

## The consulting cycle: agree the question before the answer

SHRM's Consultation competency describes a consulting cycle of discovery, analysis, recommendation and implementation. Peter Block's version for internal consultants puts one step before all of them: contracting.

<Figure title="The consulting cycle for internal HR" caption="Follow the arrows from contracting to evaluation. Most failures start at contracting, when the requested solution is accepted without agreeing the real problem." wide>

    ]}
    outcomes={[
      { title: "Owned solution", detail: "the client acts and sustains it", tone: "good" },
      { title: "Shelf report", detail: "advice nobody acts on", tone: "bad" }

  />

Contracting is where most HR advice goes wrong. A manager asks for a solution: "run a team-building offsite", "buy a flight-risk tool". The consultant's first job is to turn the requested solution back into a problem. What have you seen? What outcome do you want? What is in scope, who else must be involved, and what will each of us do? Edgar Schein called the alternative the "purchase of expertise" model: you supply what the client ordered, even when it treats the wrong problem.

Two more habits make the cycle work. Feed findings back in plain language before recommending anything, and let the client own the decision. Then agree in advance how you will both judge whether it worked. The [HR business partnering chapter](/handbooks/hr-professional/hr-business-partnering/) shows how to run this rhythm with a business unit.

## Ethical frameworks for hard calls

Most HR dilemmas are not choices between right and wrong. They are choices between competing goods: retention and privacy, speed and fairness, the business and one person. Four classic lenses help, and each asks a different question.

<Figure title="Four ethical lenses for HR decisions" caption="Read each row: the question the lens asks, how it applies to a plan to monitor staff, and what it can miss. Use two lenses that pull in different directions." wide>
  <Compare
    columns={["The question it asks", "Applied to monitoring staff", "Blind spot"]}
    rows={[
      { label: "Utilitarian (Bentham, Mill)", values: ["Which option does the most good for everyone affected?", "Weigh retention gains against stress, distrust and wrong flags", "Can justify harming a few for the many"] },
      { label: "Duty (Kant)", values: ["What do I owe people, whatever the outcome?", "Be honest about monitoring; don't treat people merely as means", "Rigid when two duties conflict"] },
      { label: "Virtue (Aristotle)", values: ["What would a person of good character do?", "Would a fair, honest HR leader be proud of this?", "Vague when good people disagree"] },
      { label: "Justice (Rawls)", values: ["Would I accept this rule without knowing my place in it?", "Would you accept it as the employee being scored?", "Says little about cost or efficiency"] }
    ]}
  />
</Figure>

Pair a consequences lens with a duty or justice lens. If both point the same way, act with confidence. If they conflict, look for an option that keeps most of the benefit while respecting the duty.

<Worked title="Worked example: the flight-risk tool through two lenses">

**The proposal:** score all 900 employees for flight risk from badge swipes, laptop activity and calendar data, and hold back increments for anyone flagged.

**Lens 1: consequences (utilitarian).** List everyone affected and what they stand to gain or lose. These are judgements from conversations and exit data, not measurements.

| Who is affected | Likely benefit | Likely harm |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The company | Some early warning of exits | Engineers hear about the tool and leave sooner; legal and reputational risk |
| Employees flagged correctly | None: their increment is cut | Pushed out faster, on less pay |
| Employees flagged wrongly | None | A pay cut for a prediction, not for anything they did |
| All employees | None | Feeling watched; less candour with managers |
| Managers | Data to start conversations | Less trust in one-to-ones |

Holding back increments turns a prediction into a penalty, and it is most likely to drive out the very people it is meant to keep. On the consequences test, the proposal fails as framed.

**Lens 2: duty and justice.** Two duties hold whatever the outcome: be honest with people about how they are monitored, and judge pay on what people have done, not on a guess about what they might do. Rawls' test is sharper still. Would the CEO accept this rule if he didn't know whether he would be the one scored? Penalising a forecast fails both.

**Where the lenses agree:** no individual pay decisions from the scores, and no covert monitoring.

**Where they leave room:** the business problem is real, so redesign rather than refuse. This plan passes both lenses:

1. Study attrition drivers at team level from exit interviews, engagement data and pay benchmarks, using anonymised data.
2. Offer stay interviews to everyone in the teams losing most people.
3. Fix the top two causes, such as below-market pay for senior engineers.
4. Send any future monitoring through a privacy and legal review, with clear notice to employees.

**The decision, and how to argue it.** The HR head backs the goal, opposes the pay penalty and offers the redesigned plan with a 90-day target. She uses rational persuasion (the table), consultation (asking the business head to co-own the study) and an appeal to the company's own stated values. She also records her advice in writing.

</Worked>

## Two professional standards: the CIPD Code and SHRM's Ethical Practice

Both professional bodies turn ethics into expectations you can be held to. CIPD's Code of Conduct and Ethics has applied to every member since 1 January 2023, and it says members must comply with the law as a priority. SHRM's 2026 BASK makes Ethical Practice one of eight behavioural competencies, tested through judgement questions in the SHRM-CP and SHRM-SCP exams.

| CIPD Code principle (2023) | What it asks of you | Closest SHRM Ethical Practice expectation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Positive and active impact on working lives | Fair, inclusive treatment; no bullying or discrimination; develop people; speak up about concerns | Ethical agent: let people report without fear of reprisal; reduce bias in decisions |
| Civic virtue and stewardship | Consider the wider community; meet legal duties; respect privacy; serve the employer unless ethics or law say otherwise | Professional integrity: protect privacy; balance business needs, law and ethics |
| Good character | Honesty and integrity; no personal gain from your role; objectivity; declare conflicts of interest | Personal integrity: live the values you state; raise conflicts of interest |
| Professional service and competence | Keep skills current; evidence-based work; refer what is beyond your expertise; protect data | Professional integrity: prompt, impartial investigations; explain the limits of confidentiality |
| Personal responsibility | Own your actions; report suspected breaches; resolve complaints; behave professionally, including online | Personal integrity: own your mistakes; hold others to their commitments |

The two agree on far more than they differ. The main difference is how they are used. CIPD's Code is a condition of membership, and alleged breaches go to a conduct panel. SHRM's Ethical Practice is a competency standard that the exams test through scenarios.

For the HR head in Gurugram both point the same way: speak up, protect employees' privacy, base decisions on evidence and record the advice. For everyday grey-zone cases, the [ethics and professional courage chapter](/handbooks/hr-professional/ethics-and-professional-courage/) has a five-question test and four worked dilemmas.

## Where experts disagree

Expect these debates in an XLRI case discussion or a CIPD Level 7 assignment.

- **Does leadership matter as much as we think?** Meindl and colleagues (1985) described a "romance of leadership": people credit leaders with outcomes that other factors drove. Pfeffer (2015) argued that the leadership-development industry promises far more than the evidence supports.
- **Are the newer theories really new?** Meta-analyses find authentic and servant leadership overlapping heavily with transformational leadership (Banks and colleagues, 2016; Hoch and colleagues, 2018), and transformational measures have been criticised for mixing the style with its effects.
- **Can HR be both guardian and agent?** Winstanley and Woodall (2000) argued that HR's ethical position is inherently ambiguous, because it advocates for employees while acting for management. The employee-champion role is usually the first to be squeezed.
- **Do Western models travel?** The GLOBE studies and Sinha's work suggest leadership ideals vary by culture, so models built in the US may need adapting for hierarchical, relationship-oriented workplaces.

A good answer names the model, applies it to the case, states its limit, and brings in a second perspective to cover the gap.

## In India

- **Speak-up channels are a legal duty for many companies.** Listed companies and certain others, such as those that accept public deposits, must have a vigil mechanism for directors and employees to report genuine concerns, with safeguards against victimisation (Companies Act 2013, s.177). Listed companies have a parallel duty under SEBI's listing regulations. HR often runs or supports the channel.
- **Monitoring meets data protection.** The DPDP Rules were notified in November 2025 with an 18-month phase-in, so most duties apply from May 2027. Plans like the flight-risk tool need a privacy and legal review now. See [digital agility and AI](/handbooks/hr-professional/digital-agility-and-ai/) and the glossary entry on the [DPDP Act](/glossary/#dpdp-act).
- **Confidentiality has legal force in harassment cases.** The POSH Act requires the identities of the people involved and the details of an inquiry to be kept confidential. See [POSH compliance](/handbooks/labour-law/posh-compliance/).
- **Hierarchy raises the price of courage.** Where contradicting a senior leader in public is costly, raise concerns privately first, put your advice in writing, and know your route to the audit committee or board. Sinha's nurturant-task leader is a useful bridge here: firm on the task, caring towards people.
