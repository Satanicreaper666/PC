## Look beneath the dispute first

The plant's two problems look separate: a discipline case and a wage negotiation. They aren't. The union reads the suspension as a bargaining move, management reads the charter as unaffordable, and each side will judge the other's next step in the light of both. Map the system before you choose a move.

**Dunlop's industrial relations system** (1958) is the map. Three actors (workers and their unions, managers, and government agencies) act within three contexts: technology, the market or budget, and the balance of power in society. Shared ideas hold the system together, and its output is a *web of rules*: substantive rules such as wage rates, and procedural rules for disputes, grievances and discipline.

Dunlop's picture is static. **Kochan, Katz and McKersie** (1986) added *strategic choice*: management reshapes the system through decisions at three levels, from business strategy (where to invest, what to outsource), through bargaining and HR policy, to the workplace (how supervisors lead and discipline). After 1991 many Indian employers chose plant-level bargaining, more contract and fixed-term staff, and union-free sites, and unions responded. The lesson for HR: a supervisor's suspension order is a workplace choice that can derail a strategy-level negotiation, so coordinate across the levels.

## How you see conflict decides what you do

Ask three managers about the scuffle and you may hear three stories: an agitator attacked a supervisor and must go; the union and management want different things, so both issues need a fair process; or the suspension shows who really holds power on the shop floor. Alan Fox (1966; 1974) called these **frames of reference**:

- **Unitarist:** one team with shared goals. Conflict is a communication failure or the work of troublemakers, and unions are unnecessary.
- **Pluralist:** employees and managers share some interests and differ on others, such as pay and security. Conflict is normal and is handled through bargaining and agreed rules.
- **Radical:** conflict comes from the unequal power of capital and labour. Procedures manage it but never remove it.

Frames predict behaviour: a unitarist plant head reaches for discipline and communication campaigns, a pluralist for negotiation and procedures. India's IR Code rests on pluralist assumptions, with its negotiating union, conciliation and tribunals. CIPD Level 7 asks you to compare the frames critically (later writers add a market-driven 'egoist' frame), and in SHRM situational questions the best answer is rarely anti-union.

<Callout type="note" title="At strategic level (SHRM-SCP, CIPD Level 7)">
Make the organisation's frame explicit and choose an employee-relations strategy on purpose: union avoidance, union substitution, paternalism, partnership or engagement with independent unions. Each has costs. Avoidance can fail suddenly when a union forms, and partnership needs trust on both sides.
</Callout>

## Unions, recognition and voice in India

Indian unions are older than the Republic. The Madras Labour Union, usually called the first organised trade union, dates from 1918, and the Trade Unions Act 1926 gave unions registration and legal immunities; the IR Code has now replaced that Act. Most large unions belong to central trade union organisations, several linked to political parties, such as INTUC (Congress) and CITU (CPI(M)). Ten central unions called a nationwide strike against the Labour Codes on 12 February 2026. Rivalry between affiliated unions, outside leaders and small, thinly funded unions all shape bargaining here.

**Recognition is now a statutory rule.** Before the Codes only some states, such as Maharashtra, had recognition laws. Under the IR Code, a union backed by 51% or more of the workers on the muster roll is the sole negotiating union. If none reaches 51%, unions with at least 20% each form a negotiating council. How support is verified depends on the rules, which many states were still finalising in 2026.

Unions are one channel of **employee voice**. Others are direct (town halls, pulse surveys, skip-level meetings, speak-up lines) or representative without a union (works and grievance committees). Hirschman (1970) explains why voice matters: unhappy people speak up or leave, and where voice is safe, fewer leave. Freeman and Medoff (1984) found two faces in unions: a monopoly face that raises wages and can restrict work practices, and a voice face that cuts quits and improves procedures. In non-union firms voice must be designed, because silence is the default where people fear their manager's reaction (Morrison and Milliken, 2000).

## Collective bargaining and negotiation

Most private-sector bargaining in India happens at plant or company level, with settlements of three to five years; banking and some other public-sector industries bargain nationally. A **settlement** reached in conciliation binds every worker in the establishment, including future joiners, while one signed outside conciliation binds only its parties. The usual sequence is a charter of demands, a costed counter-proposal, rounds of talks while each side builds a mandate at home, then a memorandum of settlement, often with a peace clause.

**Walton and McKersie** (1965) showed that every negotiation runs four processes at once: *distributive* (dividing a fixed amount), *integrative* (creating value together), *attitudinal structuring* (shaping trust for the next round) and *intra-organisational* (aligning your own side). **Fisher and Ury** (1981) added a method: separate the people from the problem, focus on interests not positions, invent options for mutual gain and use objective criteria. Above all, know your **BATNA**, your best alternative to a negotiated agreement. It sets your **reservation point**, the worst deal you will accept. The gap between the two sides' reservation points is the **zone of possible agreement (ZOPA)**.

The IR Code shapes both BATNAs. A lawful strike needs notice and is barred during conciliation, so nobody walks out overnight, and workers are paid for a strike period only if it was both legal and justified (*Syndicate Bank v. K. Umesh Nayak*, 1994).

## Conflict and grievances: choose the style, use the channel

Most conflict at work is between people, not between a union and management. **Thomas and Kilmann** (1974), building on Blake and Mouton, describe five ways to handle it, depending on how far you pursue your own concerns (assertiveness) and how far you meet the other person's (cooperativeness).

Every mode fits somewhere; habit is the problem. Managers who always compete create grievances, and managers who always avoid let them grow.

A **grievance** is any sense of injustice an employee feels about their employment. A good procedure is a ladder with time limits, from supervisor to head of department, committee and top management, with voluntary arbitration last. The 1958 Code of Discipline model, still widely taught, allows 48 hours at the first step.

The IR Code makes part of this compulsory. Every industrial establishment with 20 or more workers needs a **grievance redressal committee** of up to 10 members, half from each side, with women represented at least in proportion to the workforce. It should decide within 30 days, after which the worker can go to conciliation. Other channels sit beside it, such as the POSH Internal Committee and the complaint officer under the Transgender Persons Act. Design the whole system as Ury, Brett and Goldberg (1988) suggest: settle most disputes on **interests**, fewer on **rights** and the fewest on **power**, with routes back to negotiation.

## Discipline and the domestic enquiry

The suspended office-bearer is where discipline and industrial relations collide. Rush the case and the union will call it victimisation, and a tribunal may agree; ignore it and supervisors will conclude that office-bearers are untouchable.

**Start with the rules.** Industrial establishments with 300 or more workers must have **standing orders** covering classes of workers, shifts, attendance, leave, misconduct and penalties. Adopting the Model Standing Orders 2026 (notified on 8 May 2026) counts as certification, and existing certified orders continue where they fit the Code. Smaller employers should still have written service rules. Don't assume Karnataka's old exemption for IT and ITES firms carries over: it is reported to be at risk under the Code.

**Then apply natural justice:** hear the other side (*audi alteram partem*), let no one judge their own cause (*nemo judex in causa sua*), and decide with reasons. The standard of proof is the preponderance of probabilities, but findings must rest on evidence.

Four Supreme Court rulings show why the steps matter:

- *ECIL v. B. Karunakar* (1993): give the worker the enquiry report before deciding the penalty.
- *Firestone Tyre* (1973): a tribunal can re-weigh the evidence and substitute a lighter penalty. After a defective enquiry the employer can prove misconduct afresh, but only if it asks to in its pleadings (*Shankar Chakravarti*, 1979).
- *Bharat Forge* (2005): courts rarely interfere with a penalty unless it is shockingly disproportionate.
- *Jaipur Zila Sahakari Bhoomi Vikas Bank* (2002): dismissing a worker while a dispute is pending, without applying for approval, is void.

That last rule bites here. The IR Code keeps the old section 33 protection: while conciliation or adjudication is pending, dismissal needs the authority's permission, or a month's wages and an application for approval, depending on whether the misconduct is connected with the dispute. A union office-bearer recognised under the rules as a 'protected worker' needs the authority's express permission either way. A strike notice puts the wage talks into conciliation, so take legal advice before deciding the office-bearer's case.

## Strikes and lockouts under the IR Code

The IR Code has changed how the union's strongest card can be played:

- **Notice everywhere.** Strike and lockout notice, once needed only in public utility services, now applies in every industrial establishment. It must be given within 60 days before the strike, and no strike may start within 14 days of it.
- **No strike during formal proceedings:** while conciliation is pending and for 7 days after, while tribunal or arbitration proceedings are pending and for 60 days after, or on matters covered by a settlement or award in force.
- **Mass casual leave counts:** casual leave taken together on one day by 50% or more of the workers is a strike.
- **Lockouts** follow the same rules.

Illegal strikes and lockouts can bring fines and short prison terms, and the Supreme Court has held there is no fundamental right to strike (*T.K. Rangarajan*, 2003). When a notice lands, treat it as the start of conciliation, keep service conditions unchanged, plan lawful contingencies without victimising anyone, and record every meeting and offer. The detailed rules are in [termination, grievances and disputes](/handbooks/labour-law/termination-grievance-and-disputes/).

## Where experts disagree

Expect these debates in an XLRI case discussion or a CIPD Level 7 assignment.

- **Is HRM unitarist by design?** Critics argue that commitment-based HRM treats conflict as a communication failure and quietly sidelines collective voice (Guest, 1987; Legge, 1995). Pluralists reply that voice without independence isn't really voice.
- **Partnership: mutual gains or co-option?** Partnership deals can deliver joint gains (Kochan and Osterman, 1994), but UK studies found the gains often tilted towards management and turned unions into junior partners (Guest and Peccei, 2001; Kelly, 2004).
- **Principled negotiation and power.** Critics say *Getting to Yes* underplays power and the distributive side of bargaining (White, 1984), and that negotiation norms differ across cultures (Brett, 2001).
- **The IR Code's balance.** Employers welcome the 300-worker thresholds and strike notice in every establishment as flexibility; most central unions see them as weakening protection, while the statutory route to recognition is a gain for unions. Budd's (2004) test of efficiency, equity and voice is a useful way to weigh them.

A strong answer names the frame behind each argument, uses evidence from both sides, and says what evidence would change its conclusion.

## In India

- **Rules in transition.** The IR Code and its Central Rules (8 May 2026) are in force, but Karnataka, Goa and Delhi had no final rules under the Code in November 2026 (sources disagree on Karnataka), so old state rules continue where they fit the Code. See [the four Labour Codes](/handbooks/labour-law/the-four-labour-codes/) and [state rules](/handbooks/labour-law/state-specific-rules/).
- **Who is a 'worker'.** Most IR protections cover workers, which leaves out people employed mainly in managerial or administrative roles and supervisors earning more than <Fact id="osh.supervisorWageLimit" />. Engineers doing technical work can be workers whatever their pay.
- **Exits.** Retrenching a worker needs <Fact id="notice.retrenchmentNoticeMonths" />'s notice or pay, compensation of <Fact id="notice.retrenchmentCompensationDays" /> per completed year and <Fact id="notice.reskillingFundDays" /> paid into the re-skilling fund; factories, mines and plantations with <Fact id="notice.layoffPermissionWorkers" /> or more also need permission. See [termination, grievances and disputes](/handbooks/labour-law/termination-grievance-and-disputes/).
- **Contract labour.** Many disputes start where contract and permanent workers do similar work for different pay. The OSH Code's contract-labour rules apply at 50 or more contract workers. See [contracts and contract labour](/handbooks/labour-law/contracts-and-fixed-term-employment/).
- **New frontiers.** IT and ITES employees in Karnataka, and gig workers, have formed unions, and Karnataka's 2025 gig-workers law requires dispute committees with equal worker and management members.
