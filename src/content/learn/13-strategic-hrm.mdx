models:
  - id: michigan-matching-model
    name: "Matching model (Michigan)"
    by: "Charles Fombrun, Noel Tichy and Mary Anne Devanna"
    year: "1984"
    idea: "Strategy, structure and HR systems must fit together. The HR cycle of selection, appraisal, rewards and development is tuned to deliver the performance the strategy needs."
    use: "Checking that hiring, appraisal, pay and development all pull towards the same business goal."
    watch: "Treats people as a resource to be matched; little room for employee voice or conflicting interests."
    cite: "Fombrun, C., Tichy, N. M. and Devanna, M. A. (1984) Strategic Human Resource Management. Wiley."
  - id: harvard-hrm-map
    name: "Harvard map of HRM territory"
    by: "Michael Beer, Bert Spector, Paul Lawrence, D. Quinn Mills and Richard Walton"
    year: "1984"
    idea: "Stakeholder interests and situational factors shape four policy choices, which drive commitment, competence, congruence and cost-effectiveness, and long-term wellbeing."
    use: "Building an HR strategy that weighs employees, unions, community and owners, not just the business plan."
    watch: "Broad and descriptive: it says what to consider, not how to choose when stakeholders want different things."
    cite: "Beer, M., Spector, B., Lawrence, P. R., Quinn Mills, D. and Walton, R. E. (1984) Managing Human Assets. Free Press."
  - id: schuler-jackson-strategy-fit
    name: "Competitive strategy and HR fit"
    by: "Randall Schuler and Susan Jackson"
    year: "1987"
    idea: "Cost, quality and innovation strategies need different employee behaviours, so each needs a different menu of HR practices."
    use: "Translating a change in business strategy into specific changes in hiring, pay, appraisal and training."
    watch: "Firms pursue several strategies at once, and strategies change faster than HR systems can."
    cite: "Schuler, R. S. and Jackson, S. E. (1987) Linking competitive strategies with human resource management practices. Academy of Management Executive, 1(3)."
  - id: resource-based-view-hr
    name: "Resource-based view (VRIN)"
    by: "Jay Barney (after Birger Wernerfelt)"
    year: "1991"
    idea: "Lasting advantage comes from resources that are valuable, rare, hard to imitate and hard to substitute. People, skills and culture can qualify."
    use: "Deciding which capabilities to invest in deeply because rivals can't easily copy or buy them."
    watch: "Hard to test and close to circular: winners are said to have valuable resources because they win."
    cite: "Barney, J. (1991) Firm resources and sustained competitive advantage. Journal of Management, 17(1)."
  - id: amo-framework
    name: "AMO framework"
    by: "Eileen Appelbaum, Thomas Bailey, Peter Berg and Arne Kalleberg"
    year: "2000"
    idea: "Performance depends on employees' ability, motivation and opportunity to contribute, so HR practices work best as a bundle that lifts all three."
    use: "Auditing whether the practices for a key role cover skills, incentives and the chance to use them."
    watch: "It names what to cover, not the right mix; whether the three add or multiply is still debated."
    cite: "Appelbaum, E., Bailey, T., Berg, P. and Kalleberg, A. L. (2000) Manufacturing Advantage: Why High-Performance Work Systems Pay Off. Cornell University Press."
  - id: lepak-snell-hr-architecture
    name: "HR architecture"
    by: "David Lepak and Scott Snell"
    year: "1999"
    idea: "How valuable and how unique a group's skills are decides whether to develop, buy, contract or ally, and each group needs its own HR system."
    use: "Deciding which roles to build, buy, contract or partner for, and how to manage each group fairly."
    watch: "Roles move between quadrants as strategy and technology change, and Indian law limits contracting in core work."
    cite: "Lepak, D. P. and Snell, S. A. (1999) The human resource architecture: toward a theory of human capital allocation and development. Academy of Management Review, 24(1)."
  - id: ulrich-hr-roles
    name: "HR roles and the three-pillar model"
    by: "Dave Ulrich (later with Wayne Brockbank)"
    year: "1997; 2005"
    idea: "HR adds value as strategic partner, change agent, administrative expert and employee champion. Firms later built this into business partners, centres of expertise and shared services."
    use: "Designing or reviewing how an HR function is organised and what each team owns."
    watch: "Often copied as a structure; brings hand-off problems, hollowed-out generalists and a squeezed employee champion."
    cite: "Ulrich, D. (1997) Human Resource Champions. Harvard Business School Press."
  - id: hr-scorecard
    name: "HR scorecard"
    by: "Brian Becker, Mark Huselid and Dave Ulrich"
    year: "2001"
    idea: "Measure HR by its contribution to strategy: the workforce outcomes it delivers, the strength and alignment of the HR system, and HR's efficiency."
    use: "Choosing a few HR measures that a CEO recognises as linked to business results."
    watch: "Easily becomes a long list of activity metrics; proving that HR caused a result is hard."
    cite: "Becker, B. E., Huselid, M. A. and Ulrich, D. (2001) The HR Scorecard: Linking People, Strategy, and Performance. Harvard Business School Press."

## Start with the business, then choose the HR

The Hyderabad CHRO's 27 initiatives are not wrong one by one. Each has a sponsor and a reason. Together they are not a strategy, because nothing on the list says which of them matter most for the business the CEO is trying to build.

Strategic HRM asks two questions of every people practice:

- **Vertical fit:** does it build what the business strategy needs?
- **Horizontal fit:** does it reinforce the other HR practices, or pull against them?

A sales incentive that rewards individual targets fits a volume strategy. It undercuts a team-selling strategy, however well it is designed.

Two models from 1984 still frame the subject. The **Michigan model** (Fombrun, Tichy and Devanna) is the "hard" version. Decide the strategy and the structure first, then tune the HR cycle of selection, appraisal, reward and development to deliver the performance they need.

For HR the difference is practical. The Michigan question is "what does the strategy need from people?" The Harvard question adds "what do employees, unions and the community need from us, and what will this cost them over time?" A sound HR strategy asks both.

## Best practice, best fit or resources rivals can't copy

Ask three HR heads what the Hyderabad company should do and you may hear three schools of thought:

- **Best practice** (Pfeffer; Huselid) says some practices raise performance almost everywhere. Pfeffer's list of seven is the best known: employment security, selective hiring, self-managed teams, high pay linked to company performance, extensive training, small status gaps and sharing information.
- **Best fit** (Schuler and Jackson; Miles and Snow) says HR must match the strategy. A cost strategy needs predictable, efficient behaviour; a quality strategy needs care and commitment; an innovation strategy needs risk-taking and collaboration. Each calls for different hiring, pay and appraisal.
- **The resource-based view** (Barney) says lasting advantage comes from resources that are valuable, rare, hard to imitate and hard to substitute. A competitor can copy a policy within a year. It can't easily copy a workforce and culture that have learned, over a decade, to solve quality problems well.

Boxall and Purcell offer the resolution examiners like. General principles travel: hire carefully, develop people, treat them fairly. The specific practices that express those principles must fit the context. In Hyderabad, best practice says "invest in training". Best fit says "train for sterile manufacturing, not general skills". The resource-based view says "make that sterile capability so deep that a rival can't poach it with one hiring drive".

## Inside the black box: ability, motivation and opportunity

Even when HR fits the strategy, how does it turn into performance? Researchers call the missing link the "black box". The most widely used way to open it is **AMO**: people perform when they have the ability, the motivation and the opportunity to contribute (Appelbaum and colleagues, 2000).

</Figure>

The practical lesson is to design HR practices as a bundle. Training (ability) is wasted if pay penalises the time learning takes (motivation), or if operators are never allowed to change a process (opportunity). Two further findings matter for strategy:

- **Line managers make or break it.** CIPD-funded research by Purcell and colleagues (2003) found that line managers bring HR policies to life, so what people experience depends on their manager. Researchers now separate intended, implemented and perceived HR.
- **Strong systems send clear signals.** Bowen and Ostroff (2004) argued that HR works when its messages are distinctive, consistent and widely agreed, so that hiring, appraisal and pay all point to the same priorities.

<Callout type="note" title="At strategic level (SHRM-SCP, CIPD Level 7)">
Expect to be asked how HR adds value, not just what it does. Strong answers trace a chain: business goal, then the capability it needs, then the AMO bundle for the pivotal roles, then employee outcomes, operational results and financial results. They also admit where the evidence is thin: most HR–performance studies are correlational, so claim contribution, not proof.
</Callout>

## Different deals for different roles

A 1,500-person pharma company employs formulation scientists, QA reviewers, line operators, sales representatives, accountants, canteen staff and security guards. One HR system for all of them is either too expensive for some groups or too thin for others.

Lepak and Snell's **HR architecture** (1999) sorts work by two questions. How *valuable* are these skills to the strategy? How *unique* are they to this firm? The answers suggest an employment mode, and an HR system to match.

Three cautions keep the grid honest:

- **Roles move.** Sterile-manufacturing skill may be generic across the industry today and firm-specific once your own processes mature. Review the map every year.
- **Fairness still applies.** A "contract" box is not a licence to treat people worse. Indian law gives fixed-term staff the same wages and benefits as permanent colleagues doing the same work, and makes the principal employer answerable if a contractor fails to pay its workers.
- **The law limits contracting.** Contract labour can't be used for core activities except in specified cases, so the bottom-left box is narrower in India than in the textbook.

## How HR organises itself, and how AI is changing it

In *Human Resource Champions* (1997) and a 1998 Harvard Business Review article, Dave Ulrich argued that HR should be defined by what it delivers, not by what it does. He described four roles on two axes: a strategic or day-to-day focus, and attention to processes or to people.

Organisations turned the roles into a structure, often called the three-legged stool or three-pillar model: **business partners** who advise leaders, **centres of expertise** (COEs) that design programmes, and **shared services** that handle transactions at scale. Ulrich and Brockbank later revised the roles (2005). The critiques are well documented:

- **Hand-offs fail.** When shared services and COEs don't connect, managers go back to their business partner, who is dragged back into admin.
- **The employee champion gets squeezed.** Francis and Keegan (2006) found the employee-facing role weakening as HR chased strategic status.
- **Generalists hollow out.** Junior HR people lose the varied work that used to grow future business partners.
- **Small firms copy a big-company structure.** A 300-person company needs the three kinds of work done well, not three departments.

The model is changing again, mainly because of AI. The direction is clear even though the evidence is early and partly from vendors:

| Element | Classic three-pillar model | What is emerging (2025–26) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Business partners | Generalist advisers to each business unit | Fewer, more senior problem-solvers who use data to redesign work |
| Centres of expertise | Specialists designing programmes by function | Product teams that own whole journeys, such as hiring or onboarding |
| Shared services | Tiered service centres, many of them in India | AI answers routine queries; people take complex and sensitive cases |
| HR capabilities | HR expertise, relationship management | Workforce planning, organisation design, analytics, AI literacy, change |

IBM's own case study says its AskHR assistant now handles most routine employee queries; treat company-reported figures as indicative. The capability gap is better documented. In CIPD's 2026 HR pulse survey of UK people professionals, 77% called workforce planning a priority but only 14% counted it as a specialist skill. For organisation design the figures were 70% and 12%.

## An HR strategy on one page, measured and audited

An HR strategy earns its place on one page when each line connects a business goal to a capability, the roles that carry it, the HR priorities that build it, and a measure the CEO already cares about.

The **HR scorecard** (Becker, Huselid and Ulrich, 2001) helps choose the measures. It separates four kinds:

- **HR deliverables:** the workforce results the strategy needs, such as certified operators or retention in pivotal roles.
- **The high-performance work system:** how strong the core practices are.
- **Alignment:** whether the practices fit the strategy and each other.
- **HR efficiency:** what HR costs, such as HR cost per employee.

Most HR dashboards are full of the last kind. The first kind is what a board reads.

<Worked title="Worked example: an HR strategy on one page for a Hyderabad pharma company">

The CHRO spends an hour with the CEO and two business heads. They agree three goals for the next three years:

1. Get the new sterile injectables plant approved at its first USFDA inspection.
2. Launch four complex injectable products.
3. Hold unit costs flat on the existing oral-solids lines.

**Step 1: find the capabilities and the pivotal roles.** All figures are examples.

| Business goal | Capability it depends on | Pivotal roles and employment mode |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Plant approved first time | Aseptic discipline, data integrity | 120 sterile operators and 25 QA reviewers: develop |
| Four launches | Formulation and analytical science | 40 formulation scientists: develop. Niche testing: ally with a laboratory |
| Flat costs on oral solids | Productivity, flexibility | 600 line operators: acquire and retain. Canteen and housekeeping: contract |

**Step 2: diagnose with AMO.** QA reviewers are leaving at 22% a year, so ability drains away. No operator has worked in a sterile area (ability). Scientists leave for rivals that offer technical career ladders (motivation and opportunity).

**Step 3: write the page.**

| Priority | What HR will do | Measure the CEO sees |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Build sterile capability | A 12-week aseptic academy with a local pharmacy college; skill-based pay steps; operators lead deviation reviews | 120 operators certified before the mock inspection |
| 2. Keep pivotal talent | Market pay review for QA; a technical career ladder for scientists; scientists present at portfolio reviews | Regretted attrition in pivotal roles: 22% down to 10% |
| 3. Productive, fair oral-solids lines | Multi-skilling; productivity gain-sharing negotiated with the union | Output per operator-hour; grievances per 100 workers |

**Step 4: decide what stops.** Of the 27 initiatives, 9 serve these priorities and 8 keep the company compliant and running. The other 10, including the festive-gifting policy and a second engagement survey, pause for a year, with the CEO's agreement.

**Step 5: check the logic.**

- *Vertical fit:* every priority traces back to a business goal.
- *Horizontal fit:* skill-based pay rewards the learning the academy provides.
- *Resource-based view:* deep sterile capability is the hardest thing here for a rival to copy.
- *Harvard check:* the union is consulted on gain-sharing, and contract staff keep their full statutory protection.

The page now fits on one sheet, and every line can be defended in the board room.

</Worked>

The **HR audit** checks whether the system on that page is working. It comes in three kinds:

- **Compliance audit:** registers, wages, the Labour Codes, POSH, PF and ESI. The [HR compliance audit checklist](/toolkit/hr-compliance-audit-checklist/) covers the statutory basics.
- **Practice audit:** policies and processes against good practice and the company's own standards.
- **Strategic audit:** whether HR's money and time go to the priorities on the page.

Whatever the kind, an audit runs in the same order. Agree the scope and the criteria. Gather evidence from documents, data, interviews and surveys. Compare it with the law, the strategy and benchmarks. Rate each gap by risk and impact. Then agree actions with owners and dates, and re-audit a year later. For a quick view of HR's overall maturity, try the [HR maturity self-assessment](/toolkit/hr-maturity-self-assessment/).

## Where experts disagree

Expect these debates in an XLRI case discussion or a CIPD Level 7 assignment.

- **Fit or best practice?** Boxall and Purcell call it a false choice: principles travel, specific practices must fit. Critics of best fit add that strategies change faster than HR systems, so too tight a fit can lock in yesterday's strategy.
- **Does HR cause performance, or follow it?** Most studies are correlational. Wright and colleagues (2005) found HR practices related as strongly to past performance as to future performance, so profitable firms may simply be able to afford better HR.
- **Rhetoric or reality?** Legge (1995) argued that "soft" HR language can disguise "hard" practice. High-performance work systems can mean shared gains or simply more intense work, depending on who you ask.
- **Is the Ulrich model the problem?** Charan (2014) proposed splitting HR into an administrative arm and a leadership-and-organisation arm. Cappelli (2015) argued that much of HR's credibility problem is self-inflicted.

A good answer names the model, applies it to the case, states its limit, and brings in a second perspective to cover the gap.

## In India

- **The contract box has legal limits.** The OSH Code bars contract labour from core activities except in specified situations. Fixed-term employees must get the same wages and benefits as permanent staff doing the same work, with gratuity after <Fact id="gratuity.fixedTermMinYears" /> of service. See [contracts and fixed-term employment](/handbooks/labour-law/contracts-and-fixed-term-employment/).
- **Boards own part of HR strategy.** Listed and certain other companies must have a Nomination and Remuneration Committee. Its remuneration policy covers directors, key managerial personnel and other employees (Companies Act 2013, s.178), so senior pay and succession go through it.
- **People data is becoming audit-grade.** SEBI's BRSR Core asks the largest listed companies to disclose some people metrics, and to have them assured or assessed, such as safety, wellbeing spending and wages paid to women. Scorecard data needs clear definitions and an audit trail.
- **Shared services are an Indian story.** Many global HR service centres sit in Indian cities, so AI's first effects on HR jobs land here. Plan reskilling and redeployment before the savings targets are set.
- **The HR audit has Indian roots.** T. V. Rao's work on the HRD audit and Udai Pareek's diagnostic instruments are widely used, and XLRI teaches HR audit as an elective.
