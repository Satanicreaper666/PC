    by: "Howard Perlmutter; regiocentric added with David Heenan"
    year: "1969; 1979"

    by: "C. K. Prahalad and Yves Doz; Christopher Bartlett and Sumantra Ghoshal"

      - "Power distance is about pay, not hierarchy"
      - "Hofstede's data are too recent to rely on"
    answer: 1
    feedback:
      - "Hofstede's data came from IBM employees in many roles; that isn't the flaw here."

    answer: 1

      - "Activities that benefit only employees and their families are excluded from CSR."

  - id: gh-written-hofstede
    type: written
    level: advanced
    q: "Evaluate how far Hofstede's model helps an Indian GCC design a performance-management process for a team that reports to managers in Germany and the Netherlands. (About 200 words.)"
    why: "Model answer: Hofstede's model is a useful starting point. In his classic data India scores higher on power distance than Germany or the Netherlands, so Indian engineers may be less likely to challenge a rating openly, while their managers may expect open disagreement. The model therefore suggests building explicit channels for upward feedback, such as structured self-assessments and calibration meetings where disagreement is invited, and training managers to give feedback the team can hear. But the model has clear limits. It rests on IBM data from around 1967–73. It describes national averages, and applying them to individuals is the ecological fallacy (McSweeney, 2002). Variation within countries often exceeds variation between them (Taras and colleagues, 2016), and India's own diversity makes 'the Indian employee' an abstraction; young GCC engineers may be closer to their European peers than the averages imply. Institutions matter too: Indian labour law, not culture, governs how poor performance can lawfully be managed. A balanced conclusion is that Hofstede helps frame questions and design safeguards, but the process should be tested with the actual team, through pulse surveys and calibration data, rather than built on country stereotypes."

## Converge or diverge: what travels across borders

Headquarters in the Netherlands sees one leave policy as fair and efficient: the same rules, the same system, the same reports. In Pune, that policy collides with Indian holiday law, statutory leave rules and a team that works EU hours. Both views are reasonable. International HRM is the craft of deciding which one wins, policy by policy.

Two forces pull in opposite directions. **Convergence**: technology, global HR systems and multinational practice push countries towards similar HR. **Divergence**: labour law, unions, training systems and culture keep them apart. Most organisations end up with hybrids, sometimes called crossvergence. The **integration–responsiveness framework** (Prahalad and Doz; Bartlett and Ghoshal) frames this as a trade-off between global efficiency and local fit; the "transnational" firm tries to get both, and to learn across its units.

**Perlmutter's EPRG model** describes the mindset behind these choices.

For HR, the practical rule is to decide clause by clause. Standardise what gives fairness and control at scale: principles, the ethics code, job architecture, data definitions and the pay-equity method. Localise what law, institutions and culture shape: statutory pay and leave, holidays, benefits, and how feedback is given. The worked example later in this module does this for a leave policy.

## Culture models are maps, not verdicts

A Dutch manager tells his Pune team their design is "not good enough". He meant it as useful feedback; the team heard a public rebuke. A week later, the team's silence in a review is read in Rotterdam as agreement. Neither side is wrong about its own norms, and culture models help both see why.

Use these models with three cautions:

- **They describe averages.** Applying a country score to one person is the ecological fallacy (McSweeney, 2002), and variation within a country often exceeds variation between countries (Taras, Steel and Kirkman, 2016).
- **India is many cultures.** Language, region, religion, caste and generation vary widely, so "Indian culture" is itself an average. Hofstede's classic data place India high on power distance, yet young engineers in a GCC may be closer to their European peers than the averages suggest.
- **Institutions matter as much as values.** Labour law, unions and education systems shape what HR can do, whatever the culture.

Culture models are most useful for designing processes that work across differences: explicit channels for upward feedback, calibration meetings where disagreement is invited, and managers trained to give feedback in a form the other side can hear. Erin Meyer's *Culture Map* turns this into practical scales, such as how people disagree and how they give negative feedback.

## The expatriate cycle

A Pune engineering manager spends three years in Rotterdam setting up a product line. She comes back to her old role, at her old grade, with no use for what she learned, and leaves within a year. The company paid for the assignment and lost the return on it.

Research points to where assignments go wrong:

- **Adjustment has three parts** (Black, Mendenhall and Oddou, 1991): to the job, to interacting with locals, and to daily life. Family adjustment often decides all three, which is why Tung (1981) found family problems behind many failures.
- **Failure is more than early return.** Harzing (1995) showed that the widely quoted high failure rates rest on weak sources. Measure performance during the assignment and the return on it, including whether the person stays afterwards.
- **Pay needs a method.** The balance-sheet approach protects home-country buying power; host-based or "local-plus" pay costs less. Tax equalisation or protection stops tax from deciding who goes.
- **There are alternatives.** Short-term, commuter and virtual assignments, inpatriates coming to India, and employer-of-record hires abroad each carry different tax, visa and data risks.

For Indian assignees, check visas, the host country's social security rules and any social security agreement with India before you announce a move.

India's global capability centres (GCCs) are no longer back offices. They run products, analytics, reward and hiring for their parent companies, and increasingly host global leadership roles.

Three consequences for HR:

- **A two-speed labour market.** GCCs project higher pay rises and lower attrition than IT services, and pay premiums for scarce AI skills.
- **Global rules, local law.** GCC HR teams run global job architecture and cross-border duties: the EU AI Act for AI used on EU roles, EU pay-transparency rules where they run reward or hiring for EU entities, and the DPDP Act for every Indian employee.
- **The flow runs both ways.** Practices built in Indian centres now travel back to headquarters, the "reverse diffusion" that international HRM research describes.

<Worked title="Worked example: adapting a global leave policy for the Pune GCC">

All figures are an illustrative example. A 300-person team in the Pune GCC supports EU customers on a 12:30 p.m. to 9:30 p.m. shift. Headquarters' standard gives 30 days' annual leave that lapses if unused, 10 company holidays on the Dutch calendar, and 16 weeks' paid leave for every new parent. Those 30 days are a company choice above the EU's legal minimum of four weeks' paid annual leave, which doesn't apply to staff employed in India.

**Step 1: map each clause against the Indian floor.** State figures are from the [state rules chapter](/handbooks/labour-law/state-specific-rules/), for establishments Maharashtra's Shops and Establishments Act covers.

| Clause | Headquarters | Indian floor | Adapted policy |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Annual leave | 30 days; lapses if unused | <Fact id="leave.accrualDays" /> for OSH Code workers, who can carry forward up to <Fact id="leave.carryForwardMax" />; up to 45 under the state Act | 21 days' earned leave; carry forward up to 45 |
| Casual leave | Not separate | 8 days a year | 8 days |
| Holidays | 10, Dutch calendar | 8 paid holidays, including 26 Jan, 1 May, 15 Aug and 2 Oct | The 8 Indian holidays, plus 3 floating days |
| Parental leave | 16 weeks, every parent | Maternity <Fact id="maternity.weeks" /> for the first two children; no statutory paternity leave | 26 weeks for birth mothers; 16 weeks for other new parents |
| Hours | Office hours | Up to <Fact id="osh.weeklyHours" /> a week; women's late work needs consent and safeguards | EU-overlap shift, transport home, consent recorded |

**Step 2: check the principle survives.** Planned time off at headquarters is 30 + 10 = **40 days**. The adapted policy gives 21 + 8 + 8 + 3 = **40 days**. The principle of comparable rest holds; only the mix changes. Sick leave sits outside both totals.

**Step 3: apply the more generous rule to each group.** For birth mothers, Indian law (26 weeks) beats the global standard (16 weeks). For other parents, the global standard (16 weeks) beats Indian law (none). For leave that lapses, Indian law wins: covered staff can carry leave forward.

**Step 4: meet the business need through rosters, not rights.** EU demand drops in Christmas week, so encourage floating days and leave then. On Indian holidays when EU customers are working, run a volunteer skeleton roster, with the substitute holiday or extra pay the state law requires.

**Step 5: publish and review.** Put the result in the [leave policy](/toolkit/leave-policy/), explain the changes to headquarters as "same principle, local rules", and review after a year using leave taken, cover gaps and attrition.

## CSR: from Carroll's pyramid to section 135

The India board's scholarship idea is generous and would be popular. It also fails the law: activities that benefit only a company's employees and their families can't count towards its CSR obligation.

Milton Friedman (1970) argued that business's social responsibility is to increase profits within the rules. **Stakeholder theory** (Freeman, 1984) replies that firms create value for, and answer to, everyone they affect. **Carroll's pyramid** sorts business responsibilities into four layers.

<Figure title="Carroll's pyramid of corporate social responsibility" caption="Read from the base up: each layer rests on the ones below. The notes give an HR example for each, and the highlighted layer is where most HR judgement calls sit.">
  <Pyramid

      { label: "Philanthropic: be a good corporate citizen", note: "Community skills programmes, volunteering" },
      { label: "Ethical: do what is right and fair", note: "Fair treatment of contract workers", accent: true },
      { label: "Legal: obey the law", note: "Wages on time, safety, equal pay" },
      { label: "Economic: be profitable", note: "Sustainable jobs and pay" }
    ]}
  />
</Figure>

India is unusual: section 135 of the Companies Act 2013 turns part of the philanthropic layer into a legal duty.

| Rule | Requirement |
| --- | --- |
| Who must spend | Net worth of ₹500 crore or more, turnover of ₹1,000 crore or more, or net profit of ₹5 crore or more, in the preceding financial year |
| How much | At least 2% of average net profits of the three preceding financial years |
| Committee | A board CSR committee, unless the amount to be spent is ₹50 lakh or less |
| What counts | Schedule VII activities, such as poverty, health, education and skills, gender equality and the environment |
| What doesn't | Normal business, benefits only for employees and their families, political contributions, marketing sponsorship, meeting other statutory duties |
| Unspent money | Ongoing projects: to an Unspent CSR Account within 30 days of year-end. Otherwise: to a Schedule VII fund within six months |
| Penalty | Company: twice the amount due, or ₹1 crore if less. Each officer in default: one-tenth of it, or ₹2 lakh if less |

HR's role is to connect CSR with people strategy without blurring the two: employee volunteering, skills and apprenticeship projects in the community, CSR as part of the employee value proposition, and employee welfare kept firmly in the benefits budget.

## Reporting people data: BRSR Core and ISO 30414

The group sustainability team's request is part of a wider shift: people data is moving from HR dashboards into audited reports.

SEBI's Business Responsibility and Sustainability Report (BRSR), introduced in 2021, is organised around the nine principles of India's National Guidelines on Responsible Business Conduct. **BRSR Core** (July 2023) picks out key indicators that need independent assurance, phased in by market capitalisation from the top 150 listed companies in FY 2023–24 to the top 1,000 in FY 2026–27. A March 2025 circular allowed "assessment or assurance".

| BRSR Core attribute | People metric | Where HR finds the data |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Employee wellbeing and safety | Wellbeing spend as % of revenue; lost-time injury rate; fatalities; permanent disabilities | Benefits ledger; safety records |
| Gender diversity | Gross wages paid to women as % of total wages; POSH complaints as % of female staff, and how many were upheld | Payroll; Internal Committee records |
| Inclusive development | Wages paid in smaller towns as % of total wages | Payroll by location |

**ISO 30414**, the international standard for human-capital reporting, published its second edition on 24 August 2025. It keeps 11 human-capital areas, sets 14 required metrics for every organisation, and adds auditable requirements. It is voluntary, but it is a sensible template when a group asks for people metrics. European groups also report on their own workforce under EU sustainability rules, whose scope and timing the EU has been revising since 2025, so ask the group exactly which metrics and definitions it needs.

## People risk: ERM and business continuity

SHRM's Risk Management area asks HR to treat people risks the way finance treats market risk: identify them, size them, treat them and keep watching.

Each risk then gets one of four treatments: **avoid** it (don't run the activity), **reduce** it (controls, cross-training, a backup site), **share** it (insurance, or contracts that allocate liability) or **retain** it (accept it knowingly and budget for it).

Quantify where you can. If a payroll outage would cost ₹40 lakh (the single loss expectancy) and is expected once in ten years (an annual rate of 0.1), the annualised loss expectancy is ₹4 lakh. A second site costing ₹15 lakh a year can't be justified on that risk alone; a ₹2 lakh backup arrangement with another team can. These are example figures.

Business continuity for people means named cover for critical roles, cross-trained backups, tested remote working, payroll that can run from another site, and a way to reach every employee in an emergency. People's safety always comes first. The [HR crisis playbook](/handbooks/hr-professional/the-hr-crisis-playbook/) and the [crisis communication templates](/toolkit/crisis-communication-templates/) cover the response itself.

## Where experts disagree

- **Hofstede's critics.** McSweeney (2002) argues that one company's data can't define national cultures. A meta-analysis found cultural values do predict outcomes, but less strongly for individuals than often assumed (Taras, Kirkman and Steel, 2010). Hofstede and the GLOBE team also disputed what GLOBE's scales measure.
- **The expatriate-failure myth.** Harzing (1995) traced the famous high failure rates to weak sources; measuring return on the assignment matters more than counting early returns.
- **Convergence or divergence?** Technology and multinational practice push convergence; institutions and culture sustain divergence. Ralston and colleagues (1997) proposed "crossvergence", new hybrids.
- **Mandatory CSR: responsibility or tax?** Share prices of affected Indian firms fell when the mandate was announced (Manchiraju and Rajgopal, 2017). Critics call it a tax run by companies; supporters point to large, steady funding for social projects.
- **Shared value and ESG ratings.** Critics say "creating shared value" glosses over real trade-offs (Crane and colleagues, 2014), and ESG ratings from different providers disagree sharply (Berg, Kölbel and Rigobon, 2022). People metrics therefore need tight definitions and assurance to avoid "social-washing".

A good answer uses a model to frame the case, tests it against evidence from the setting, and names the institutional factors a culture-only view would miss.

## In India

- **CSR detail.** Implementing agencies must be registered with the Ministry of Corporate Affairs (since April 2021). Companies with an average CSR obligation of ₹10 crore or more need independent impact assessments of projects of ₹1 crore or more, and administrative overheads are capped at 5% of CSR spend.
- **BRSR Core.** FY 2026–27 is the year the top 1,000 listed companies come into scope for assurance or assessment of key indicators, so many HR teams are building audit trails for the first time.
- **GCCs and EU pay transparency.** EU member states were due to bring the Pay Transparency Directive into law by 7 June 2026; most missed the deadline, but there is no EU-level delay. Employers with 150 or more staff in a member state report gender pay gaps from June 2027, so Indian GCCs that run reward, payroll or hiring for EU entities need pay ranges, gender-neutral job evaluation and auditable pay data.
- **State rules.** Leave, holidays and hours still differ by state under the Shops and Establishments Acts, alongside the Labour Codes. Karnataka, Goa and Delhi rules were still in draft in early November 2026, so check the [state rules chapter](/handbooks/labour-law/state-specific-rules/) before localising any global policy.
- **Assignments.** Before sending people abroad, check visas, host-country social security and any social security agreement with India, and the EPF rules for international workers.
