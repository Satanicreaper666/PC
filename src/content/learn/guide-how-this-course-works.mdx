PeopleCraft Learn is a free, self-paced HR course for people moving into HR business partner and strategic roles. It is built from what XLRI teaches in its PGDM (HRM), what CIPD assesses at Level 5 and Level 7, and what SHRM tests in SHRM-CP and SHRM-SCP, plus Indian law and the standard HR books. This page explains how to use it.

## How to study so it sticks

Most people study by rereading and highlighting, because it feels productive. The research says it is one of the weakest ways to learn. In a large review of ten common study techniques, Dunlosky and colleagues (2013) rated **practice testing** and **spacing** as the most useful, **interleaving** as moderately useful, and rereading and highlighting as low in usefulness. Four habits follow from the evidence:

1. **Test yourself before you reread.** Close the page and write down what you remember, then check. In a well-known study, students who practised recalling a passage remembered more a week later than students who reread it, even though rereading felt more effective at the time (Roediger and Karpicke, 2006). Every quiz and cheat sheet in this course is built for this.
2. **Space it out.** Short sessions spread over weeks beat one long session, and the longer you need to remember something, the wider the gaps can be (Cepeda and colleagues, 2006). Spend ten minutes a week on cheat sheets from earlier modules.
3. **Mix your practice.** When you practise one type of question at a time, you never have to decide which idea applies. Mixing questions from several topics makes you choose, which is exactly what an exam or a real workplace problem asks. SHRM's exam mixes every competency in one sitting.
4. **Study a worked example, then try a similar problem.** Learners who study worked examples first learn problem-solving faster than those who struggle alone (Sweller and Cooper, 1985). The benefit fades as you become expert, so once a type of problem feels familiar, go straight to practice (Kalyuga and colleagues, 2003).

## Using the course for SHRM

SHRM tests the same content at two levels, mostly through judgement questions. To use the course for SHRM-CP or SHRM-SCP:

- **Answer every judgement question before you look at the feedback,** and read why each wrong option is weaker. SHRM credits only the single most effective response, so the reasoning matters more than the answer.
- **Keep an error log** by competency: Ethical Practice, Consultation and so on. After a few modules you will see where you lose marks.
- **Mind the altitude.** Questions marked advanced are pitched at SHRM-SCP. If you are sitting CP, practise them anyway: they show you what a CP answer is not.
- **US law.** [Module 12](/learn/uk-and-us-employment-law/) covers it at a lighter level. {CRED.shrmUsLaw.display}; confirm this for your window before you skip it.
- **Finish with the sprint.** [Module 19](/learn/shrm-exam-prep/) is an eight-week plan to exam day, using the exam weights and timed mixed practice. Dates, fees and logistics are in the [credentials guide](/learn/credentials/).

## Using the course for CIPD

CIPD tests the same knowledge in writing, against fixed criteria. To use the course for Level 5 or Level 7:

- **Write the written question in full** at the end of each module, in about 200 words, before you read the model answer. Then compare your structure with it: point, evidence, application to the case, limitation, conclusion.
- **Do the block tasks.** The six written tasks build the habit of long, structured writing that CIPD assignments need, and they give you material from your own organisation to draw on later.
- **Use "Where this sits".** Each module lists the CIPD units it supports, so you can find your notes again when a brief arrives.
- **Read [module 20](/learn/cipd-assignments-and-capstone/) before your first assignment.** It covers command verbs, mapping every criterion, Harvard referencing and the Level 7 research project.
- **Record it as CPD.** CIPD members keep continuing professional development records; CIPD recommends {CRED.cipdCpd.display} of planned learning a year. What counts is a short reflection on how the learning changed your practice.

## Run a study circle at work

Learning with colleagues keeps you going when a week gets busy, and arguing about a scenario teaches more than reading about it. A study circle needs no budget, only a room or a video call and an agreed time.

  <Steps
    items={[

<Callout type="tip" title="Keep it safe to speak">
Agree at the first meeting that cases stay in the room. Never bring names, pay details or health information into the circle. Ask your manager or L&D team to count the hour as learning time; it is cheaper than most training, and the "try it at work" tasks produce visible results.
</Callout>
