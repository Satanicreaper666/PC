  - id: ba-roi-payback
    type: knowledge
    q: "A programme costs ₹60 lakh and is expected to save ₹80 lakh a year. What are its first-year ROI and its payback period?"
    options:
      - "ROI 133%; payback 9 months"
      - "ROI 33%; payback 16 months"
      - "ROI 33%; payback 9 months"
      - "ROI 25%; payback 9 months"
    answer: 2
    feedback:
      - "133% is benefits divided by costs. ROI uses the net gain: benefits minus costs."
      - "16 months comes from dividing the benefit by the cost. Payback divides the cost by the yearly benefit."
      - "Correct. ROI = (80 − 60) ÷ 60 × 100 = 33%. Payback = 60 ÷ 80 = 0.75 years, which is 9 months."
      - "25% divides the net gain by the benefit. ROI divides it by the cost."
    why: "ROI = (benefits − costs) ÷ costs × 100. Payback = investment ÷ annual cash benefit."

    q: "Evaluate how useful ROI is as the main test for approving HR programmes. Use an example from an Indian organisation. (About 200 words.)"
    why: "Model answer: ROI is useful because it puts HR proposals in the language finance uses to compare every claim on money. ## Read the business through its three statements

Start where the CFO starts. The Bengaluru firm earns its revenue by billing engineers to clients: roughly, billable people × hours billed × rate per hour. An empty seat, a slow ramp-up or engineers sitting on the bench is lost revenue, not just an HR problem. The same engineers are also its biggest cost. That is why the CFO hears "22% attrition" as a margin problem.

Every company reports three financial statements. Each answers a different question, and people costs appear in all three.

| Statement | The question it answers | Where people show up |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Profit and loss (P&L) | Did we make money over the year? | Employee benefits expense: salaries, PF, gratuity, bonus, ESOP cost, staff welfare |
| Balance sheet | What do we own and owe on one day? | Provisions for gratuity and leave encashment; bonus and statutory dues payable; the ESOP reserve |
| Cash-flow statement | Where did cash come from, and where did it go? | Payroll and statutory dues, which leave every month whether or not clients have paid |

Read the P&L from top to bottom. Revenue minus operating costs gives EBITDA (earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation). Take off depreciation and interest to get profit before tax, then tax to get profit after tax.

Two points matter most for HR. First, **profit is not cash**. A growing firm can report a healthy profit while clients take 90 days to pay and salaries fall due every month. Second, **people are not on the balance sheet as assets, but the promises made to them are**. Gratuity and leave liabilities sit there as provisions, valued by an actuary. Change the wage definition or who qualifies for gratuity, and those provisions, and the year's profit, change with them.

Read each ratio against the right comparison: your own trend over several years, and firms with the same business model. People-intensive services spend a far larger share of revenue on people than capital-intensive manufacturers. A 55% people cost ratio can be healthy in IT services and alarming in a cement plant. If the ratio rises, ask whether that was planned (hiring ahead of a new contract) or drift (increments without matching productivity).

How a cost behaves matters too. Rent is fixed; subcontractors billed by the hour are variable. Labour sits in between. The economist Walter Oi called it a **quasi-fixed factor** (1962): hiring and training are paid for up front, so firms hold on to skilled people in a slowdown and flex through contract labour instead. That is also why attrition is expensive. Each exit throws away hiring and training the firm has already paid for.

Put a rupee value on that loss and the CFO's question changes. In the Bengaluru firm, 100 of last year's 132 leavers were people it wanted to keep. At a cautious ₹4 lakh per exit (built up in the worked example below), regretted attrition cost about ₹4 crore: one-eighth of EBITDA. The handbook chapter [business acumen: speak the language of money](/handbooks/hr-professional/business-acumen/) has more formulas and a structure for presenting them.

## Read the strategy from the outside in

Strategy answers two questions: where does the business compete, and how does it win? Three classic tools look outward first.

**PESTLE** scans six kinds of outside change: political, economic, social, technological, legal and environmental. It grew from Francis Aguilar's 1967 "ETPS" scan; the six-letter version came later. The skill is the last column: turning a trend into a people decision.

| Factor | A trend the Bengaluru firm faces | What it means for HR |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Political | Visa and outsourcing rules in client countries | Fewer onsite roles; more delivery from India |
| Economic | Clients trimming discretionary tech spend | Slower hiring; bench cost rises |
| Social | Engineers expect hybrid work and visible growth | Career paths and flexibility drive retention |
| Technological | AI tools that write and test code | Fewer entry-level roles; reskilling at scale |
| Legal | The four Labour Codes, in force since 21 November 2025 | A wider wage definition raises PF and gratuity cost |
| Environmental | Large clients asking suppliers for ESG data | Safety, diversity and pay data may be checked |

**Five forces** (Michael Porter, 1979) explain why some industries earn fatter margins than others. Profit depends on rivalry between competitors, the threat of new entrants, the threat of substitutes, and the bargaining power of buyers and of suppliers. HR's angle is that **labour is a supplier**. When a skill is scarce, such as cloud security, the people who have it hold supplier power and pay rises. When buyers are large clients who negotiate rates hard, the margin squeeze lands on people costs.

**Generic strategies** (Porter, 1980) describe how a firm wins: lower cost than its rivals, or something customers will pay more for, across a broad market or in a narrow niche. Randall Schuler and Susan Jackson (1987) showed that each strategy needs different behaviour from employees, and so different HR practices.

## Find what competitors can't copy

Outside-in tools explain the market. The **resource-based view** looks inside: lasting advantage comes from resources and capabilities that rivals can't easily get. Jay Barney (1991) set out the theory, and in 1995 turned it into four questions known as **VRIO**. Each question filters out more of what a firm has, which is why so few capabilities give a lasting edge.

<Figure title="The VRIO test: four questions, fewer survivors at each" caption="Read from the top. A resource that fails a question stops there, with the result shown in its note; only one that passes all four gives a sustained advantage.">
  <Funnel
    stages={[
      { label: "Valuable?", note: "No: a competitive disadvantage" },
      { label: "Rare?", note: "No: parity, the same as rivals" },
      { label: "Costly to imitate?", note: "No: a temporary advantage" },
      { label: "Organised to use it?", note: "No: an advantage left unused" },
      { label: "Sustained competitive advantage", note: "Passes all four tests" }
    ]}
  />
</Figure>

Apply it to the Bengaluru firm. Its engineers who know healthcare claims systems are valuable and fairly rare. A rival can hire a few, but a team that knows the clients' systems, works well together and has earned their trust over years is costly to copy. Whether this becomes a *sustained* advantage depends on the last question. Are career paths, project allocation and pay organised to keep and grow that team? With 22% attrition the honest answer is "not yet", and the advantage is leaking.

That is the strategic case for retention. It isn't "people are our greatest asset". It is "this capability passes three of the four tests, and we are failing the fourth".

The **balanced scorecard** (Robert Kaplan and David Norton, 1992) turns strategy into measures across four linked perspectives. People capability sits at the base, under "learning and growth", and drives everything above it. Their later strategy maps (2004) draw the chain of cause and effect.

<Figure title="The balanced scorecard as a chain of cause and effect" caption="Follow the arrows: each perspective is expected to drive the next. Each arrow is a claim the firm should test with its own data." wide>

      { who: "Customers", title: "Clients renew and add work", detail: "Measure: renewal rate" },
      { who: "Financial", title: "Revenue and margin grow", detail: "Measure: EBITDA margin" }

  />
</Figure>

The scorecard shows the CFO where an HR measure fits. Retaining domain engineers should predict on-time delivery, which should predict renewals, which should predict revenue. Each link is a hypothesis, so test it with your own data; [evidence-based HR](/learn/evidence-based-hr/) shows how.

## Build a costed business case

A business case answers the CFO's two questions. Return on investment (ROI) says what the company gets back. Payback period says how soon.

<Figure title="ROI and payback period" caption="ROI compares the net gain with the cost over the same period. Payback is a quick test of risk: the shorter it is, the less the case depends on forecasts far ahead.">
  <Formula
    parts={[
      { value: "(Benefits − Costs)", label: "net gain over the period, in ₹" },
      { op: "÷" },
      { value: "Costs", label: "same period, in ₹" },
      { op: "×" },
      { value: "100" }
    ]}
    result={{ value: "ROI", label: "% returned on every rupee spent" }}
    example="Example: (₹1.2 crore − ₹75 lakh) ÷ ₹75 lakh × 100 = an ROI of 60%"
  />
  <Formula
    parts={[
      { value: "Investment", label: "up-front cost, in ₹" },
      { op: "÷" },
      { value: "Annual cash benefit", label: "net of any running costs, ₹ a year" }
    ]}
    result={{ value: "Payback period", label: "in years; × 12 for months" }}
    example="Example: ₹75 lakh ÷ ₹1.2 crore a year = 0.625 years, a payback of about 7.5 months"
  />
</Figure>

A case that a finance team will trust has six parts: the problem in rupees, the options (including doing nothing), costs, benefits, risks, and a recommendation with a measure of success. Three habits make it credible:

- Build the benefit from your own data, not a borrowed "attrition costs X times salary" multiple.
- Show a range: cautious, expected and strong.
- Say how you'll know the programme caused the result, for example a pilot with a comparison group.

<Worked title="Worked example: a retention business case for the Bengaluru firm">

All figures are an Example for the fictional firm. Every number is an assumption the CFO can challenge, which is exactly why you write it down.

**1. The problem in rupees.** Of last year's 132 leavers, 100 were regretted. The cost of one regretted exit, built from the firm's own records:

| One regretted exit (Example) | Cost |
| --- | --- |
| Hiring: fees, interviews, checks | ₹1.3 lakh |
| Vacancy: a month's lost margin | ₹1.0 lakh |
| Ramp-up: three months at half pace | ₹1.7 lakh |
| **Total** | **₹4.0 lakh** |

Hiring covers agency or referral fees, interview hours and background checks. Vacancy is the margin lost while a billable seat sits empty. Ramp-up counts half of three months' pay, because a new joiner works at about half productivity at first. The total is under a third of a leaver's average CTC of ₹14 lakh, which is deliberately cautious. So 100 regretted exits × ₹4 lakh = **₹4 crore a year**.

**2. The programme: ₹75 lakh in year one.** Training for 50 delivery managers (₹20 lakh); a career framework and internal job board (₹15 lakh); retention pay for 60 hard-to-replace engineers (₹30 lakh); stay interviews and running costs (₹10 lakh).

**3. Benefit, ROI and payback, as a range.**

| Scenario (Example) | Benefit | ROI; payback |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cautious: 15 exits avoided | ₹60 lakh | −20%; 15 months |
| Expected: 30 exits avoided | ₹1.2 crore | 60%; 7.5 months |
| Strong: 40 exits avoided | ₹1.6 crore | 113%; 5.6 months |

The programme breaks even at about 19 avoided exits (₹75 lakh ÷ ₹4 lakh). That is the number to test. Payback assumes savings start at once; if they lag a quarter while managers are trained, add about three months.

**4. What the CFO will test, and your answers.**

- *Is ₹4 lakh right?* Show the three parts and where each came from.
- *Did the programme cause the fall?* Run it first in two delivery units and compare their exits with two similar units that start six months later.
- *What if the job market cools?* Exits may fall anyway. That is why you need the comparison group.

**5. The answer in the CFO's terms.** "Regretted attrition costs us about ₹4 crore a year. A ₹75 lakh programme pays for itself within the year if it prevents 30 of those exits, and breaks even at 19. We'll prove it in two units before we scale it."

</Worked>

<Callout type="note" title="At strategic level (SHRM-SCP, CIPD Level 7)">
Senior HR is judged on the portfolio, not on one case. Compare the retention programme with other uses of the same ₹75 lakh, such as hiring ahead of a large contract or reskilling the bench. For cases that run over several years, use net present value (NPV), which discounts future benefits to today's rupees. The CFO weighs every proposal against every other one, so show why yours ranks first.
</Callout>

## Where experts disagree

Expect these debates in an XLRI strategy case or a CIPD Level 7 assignment.

- **Talking money can shrink HR to cost.** Accounts treat training and wellbeing as expenses, not investments, so they are the easiest lines to cut. Attempts to put people on the balance sheet (human-resource accounting) never caught on. Critics warn that the ROI habit can crowd out arguments about fairness and wellbeing that matter for their own sake.
- **HR ROI claims are often inflated.** Attribution, double counting and borrowed "replacement cost equals X% of salary" multiples produce heroic numbers. Finance teams discount them, so a range and a comparison group earn more trust than one big figure.
- **The strategy tools have limits.** Five forces is a snapshot of one industry, and platforms blur industry lines. The resource-based view can be circular: a resource is called valuable because the firm succeeded (Priem and Butler, 2001). And many firms win by combining low cost and differentiation, despite Porter's warning about being "stuck in the middle".
- **Budgets invite gaming.** Managers pad budgets and spend to the limit. The "beyond budgeting" school argues for rolling forecasts instead (Hope and Fraser, 2003).

A good answer uses the tool, names its limit, and says what else it would check: attribution and a range for a business case, and whether the analysis still holds as the market moves for strategy.

## In India

- **Find people cost in any annual report.** Indian companies show employee benefits expense as one line in the statement of profit and loss, and the notes split it into items such as salaries and wages, contributions to provident and other funds, and staff welfare. Listed companies' reports are public, so you can compare yourself with peers in minutes.
- **The Labour Codes changed the cost base.** Since 21 November 2025, if excluded allowances such as HRA add up to more than <Fact id="wages.halfRule" /> of total pay, the excess counts as wages for PF, gratuity and similar dues. Fixed-term employees now qualify for gratuity after <Fact id="gratuity.fixedTermMinYears" />. Both raise payroll cost and the gratuity provision on the balance sheet. See [wages and pay](/handbooks/labour-law/wages-and-pay/).
- **Budget for the new PF ceiling.** The EPF wage ceiling rose to <Fact id="epf.wageCeiling" /> from 17 September 2026. A budget built on the old ceiling understates employer PF cost.
- **Exits cost more than the last payslip.** Retrenching a worker means <Fact id="notice.retrenchmentCompensationDays" /> for each completed year of service, plus <Fact id="notice.reskillingFundDays" /> paid into the re-skilling fund. Industrial establishments with <Fact id="notice.layoffPermissionWorkers" /> or more need government permission first. Karnataka, Goa and Delhi rules were still in draft in early November 2026, so state details may change. See [termination, grievance and disputes](/handbooks/labour-law/termination-grievance-and-disputes/).
- **Know which money language your business speaks.** Family business groups, public sector undertakings (PSUs), multinational subsidiaries and global capability centres (GCCs), and venture-funded start-ups judge HR proposals differently. A start-up counts runway in months, a listed firm watches margins and earnings per share, and a PSU works within budget allocations.
