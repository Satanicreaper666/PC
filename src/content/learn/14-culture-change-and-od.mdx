## Look beneath the merger's symptoms

The board sees symptoms: slow decisions, sniping between teams and good people leaving Bengaluru. Some may have plain structural causes, such as approval limits or pay gaps. Others are cultural: the unspoken rules each company has learned, over years, about how work gets done here.

Edgar Schein's model separates culture into three levels:

- **Artefacts** are what you can see and hear: offices, dress, meetings, jargon, how quickly emails get answered.
- **Espoused values** are what people say matters: values statements, strategy documents, town-hall speeches.
- **Basic underlying assumptions** are what people take for granted without saying. They drive behaviour most.

The method matters as much as the model. Artefacts are easy to see and easy to misread: casual dress in Bengaluru may signal speed, or simply youth. Ask people to explain the artefacts and you hear the espoused values. Notice where behaviour contradicts the values, such as "we empower our branches" alongside four signatures for a small exception, and you have found an assumption.

For HR this means culture work starts with evidence, not with a workshop on new values. Interviews, watching real decisions being made and looking at who gets promoted reveal more than a values survey.

## Profiling culture, and why national scores mislead

Schein tells you where to look. To compare two cultures, or today's culture with the one you need, a common yardstick helps. The **Competing Values Framework** (Quinn and Rohrbaugh; Cameron and Quinn) places organisations on two tensions: flexibility versus stability and control, and an internal versus an external focus. The four quadrants are four culture types.

The usual tool is the Organizational Culture Assessment Instrument (OCAI). Employees split 100 points across the four types on six aspects of the organisation, once for "now" and once for "preferred". The gaps show where people want change, and where two merging firms differ most.

**Be careful with national culture.** Hofstede compared countries on dimensions such as power distance and individualism. His later study of organisations (Hofstede and colleagues, 1990) found that organisational cultures differ mainly in *practices*, while national cultures differ mainly in *values*. Two lessons follow:

- A national score is an average. It can't tell you how a particular person, team or company behaves.
- Chennai and Bengaluru staff share a national culture. What divides them is organisational practice, which is good news, because practices can change.

Use national data to anticipate patterns, such as reluctance to contradict a senior person in public, then test them with the people in front of you.

## Leading change: the organisation and the individual

Once you know the gap, you need a way to close it. The best-known models answer different questions, so use them together rather than picking one:

- **Lewin** (1947) saw change as unfreezing current habits, moving, and refreezing. His force-field analysis lists the forces driving a change and those restraining it. Weakening a restraint usually works better than pushing harder.
- **Kotter** (1995, 1996) studied why big transformations fail and set out eight steps for leaders: create urgency, build a guiding coalition, form a vision, communicate it, remove obstacles, create short-term wins, keep up the momentum, and anchor the change in the culture.
- **ADKAR** (Hiatt, at Prosci) works at the level of each person: awareness of why, desire to take part, knowledge of how, ability to do it, and reinforcement to make it last.

People also need time. **Bridges** (1991) separated change, the event on the calendar, from transition, the inner process of letting go. Transition has three phases. The **Kübler-Ross** grief curve (denial, anger, bargaining, depression, acceptance) is often borrowed for the same purpose. Use it with care: it came from work with dying patients, not employees, and even grief rarely follows a fixed order.

For HR the combined message is to plan at two levels. Kotter's steps are the leadership agenda. ADKAR and Bridges tell each manager what their people need this month.

## Resistance is information

Branch managers who say the digital process "will kill collections" may be protecting their turf. They may also be right. Kotter and Schlesinger (1979) found four common causes of resistance and matched a response to each.

| Cause of resistance | What it sounds like | Responses that fit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Self-interest: people expect to lose something | "My branch loses its credit authority" | Negotiation and agreement; a clear role in the new design |
| Misunderstanding and low trust | "They're closing branches next" | Education and communication; leaders answering questions in person |
| A different reading of the change | "The app ignores how rural borrowers repay" | Participation: involve the critics in fixing the design |
| Low tolerance for change | "I can't learn another system at 52" | Facilitation and support: coaching, time, practice |

They also listed manipulation and coercion. These can work when time is very short, but they cost trust, and where there is a union or an organised workforce they invite a dispute.

Later research went further. Ford, Ford and D'Amelio (2008) argued that change agents partly create "resistance" by labelling every objection as resistance and ignoring what it says. Piderit (2000) showed that most people feel ambivalent rather than simply for or against. Much earlier, Coch and French's factory study (1948) found that groups who helped design a change accepted it faster.

The HR move is to listen to the objection, test it against data, and change the design where the critics are right. Resistance that continues after that is a matter for leadership and, if needed, performance management.

## Diagnosing and changing with people: OD

Organisation development (OD) is planned change done with people rather than to them, using behavioural science. Its core method is **action research**, which Lewin described in 1946: diagnose with the people involved, act, evaluate, and go round again.

Two OD tools are worth knowing well. **Weisbord's six boxes** (1976) give a quick, structured diagnosis. Look at purposes, structure, relationships, rewards, leadership and helpful mechanisms such as planning and IT, all inside their environment. Leadership's job is to keep the other five in balance.

**Appreciative inquiry** (Cooperrider and Srivastva, 1987) starts from what works rather than from problems. Groups choose a positive topic, then move through four stages:

Appreciative inquiry suits mergers because it lets each side say what it is proud of before it is asked to change.

<Worked title="Worked example: diagnosing culture after the NBFC merger">

HR runs a four-week diagnosis: an OCAI survey of 600 staff from both firms, 40 interviews, observation of six credit committees and an analysis of exits. All figures are examples.

**Step 1: profile both cultures.** Points out of 100 on the Competing Values Framework.

| Culture type | Chennai now | Chennai preferred | Bengaluru now | Bengaluru preferred |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Clan | 35 | 30 | 20 | 30 |
| Adhocracy | 10 | 20 | 40 | 30 |
| Market | 15 | 25 | 30 | 25 |
| Hierarchy | 40 | 25 | 10 | 15 |

The two firms are almost opposites today, but their preferred profiles are close. Both want more collaboration; Chennai wants less hierarchy, and Bengaluru wants a little more structure. That shared target is the basis for change.

**Step 2: find where the cultures collide, using the six boxes.**

| Box | What the data shows | Schein level | Action |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Purposes | Chennai protects the loan book; Bengaluru chases growth; no joint goal | Espoused values differ | Three joint targets covering growth and asset quality |
| Structure | Four signatures for exceptions; the digital team sits outside the credit chain | Artefact | A delegation matrix by loan size and risk |
| Relationships | Branch staff and developers never meet; jokes about "app people" | Assumption: "they don't understand lending" | Mixed teams for the next product launch |
| Rewards | Branch bonus pays on collections only; ESOPs for Bengaluru staff alone | Artefact, and a fairness gap | Joint measures; harmonise over two pay cycles |
| Leadership | All eight top roles went to Chennai managers | Signals whose culture "won" | A balanced leadership team |
| Helpful mechanisms | Two loan systems and two HRMS | Artefact | A single integration roadmap |

**Step 3: answer the board.** It is partly a culture problem, but four of the six boxes are structure, reward or leadership choices that leaders can change this quarter. Start there, because changed decisions shift assumptions faster than workshops do. Then run appreciative-inquiry sessions in mixed groups to build a shared story, and repeat the OCAI in a year to check the gap is closing.

</Worked>

## Learning organisations: questioning the rules

Some organisations get better at changing. Peter Senge's **learning organisation** (1990) rests on five disciplines: personal mastery, mental models, shared vision, team learning and systems thinking. Systems thinking is the "fifth discipline" that ties the others together.

Chris Argyris added the distinction HR uses most. **Single-loop learning** fixes errors within the existing rules, like a thermostat that holds a room at 22°C. **Double-loop learning** questions the rules themselves: should the room be at 22°C at all? Argyris also showed why double-loop learning is rare. What people actually do (their theory-in-use) differs from what they say they do (their espoused theory), and defensive routines spare everyone embarrassment.

In the merged NBFC, single-loop learning means tightening the approval process. Double-loop learning means asking whether branch judgement and data models could each catch what the other misses. HR can help by running blameless reviews after failures, rewarding people who raise problems early, and asking leaders to go first.

<Callout type="note" title="At strategic level (SHRM-SCP, CIPD Level 7)">
Senior answers choose a change strategy, not just a model. Beer and Nohria (2000) contrast Theory E, fast change for economic value led from the top, with Theory O, slower change that builds capability through participation. Mergers often need both, in sequence: decide structure and leadership quickly, then build the shared culture with people. Say which you are proposing and why.
</Callout>

## Where experts disagree

Expect these debates in an XLRI case discussion or a CIPD Level 7 assignment.

- **Can culture be managed at all?** Smircich (1983) distinguished culture as something an organisation *has*, which leaders can engineer, from something an organisation *is*, which emerges. Much culture-change consulting quietly assumes the first.
- **Does 70% of change really fail?** The figure is repeated everywhere, yet Hughes (2011) found no sound evidence behind it. Don't use it in an assignment.
- **Planned steps or emergent politics?** Cummings, Bridgman and Brown (2016) showed that Lewin's "three steps" were largely packaged by later writers. Processual researchers such as Pettigrew see change as political and continuous rather than a sequence to follow.
- **Is resistance a problem or a signal?** Classic models treat resistance as something to overcome. Later work treats it as feedback and as partly created by how change is managed.

A good answer names the model, shows where it fits the case, states its limit, and brings in a second perspective to cover the gap.

## In India

- **Changing service conditions needs notice.** Harmonising workers' shifts, hours, wages or leave after a merger needs at least 21 days' notice to those affected under the Industrial Relations Code. See [termination, grievance and disputes](/handbooks/labour-law/termination-grievance-and-disputes/).
- **Restructuring has legal limits.** If change leads to job losses, retrenchment rules apply, and industrial establishments with <Fact id="notice.layoffPermissionWorkers" /> or more need government permission to lay off, retrench or close.
- **Hierarchy shapes how change is heard.** In many Indian organisations people won't contradict a senior leader in public. Anonymous pulse surveys, skip-level meetings and small mixed-level groups surface concerns that town halls miss.
- **Founders and families carry culture.** In founder-led and family-run firms, many artefacts and assumptions trace back to the owners. Culture change without their visible, sustained commitment rarely lasts.
- **Indian OD has its own tools.** Udai Pareek's OCTAPACE profile measures eight values, from openness to experimentation, and is widely used in Indian HRD climate and culture audits. It works well alongside the Competing Values Framework.
