    use: "Designing workshops, simulations and action learning that include doing and reflecting, not just input."
    watch: "The cycle is useful; Kolb's learning-style inventory, and matching teaching to styles, lack support."
    cite: "Kolb, D. A. (1984) Experiential Learning: Experience as the Source of Learning and Development. Prentice Hall."
  - id: knowles-andragogy
    name: "Andragogy"
    by: "Malcolm Knowles"
    year: "1968; 1970"
    idea: "Adults learn best when they know why, draw on their experience, direct their own learning and work on real problems."
    use: "Making programmes for experienced staff relevant, participative and built around problems."
    watch: "A set of design principles more than a tested theory; many children learn this way too."
    cite: "Knowles, M. S. (1970) The Modern Practice of Adult Education: Andragogy versus Pedagogy. Association Press."

    watch: "The ratio comes from executives recalling key events, not measurement; don't use it to cut training."
    cite: "McCall, M. W., Lombardo, M. M. and Morrison, A. M. (1988) The Lessons of Experience. Lexington Books."

    by: "Benjamin Bloom and colleagues; revised by Lorin Anderson and David Krathwohl"
    year: "1956; 2001"

    use: "Writing objectives and assessments at the level the job actually needs."
    watch: "The levels overlap in practice; treat it as a guide to verbs, not a strict ladder."
    cite: "Anderson, L. W. and Krathwohl, D. R. (eds) (2001) A Taxonomy for Learning, Teaching, and Assessing. Longman."

    use: "Agreeing, before a programme is designed, what evidence it must produce."
    watch: "A taxonomy, not a causal chain: good reactions don't predict learning or behaviour change."

    by: "Jack Phillips"

    use: "Large, costly programmes where leaders need a financial case."
    watch: "Isolation and money values rest on estimates; a precise-looking ROI can hide generous assumptions."

## Start with the performance problem, not the course

A vendor's feedback scores tell you that participants enjoyed the workshop. They don't tell you whether supervisors at other clients went back and ran better shifts. Before the Chennai plant spends ₹12 lakh, HR has to answer three questions: what should the new supervisors do differently, why aren't they doing it now, and would a workshop change that?

William McGehee and Paul Thayer set out the classic answer in 1961: analyse the need at three levels.

The person level comes down to a blunt question: could they do it if their job depended on it? If they could, training won't fix it. A two-week needs analysis at the Chennai plant might find three causes, only one of which is a skill:

- New supervisors haven't learned to give feedback or run a shift handover: a **skill gap**.
- Their production managers rarely coach them: a **support gap**.
- Their shifts get the newest operators: a **staffing decision**.

A workshop can close the first gap. The other two need a manager's time and a different rostering rule, and without them the workshop is likely to fail.

## How adults learn

Most of the new supervisors learned their jobs by doing them. David Kolb's **experiential learning cycle** (1984) explains why reflection matters as much as experience. People learn by having an experience, looking back at it, making sense of it, and trying something different next time.

Malcolm Knowles popularised **andragogy**, the idea that adults learn differently from children. Adults need to know why they are learning something, bring experience worth using, prefer to direct themselves, and learn best around real problems. For supervisors, that means working on their own shift's problems, not generic case studies.

The **70-20-10 model** says most development comes from challenging experience (70%), some from other people (20%) and a little from courses (10%). The figures come from executives recalling key events, not from measurement, but the point stands: a workshop is the smallest part of becoming a good supervisor.

Learning science adds a few robust findings that L&D often ignores:

- Spaced practice beats one long session.
- Recalling what you learned (retrieval practice) makes it last longer than re-reading it.
- Too much new information at once overloads learners.
- There is no good evidence that matching teaching to people's preferred "learning styles" improves learning.

## Design the programme: ADDIE or SAM, with objectives that match the job

Two design processes dominate. **ADDIE** (analyse, design, develop, implement, evaluate) grew out of instructional systems design for the US armed forces in 1975. **SAM**, the Successive Approximation Model of Michael Allen and Richard Sites (2012), builds rough versions quickly and improves them with learners in short cycles.

Whichever you use, write objectives with **Bloom's taxonomy**, as revised by Anderson and Krathwohl in 2001. Its six levels run from remembering to creating, and the verb tells you what to assess.

| Level | Typical verbs | Objective for new supervisors |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Remember | list, name | List the five items in a shift handover |
| Understand | explain, summarise | Explain why defects rise when handovers are skipped |
| Apply | use, carry out | Run a five-minute handover using the checklist |
| Analyse | compare, diagnose | Find the cause of a defect spike in shift data |
| Evaluate | judge, justify | Choose between two corrective actions and justify the choice |
| Create | design, plan | Design a skills matrix and rotation plan for the shift |

Most job training should reach at least "apply". For the Chennai supervisors, a better design is a two-day workshop built on practice and role-play, four half-day clinics spaced over eight weeks, coaching from production managers in between, and a handover checklist used on every shift.

## Evaluate what changed, and what it was worth

Donald Kirkpatrick's **four levels** (1959) are the shared language of evaluation. Many organisations stop at level 1, the feedback form. The newer version of the model, from James and Wendy Kirkpatrick, starts at the other end: agree the results you want, then the behaviours that produce them, and design backwards. Jack Phillips added a fifth level, **return on investment**, for programmes big enough to need a financial case.

Phillips' method has three disciplines. **Isolate** the programme's effect, ideally with a comparison group, so you don't claim credit for other changes. **Convert** results to money using figures finance accepts. **Count every cost**, including people's time away from work.

<Figure title="Phillips' ROI formula" caption="Divide what the programme returned, after its costs, by what it cost; above zero means it paid for itself.">
  <Formula
    parts={[
      { value: "Net programme benefits", label: "money benefits minus full costs" },
      { op: "÷" },
      { value: "Programme costs", label: "fully loaded, including time" },
      { op: "×" },
      { value: "100" }
    ]}
    result={{ value: "ROI (%)", label: "return on the money spent" }}
    example="Example: (₹26.4 lakh − ₹18 lakh) ÷ ₹18 lakh × 100 = 47%"
  />
</Figure>

<Worked title="Worked example: the ROI of the supervisor programme">

All figures are an illustrative example. The redesigned programme ran for all 40 new supervisors; benefits are counted for the first 12 months only.

**Step 1: count the full costs.**

- Vendor, for a two-day workshop and four half-day clinics: ₹9,00,000
- Supervisors' time off the line: 40 × 4 days × ₹2,500 a day = ₹4,00,000
- Production managers' coaching: 8 managers × 20 hours × ₹1,500 an hour = ₹2,40,000
- Needs analysis, design and evaluation (HR time): ₹1,60,000
- Venue, materials and travel: ₹1,00,000
- **Total: ₹18,00,000**

The vendor fee is lower than the ₹12 lakh first approved, but the full cost is higher, because people's time is a real cost.

**Step 2: isolate the effect.** Shifts run by experienced supervisors act as the comparison, because they share the same plant-wide changes.

| Measure | Effect | Trained shifts | Comparison shifts |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Defect rate | −0.4 points | 2.4% → 1.9% | 1.5% → 1.4% |
| Operator attrition | −5 points | 36% → 30% | 22% → 21% |

The trained shifts improved by 0.5 points on defects and 6 points on attrition; the comparison shifts improved by 0.1 and 1 point anyway, so only the difference is credited to the programme.

**Step 3: convert to money.**

- **Defects.** The new supervisors' shifts build 60 lakh units a year. 0.4% of 60,00,000 = 24,000 fewer defective units × ₹60 rework and scrap cost = **₹14,40,000**.
- **Attrition.** Their shifts have 600 operators. 5% of 600 = 30 fewer leavers × ₹40,000 to replace and train each one = **₹12,00,000**.
- **Total benefits: ₹26,40,000.**

**Step 4: work out the return.** Net benefits = ₹26,40,000 − ₹18,00,000 = ₹8,40,000. ROI = ₹8,40,000 ÷ ₹18,00,000 × 100 = **47%**. The benefit–cost ratio is 1.47, and the programme pays back its cost in about eight months.

**Step 5: test the assumptions.** If replacing an operator costs ₹25,000 rather than ₹40,000, the attrition benefit falls to ₹7,50,000, total benefits to ₹21,90,000, and ROI to about 22%. Show the board both figures. Report benefits you didn't convert to money, such as supervisors' confidence, as intangibles.

</Worked>

ROI tells you whether a programme paid. Robert Brinkerhoff's **Success Case Method** tells you why. Survey all participants briefly, pick the few who used the learning most and least, and interview them. The verified stories show what made the difference, often a manager who followed up, and what got in the way.

## Make it stick: transfer and leadership development

Most training that "fails" fails after the classroom. Timothy Baldwin and Kevin Ford's review (1988) found that whether people use what they learned depends on three things together.

<Figure title="What decides transfer of learning" caption="Read the three sets together: transfer happens where the learner, the programme and the workplace all support it.">
  <Venn
    sets={[
      { label: "The learner", items: ["Ability", "Motivation", "Self-belief"] },
      { label: "The programme", items: ["Realistic practice", "Spaced follow-up"] },
      { label: "The workplace", items: ["Manager support", "A chance to apply it"] }
    ]}
    shared={{ label: "Transfer", items: ["New behaviour on the job, kept up"] }}
  />
</Figure>

For HR, the cheapest lever is the manager. A ten-minute conversation before the programme about what to work on, and another afterwards about where to use it, does more than an extra day of content. Give people a chance to apply the skill within a week, a job aid such as a checklist, and a follow-up session to share what worked.

**Leadership development** shows the same pattern at a larger scale. Michael Beer and colleagues found that sending managers on programmes without changing the organisation they return to wastes much of the spend. Programmes work better when tied to real work, through stretch assignments, action learning on live business problems and coaching, and when senior leaders change what they reward.

<Callout type="note" title="At strategic level (SHRM-SCP, CIPD Level 7)">
Senior L&D leaders act as performance consultants, not course providers. Tie the learning plan to the workforce plan: which capabilities the strategy needs, which you will build rather than buy, and how you will know they are being used. Judge the L&D budget on business outcomes, and stop programmes that can't show behaviour change.
</Callout>

## Where experts disagree

- **Is Kirkpatrick a chain?** The levels are often read as cause and effect: enjoy it, learn it, use it, get results. A 1997 meta-analysis by George Alliger and colleagues found that reactions barely relate to learning or behaviour, so the levels are better treated as separate questions. Will Thalheimer's Learning-Transfer Evaluation Model (2018) goes further and ranks evaluations by how close they get to real job performance.
- **How precise is ROI?** Supporters say money is the language leaders understand. Critics say isolation and conversion rest on estimates, so a figure such as 47% looks more certain than it is. The fair answer is to state assumptions and show a range.
- **Popular models with weak evidence.** The 70-20-10 ratio is "a fact in search of evidence", as Alan Clardy put it in 2018. Reviews by Harold Pashler and colleagues (2008) found no good evidence that matching teaching to learning styles helps. Even the claim that only 10% of training transfers to the job is itself unsupported.
- **Does training work at all?** Meta-analyses find medium-sized effects when training is well designed, and Eduardo Salas and colleagues (2012) set out what matters before, during and after it. The debate is less about whether training works than about whether organisations design and support it properly.

A good answer uses the model for what it does well, names its evidence gap, and recommends an evaluation or design that fits the decision being made.

## In India

- **Apprentices Act 1961.** The Act still applies alongside the Labour Codes. Establishments in covered industries with 30 or more workers, counting contract workers, must engage apprentices at 2.5% to 15% of their total workforce; it is optional for 4 to 29. Apprentices are trainees, not workers, and must get at least the notified minimum stipend; the national scheme shares part of the cost. In September 2025 the skills ministry widened the list of covered industries to include services such as IT, retail, healthcare and finance, so check whether the duty now applies to you on the [Apprenticeship India portal](https://www.apprenticeshipindia.gov.in/).
- **Young apprentices' data.** Once the DPDP Act's main duties apply, in about May 2027, processing the personal data of apprentices or interns under 18 will need verifiable consent from a parent.
- **Worker re-skilling fund.** Under the Industrial Relations Code, an employer that retrenches a worker pays <Fact id="notice.reskillingFundDays" /> for that worker into a re-skilling fund, and the money is credited to the worker within 45 days to help them retrain. The Central Rules of May 2026 set up the fund, but operating details were still being settled, so check the current procedure.
- **India's HRD tradition.** In 1975, Udai Pareek and T. V. Rao designed an integrated HRD system for Larsen & Toubro, linking appraisal, feedback, training, career planning and organisation development. It started the separate HRD function many Indian firms still have.
- **Training bonds.** Bonds are common after expensive training. Keep the amount in proportion to the real cost of the training, reduce it as service builds up, and take legal advice; an amount that works as a penalty is less likely to be upheld. See [performance management and learning](/handbooks/hr-professional/performance-and-learning/).
