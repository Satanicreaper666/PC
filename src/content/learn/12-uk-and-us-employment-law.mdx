## Same problem, three rulebooks

The Pune HR business partner's instinct is to reach for the Indian playbook: a month's notice, a clean exit and a quick full and final settlement. In Manchester and Texas that playbook misses most of what matters. UK law judges whether the reason and the process were fair. US law starts from employment at will, then forbids decisions driven by protected characteristics or retaliation.

Law doesn't travel, but principles do. All three systems reward the same habits, which is why they turn up in XLRI, CIPD and SHRM teaching alike.

## UK: fair reasons and fair process

UK unfair-dismissal law sits in the Employment Rights Act 1996. An employee with enough service can claim if dismissed without a fair reason or a fair process. A tribunal asks three questions:

1. **Was there a potentially fair reason?** There are five: capability, conduct, redundancy, a legal bar on continuing the job, and 'some other substantial reason'.
2. **Was the procedure fair?** The Acas Code of Practice on disciplinary and grievance procedures sets the minimum, and a tribunal can raise or cut an award by up to 25% if either side unreasonably ignored it. Since *Polkey* (1987), skipping a fair procedure usually makes a dismissal unfair, even if the outcome would have been the same.
3. **Was dismissal within the band of reasonable responses?** The tribunal asks whether a reasonable employer could have dismissed, not whether it would have (*Iceland Frozen Foods v Jones*, 1982). For misconduct, the employer needs a genuine belief, reasonable grounds and a reasonable investigation (*British Home Stores v Burchell*, 1978).

**Who can claim.** For dismissals taking effect before 1 January 2027, the qualifying period is two years; from 1 January 2027 it is six months, and the cap on the compensatory award goes. Some dismissals are automatically unfair from day one, such as those for whistleblowing, pregnancy or trade union membership, and discrimination claims never need qualifying service. Since 1 October 2026 the time limit for tribunal claims is six months rather than three.

**Two other dismissal claims:**

- **Wrongful dismissal** is a breach of contract, usually dismissal without the notice the contract or the statutory minimum requires. The statutory minimum is one week after a month's service, then a week for each complete year from two years, up to 12 weeks.
- **Constructive dismissal** arises when an employee resigns in response to the employer's fundamental breach of contract, such as an unagreed pay cut or a serious breakdown of trust (*Western Excavating v Sharp*, 1978). It is then treated as a dismissal.

### Redundancy and TUPE

**Redundancy** is a potentially fair reason when work of a particular kind has ceased or diminished. A fair redundancy needs fair selection criteria, consultation and a search for other jobs, and employees with two years' service get statutory redundancy pay. Proposing 20 or more redundancies at one establishment within 90 days triggers collective consultation, at least 30 days before the first dismissal (45 days for 100 or more), and a notification to the government. Since 6 April 2026 the maximum protective award for failing to consult is 180 days' pay, and from April 2027 the threshold is due to be counted across the whole organisation.

**TUPE**, the Transfer of Undertakings (Protection of Employment) Regulations 2006, protects employees when a business or a service moves to a new employer, including most outsourcing. Employees move automatically on their existing terms. A dismissal because of the transfer is automatically unfair unless there is an economic, technical or organisational reason entailing changes in the workforce. Both employers must inform employee representatives, and consult them about any measures they plan.

## UK: equality, working time, pay and family rights

**The Equality Act 2010** protects nine characteristics: age, disability, gender reassignment, marriage and civil partnership, pregnancy and maternity, race, religion or belief, sex, and sexual orientation. It bans direct discrimination, indirect discrimination (unless objectively justified), harassment and victimisation, and requires reasonable adjustments for disabled people. There is no qualifying service and no cap on compensation. Since 30 October 2026 employers must take all reasonable steps to prevent sexual harassment of their staff, and must not permit harassment by third parties such as clients. Equal pay for equal work is implied into every contract, and employers with 250 or more employees publish their gender pay gap each year.

**Working time and pay:**

- The Working Time Regulations 1998 limit the working week to an average of 48 hours (workers can opt out in writing) and give 5.6 weeks' paid holiday a year, plus daily and weekly rest and rest breaks.
- The National Living Wage is £12.71 an hour for workers aged 21 and over from 1 April 2026, with lower rates for younger workers and apprentices.
- Statutory sick pay has been payable from the first day of sickness, with no lower earnings limit, since 6 April 2026.

**Family rights** include up to 52 weeks' maternity leave (with up to 39 weeks of statutory pay), paternity leave and unpaid parental leave as day-one rights since April 2026, shared parental leave, neonatal care leave, carer's leave, and a day-one right to request flexible working. From October 2027 employers will have to show that refusing a flexible-working request is reasonable.

## The Employment Rights Act 2025 timeline

The Employment Rights Act 2025 received Royal Assent in December 2025 and is being brought in over 2026 and 2027. A CIPD assignment must say which measures were in force on the date it describes. The Bill first proposed day-one unfair-dismissal rights with a statutory probation period; the law as passed sets a six-month qualifying period with uncapped compensation instead, so older study notes may describe the abandoned version.

## US: employment at will, limited by federal law

Most US employment is **at will**: either side can end it at any time, for any lawful reason or none, without notice or severance. 'Lawful' does a lot of work. Federal law forbids decisions based on protected characteristics or made in retaliation, and many states and contracts add more. Coverage often depends on the employer's size.

Cases SHRM candidates meet most often:

- *Griggs v. Duke Power* (1971): disparate impact.
- *McDonnell Douglas v. Green* (1973): the burden-shifting framework.
- *Meritor Savings Bank v. Vinson* (1986) and *Harris v. Forklift Systems* (1993): hostile-environment harassment must be 'severe or pervasive'.
- *Faragher* and *Ellerth* (1998): the employer's defence to supervisor harassment.
- *Bostock v. Clayton County* (2020): sexual orientation and gender identity are covered by Title VII.
- *Groff v. DeJoy* (2023): refusing a religious accommodation needs a substantial cost, not a trivial one.

**Recent shifts.** Since 2025 federal agencies have moved away from disparate-impact enforcement and ended race- and sex-based affirmative-action plans for federal contractors, and a court struck down the 2024 overtime rule, so the $684-a-week salary threshold applies. In July 2026 the EEOC rescinded its 1979 affirmative-action guidelines and announced plans to repeal the Uniform Guidelines that contain the 'four-fifths' adverse-impact rule. New items reach the SHRM exam only after field-testing, so learn the established rule and the direction of change.

## Where experts disagree

- **How deferential should tribunals be?** The band of reasonable responses lets an employer win if a reasonable employer could have dismissed. Critics say it protects harsh but common practice; defenders say tribunals shouldn't run businesses.
- **Six months or day one?** The government dropped its first plan, day-one rights with statutory probation, for a six-month period. Unions saw a lost opportunity; employer bodies argued day-one rights would deter hiring. Uncapped compensation shifts the risk back towards employers.
- **At will or just cause?** Most developed systems require a reason for dismissal, and the US is the outlier. Its defenders point to a flexible labour market; critics point to insecurity and to litigation standing in for regulation.
- **Disparate impact under pressure.** The federal retreat from disparate-impact enforcement leaves the doctrine in the statute but weakens its use; private claims and state law may fill the gap.

For a CIPD answer, state the law accurately as at your date, then evaluate it for fairness, efficiency and voice. For SHRM, pick the option that follows the established rule and a fair process.

## In India

- **No general unfair-dismissal law for managers.** Protection is strongest for 'workers' under the IR Code and standing orders; others depend on their contract and, in some states, the Shops Act. For workers, reinstatement with back wages is the normal remedy for wrongful termination. See [termination, grievances and disputes](/handbooks/labour-law/termination-grievance-and-disputes/).
- **Discrimination law is patchier.** India has no single statute like the Equality Act. Protection comes from the Code on Wages (equal pay, including for transgender persons), the POSH Act, the RPwD Act and the Transgender Persons Act, so many employers adopt a wider standard by policy. See [Indian labour law](/learn/indian-labour-law/).
- **Host-country law applies.** Staff in the UK and US are protected by local law whatever the parent company's policy, and Indian law protects staff working in India. Write one set of principles and a legal annex for each country.
- **For SHRM candidates in India,** US-law items may not appear in your exam (confirm for your window), but US cases still shape the judgement items on fairness, documentation and retaliation.
- **For CIPD candidates in India,** the law criteria are framed around UK law. Ask your centre how far you may use Indian examples alongside it.
