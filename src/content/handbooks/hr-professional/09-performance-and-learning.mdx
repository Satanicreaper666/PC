The annual appraisal is one of the least loved processes in HR. Few companies can simply abolish it, because pay and promotion decisions still need an input. The answer is a system that is continuous, fair and actually helps people improve.

## Why annual appraisals disappoint

- **Recency bias.** Managers remember the last two months, not the whole year.
- **Forced distribution in small teams.** Requiring a fixed share of "below expectations" ratings assumes every team's performance follows the same curve. In a team of eight strong performers, it forces an unfair result.
- **Feedback too late.** Hearing about a problem once a year is like checking your bank balance once a year: by then it is too late to fix.
- **Tied to pay.** When one meeting decides both development and increment, people defend their rating instead of discussing what to improve.

Calibration is where fairness is won or lost. Managers present their ratings to each other with evidence, and HR challenges patterns: one manager rating everyone highly, or women rated lower than men doing similar work. The [annual performance appraisal form](/toolkit/annual-performance-appraisal/) gives you the record for the year.

<Callout type="tip" title="Separate development from pay">
If you revise pay in April, at the start of the financial year, hold development conversations a few months earlier and take the pay decision separately, using performance as one input alongside budget and market data. People talk more honestly about what they need to learn when their increment isn't being decided in the same meeting. The [increment budget allocator](/toolkit/increment-budget-allocator/) helps with the pay step.
</Callout>

## When is an improvement plan fair?

A performance improvement plan (PIP) should give someone a real chance to succeed. Used as paperwork for an exit already decided, it is unfair to the person and weak evidence if the exit is challenged.

<Callout type="warning" title="Check the legal route before a plan ends in separation">
For employees who are "workers" under the Industrial Relations Code, ending employment for any reason other than punishment through disciplinary action can count as retrenchment. For a worker with at least a year's continuous service, retrenchment requires a month's written notice (or pay in lieu) and compensation. Contracts, standing orders and state laws may add requirements. Take advice before the final meeting, and read the [termination chapter](/handbooks/labour-law/termination-grievance-and-disputes/).
</Callout>

## Succession and the 9-box grid

Once a year, review everyone in manager roles and above. Who is ready for the next level now? Who needs one or two years of development? If a critical person left tomorrow, who would step in?

Two cautions. Potential ratings are subjective, so ask managers for evidence and look for bias, for example against people on flexible schedules or recently back from leave. And don't tell people their box as if it were permanent; talk about their next development step instead. The [9-box talent mapper](/toolkit/nine-box-talent-mapper/) helps you run the review.

## Learning that changes how people work

The Center for Creative Leadership describes the 70-20-10 rule as a guideline from its long-running research on how executives learn: people tend to learn about 70% from challenging experiences, 20% from other people and 10% from formal training. Treat it as a rule of thumb, not a precise ratio.

### Is the training working?

The Kirkpatrick Model judges training at four levels. For any significant programme, aim to measure at least level 3: whether people actually work differently.

| Level | Question | How to check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Reaction | Did participants find it engaging and relevant? | A short feedback form at the end |
| 2. Learning | Did they gain the knowledge, skills and confidence intended? | A test, a demonstration or a practice task |
| 3. Behaviour | Are they using it at work? | Manager observation and examples 60 to 90 days later |
| 4. Results | Did the business outcome improve? | The metric the programme was meant to move |

<Callout type="tip" title="Try this now">
Look at last year's L&D spend. List the three largest programmes and, for each, write down the behaviour it was meant to change. If you can't name one, or nobody checked, that is where to start.
</Callout>
