SHRM defines communication as crafting "concise and informative communications", listening to "the concerns of others" and translating information between levels of the organisation. CIPD's Professional courage and influence behaviour adds "showing courage to speak up and skilfully influencing others to gain buy in". In daily HR work, that means you can deliver bad news with care, persuade people who don't report to you, and write a policy anyone can follow.

## A structure for hard conversations

The conversations HR dreads most share a structure. Prepare it in advance, and you will be calmer and kinder in the room.

### Delivering an improvement plan

> **HR:** "Thank you for meeting. This conversation is about your performance over the last three months. I want to be direct so we can work on it together."
>
> **HR:** "Here is what we have seen: [specific examples with dates]. These fall short of what the role needs in [specific areas]."
>
> **HR:** "We are putting a performance improvement plan in place for the next [30 to 60] days. Here are the goals, which are specific and measurable. Your manager and I will meet you every week to review progress and the support you need."
>
> **HR:** "I want to be clear about what happens if the goals aren't met by [date]: the next step could be a change of role or ending your employment. I would much rather you succeed."

An improvement plan must be a genuine chance to improve, not paperwork for a decision already made. The [performance chapter](/handbooks/hr-professional/performance-and-learning/) explains when a plan is fair, and the [improvement plan template](/toolkit/performance-improvement-plan/) gives you the document.

### Communicating a pay freeze

> **HR:** "I want you to hear this from us before you hear it elsewhere. Because of [the specific business reason], there will be no salary increases this cycle. That applies at every level, including leadership."
>
> **HR:** "This is not a judgement on anyone's performance. Here is what we are doing: [retention bonuses for critical roles, a review date, or other steps leadership has actually approved]."
>
> **HR:** "I know this is disappointing. I would rather be honest now than promise something we can't deliver. Ask me anything, here or privately."

### Ending someone's employment

> **HR:** "[Name], I will be direct. After [the improvement plan or the restructuring decision], we have decided to end your employment, effective [date]."
>
> **HR:** "Here is what happens next, and you have it in writing: your last working day, notice pay, your full and final settlement and when it will be paid, insurance, and your relieving and experience letters."
>
> **HR:** "Thank you for your work, especially [a specific, genuine example]. This decision doesn't erase that."
>
> **HR:** "Take the time you need today. [Colleague] will help with the handover and logistics, and you can contact me with any questions."

<Callout type="law" title="Final dues have a deadline">
Under the Code on Wages, 2019 (section 17(2)), wages due to an employee who is removed, dismissed or retrenched, who resigns, or who loses the job because the establishment closes must be paid within two working days. Other dues, such as gratuity, have their own timelines. Use the [full and final settlement calculator](/toolkit/full-and-final-settlement-calculator/) and read the [termination chapter](/handbooks/labour-law/termination-grievance-and-disputes/) before you set the date.
</Callout>

### Rules for every hard conversation

1. Never give bad news in a group or by surprise in a public place.
2. Have the documents ready before you start.
3. Be direct. Don't hide the message in corporate language.
4. Let people react emotionally without becoming defensive.
5. Don't make promises you can't keep, such as "I'm sure you'll find something better".
6. Confirm the key points in writing within 24 hours.

## Influencing without authority

HR rarely gets to mandate. Most of the time you need managers to choose to do something differently, such as hold regular one-to-ones, hire more carefully or address a team problem.

Evidence is most persuasive when it comes from your own organisation. "In our teams, those whose managers held monthly one-to-ones lost fewer people last year" is more persuasive than any outside study, because the manager can't dismiss it as someone else's company. Check that the comparison is fair before you use it; the [analytics chapter](/handbooks/hr-professional/people-analytics-and-data-literacy/) lists the traps.

## Writing policies people understand

Every policy should pass a simple test: can its core be explained in five plain sentences? Who it covers, what they get, how to use it, who decides, and where to check. If not, the problem is usually the policy itself.

The numbers in a leave policy must meet the law. Under the OSH Code, "workers" who work at least 180 days in a calendar year earn annual leave at one day for every 20 days worked, and state Shops and Establishments Acts add their own rules. Read [leave and working hours](/handbooks/labour-law/leave-and-working-hours/), or start from the [leave policy template](/toolkit/leave-policy/).

<Callout type="tip" title="Roll out policies in conversation">
Email alone doesn't change behaviour. Explain any new or changed policy to managers first, give them two or three likely questions with answers, and let them explain it to their teams.
</Callout>

## Matching the message to the audience

| Audience | What they care about | How to frame it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Founders, CEO and board | Growth, risk, culture at scale | Three points, the cost or value in rupees, one decision needed |
| Finance | Cost, return, compliance | A budget line, the expected return and the risk of not acting |
| Line managers | Getting work done with a stable team | Their team's problem, a practical tool, how much time it takes |
| Employees | Fairness, pay, growth, respect | What changes for them, why, when, and who to ask |
