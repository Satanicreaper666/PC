HR sits where power, vulnerability and trust meet. You know things about people that their families may not. You will be asked to carry out decisions you disagree with, and to represent the company to employees and employees to the company, often in the same week. This chapter is about handling that tension with integrity.

## What the frameworks say

All three frameworks treat ethics as foundational, though they describe it differently.

- **SHRM** defines Ethical Practice as the knowledge, skills and abilities "needed to maintain high levels of personal and professional integrity, and to act as an ethical agent who promotes core values, integrity and accountability throughout the organization."
- **CIPD** lists Ethical practice as a core behaviour ("building trust by role-modelling ethical behaviour, and applying principles and values consistently in decision-making") alongside Professional courage and influence ("showing courage to speak up and skilfully influencing others to gain buy in").
- **AIHR** places ethics inside People Advocacy, in a dimension it calls Ethical and Risk Ownership.

The common thread is that ethics is a behaviour you show in decisions, not a policy you sign once a year.

## A five-question test for grey-zone decisions

Most ethical problems in HR are not clear-cut. The law allows the decision, but something about it feels wrong. Work through these questions in order before you act.

  <DecisionTree

The last question matters more than it looks. If you can't explain a decision to the person it affects, it usually isn't well reasoned, and if you wouldn't want it read out in a town hall or posted online, the company shouldn't be doing it.

## Four dilemmas, worked through

These come up in Indian workplaces every year. The responses below are a sensible approach, not the only one; your facts, contracts and policies may point elsewhere.

### The CEO wants someone let go "to make an example"

The employee is performing adequately. The CEO is frustrated with the team and wants a visible signal.

**A better response:** push back privately, and redirect to the behaviour the CEO actually wants to change.

> "I understand you want to set a standard. Letting go someone who is meeting expectations will worry the whole team and could be challenged. What behaviour do you want to see change? I can put a clear plan in place this week."

If the CEO still wants to proceed, check the appointment letter and, for employees who are "workers" under the Industrial Relations Code, whether ending the job would count as retrenchment, which has its own notice and compensation rules. Record your advice in a short email. If the decision is lawful and stands, carry it out with dignity: a private conversation, clear reasons, every rupee owed paid on time.

### An employee reports harassment but asks you to do nothing

She tells you her manager makes inappropriate comments. She asks you not to take it further; she will "handle it herself".

**A better response:** listen fully, take it seriously, and don't promise silence you may not be able to keep.

- Thank her for telling you, and ask what she needs right now.
- Explain her options: a complaint to the Internal Committee (IC) under the Sexual Harassment of Women at Workplace (Prevention, Prohibition and Redressal) Act, 2013 (the POSH Act), how that process protects confidentiality, and what interim support is possible.
- Explain honestly what your company's policy requires HR to do once it is told, and that retaliation against her is not acceptable.
- Write down what she told you, with the date, and keep it confidential.
- If your policy requires you to escalate, or you believe others are at risk, tell her what you will do and why before you do it.

<Callout type="warning">
Don't try to settle the matter yourself between the two people, and don't ask the manager about it informally. A private fix outside the IC can leave her unprotected and the company exposed. For how the IC process works, read the [POSH chapter of the labour law handbook](/handbooks/labour-law/posh-compliance/).
</Callout>

### The founder's relative is failing in the role

Everyone knows. Nobody says anything. The founder asks you to "find him a good role".

**A better response:** have a private conversation with the founder that is based on evidence, and frame it as helping the relative rather than criticising the founder.

> "Here is how his results compare with others in similar roles. The team sees it, and it is affecting morale and your credibility with them. I can set up a development plan with clear milestones, or we can look for a role that genuinely fits his strengths. Keeping him where he is struggling isn't fair to him or to the team."

### A layoff list that targets people on medical leave

During a cost cut, the CFO suggests starting with employees on long medical leave because they "aren't contributing anyway".

**A better response:** say clearly that selection must be based on roles and objective criteria, not on someone's health.

> "Choosing people because they are on medical leave is unfair, it will be seen as unfair by everyone who stays, and it is very likely to be challenged. Let me draft criteria based on the roles we no longer need, and we can review them together."

For "workers" under the Industrial Relations Code, retrenchment within a category ordinarily follows "last come, first go" unless an agreement says otherwise, and the employer must record its reasons if it departs from that order. Put your advice in writing, and if you are overruled on something you believe is unlawful, escalate to legal counsel or the board. The [crisis playbook](/handbooks/hr-professional/the-hr-crisis-playbook/) covers how to run a layoff lawfully and humanely.

## How to push back on a senior leader

Professional courage is not confrontation. It is raising the right concern, at the right level, in a way that leaves room for a better decision.

  <Flow
    steps={[

A short, factual email protects everyone: "Following our conversation, I want to record my advice that… The risks I see are… I recommend…". Keep it calm and specific. An email written in anger will be read by people who weren't in the room.

## The confidentiality spectrum

HR's value rests on trust. One leaked salary, one casual remark about someone's improvement plan or one mention of a medical condition can undo years of credibility. Treat every piece of employee information the way you would want your own medical records treated.

| Category | Examples | What to do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Act on it, carefully | Harassment, fraud, safety risks, risk of harm to someone | Don't promise secrecy. Follow the law and your policy, and tell the person what you will do. |
| Need to know only | Salary, medical records, improvement plans, disciplinary records, bank and ID details | Share only with people who need it for their job, and only the part they need. |
| Confidential by default | Resignation plans, family or health worries, conflicts with colleagues | Keep it to yourself unless someone is at risk; if you must act, tell the person first where you can. |
| Never share | Feedback on other candidates, one person's pay with another, board discussions about individuals | Don't share, even informally or "off the record". |

<Callout type="tip" title="Try this now">
A manager asks for the salary of an employee in another team "to benchmark a new hire". Give the salary band for that role and level, not the individual's pay. The manager gets what they need, and nobody's privacy is breached.
</Callout>
