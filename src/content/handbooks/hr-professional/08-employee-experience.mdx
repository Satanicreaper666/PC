AIHR calls this competency People Advocacy, CIPD's Profession Map includes Valuing people as a core behaviour and Employee experience as a specialist area, and SHRM covers Employee Engagement & Retention as a functional area. In practice it means designing each stage of working life so that people feel respected, challenged and heard.

## The employee lifecycle is a loop

Most companies put their energy into hiring and very little into development, retention and exit. But the stages connect: a careless exit becomes a story told to friends and posted online, and it affects who applies next.

### Moments that matter

| Stage | Moment | What good looks like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hire | The offer | A clear offer letter, explained, with in-hand pay shown, not only cost to company (CTC) |
| Onboard | Day one | Laptop, access and ID ready; the manager is there; lunch with the team |
| Develop | The first development talk | A named next step, not a vague promise |
| Perform | The first review | No surprises: nothing in it the person hasn't heard before |
| Retain | A personal crisis | Flexible leave and a manager who checks in |
| Exit | The last day | Relieving letter ready, dues paid on time, a genuine thank-you |

For the first and last stages, use the [onboarding checklist](/toolkit/onboarding-checklist/) and the [offboarding checklist](/toolkit/offboarding-checklist/).

## Culture as a system

Culture isn't the values painted on the wall. It is what people learn from watching what gets rewarded, what gets tolerated and what gets punished.

<Figure title="What the poster says, and what people learn" caption="Example only. When the two columns disagree, people believe the right-hand column." wide>
  <Compare
    columns={["What the poster says", "What people learn from what happens"]}
    highlight={1}
    rows={[
      { label: "Rewarded", values: ["“We value teamwork.”", "The manager who hit targets by burning out the team got promoted. Results matter, at any cost."] },
      { label: "Tolerated", values: ["“Respect for everyone.”", "A top performer shouts at juniors and nothing happens. Performance excuses behaviour."] },
      { label: "Punished", values: ["“We value transparency.”", "The person who raised a concern is now called a troublemaker. Keep your head down."] }
    ]}
  />

<Callout type="tip" title="A culture check you can run this week">
Ask five employees from different teams and levels three questions: "What kind of person gets promoted here?", "What behaviour do people get away with?" and "What happens when someone disagrees with their manager?". Their answers describe your real culture. Share the themes, not the names, with leadership.
</Callout>

## Engagement surveys that lead to action

A long annual survey whose results arrive months later, get presented to leadership and then disappear is worse than no survey. It teaches people that nobody listens, and next year fewer people answer.

### What to ask about

Good engagement questions cover a few themes, each with one or two statements people rate from "strongly disagree" to "strongly agree":

- **Clarity:** clear goals, and the tools and information to meet them.
- **Recognition:** whether good work gets noticed, and how recently.
- **Growth:** support to develop, and chances to learn new skills.
- **Manager:** a manager who takes a genuine interest in them.
- **Voice:** being listened to, and feeling safe to disagree.
- **Purpose and team:** seeing why their work matters, and a team that holds high standards.

Write your own questions or license an established survey; some well-known question sets are copyrighted. The [engagement pulse survey](/toolkit/engagement-pulse-survey/) in the toolkit is free to use and scores itself, and the [analytics chapter](/handbooks/hr-professional/people-analytics-and-data-literacy/) explains the employee net promoter score (eNPS).

<Callout type="warning" title="Protect anonymity">
Don't show results for groups smaller than five people, and don't try to work out who wrote a comment. Once people suspect a survey isn't anonymous, honest answers stop.
</Callout>

## Stay interviews

Exit interviews tell you why someone left after they decided. Stay interviews ask the same questions while you can still act. Managers should run them, because the employee's working relationship is with the manager, not with HR.

<Figure title="Five stay-interview questions" caption="Ask once or twice a year, and after any big change, starting with the people whose exit would hurt most. Listen more than you talk, and agree one follow-up.">
  <Steps
    items={[
      { title: "What do you look forward to at work?", body: "Find what to protect." },
      { title: "What are you learning, and what do you want to learn?", body: "Find the next growth step." },
      { title: "What frustrates you that we could fix?", body: "Find the irritants that build up over months." },
      { title: "Do you feel fairly paid, and is anything else worrying you?", body: "Find pay and non-pay concerns early." },
      { title: "What might make you leave, and what would keep you here?", body: "Ask directly. Most people answer honestly when they trust the manager." }
    ]}
  />
</Figure>
