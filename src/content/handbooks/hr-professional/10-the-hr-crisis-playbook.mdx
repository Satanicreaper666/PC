Sooner or later you will face a crisis: a harassment complaint against a senior leader, a layoff with little time to plan it, a storm of online reviews, a leader who walks out, or an employee in distress. What you do in the first day shapes what happens for months.

## A harassment complaint

The Sexual Harassment of Women at Workplace (Prevention, Prohibition and Redressal) Act, 2013 (the POSH Act) sets up an Internal Committee to inquire into complaints. HR's job is not to investigate or decide. It is to make the process work: receive the complaint with respect, route it, protect everyone's confidentiality, prevent retaliation and carry out the outcome.

The Act sets out who must sit on the Internal Committee and the time limits for complaints, inquiries and the employer's action. Read the [POSH chapter](/handbooks/labour-law/posh-compliance/) for the exact requirements, check your committee with the [IC constitution checklist](/toolkit/posh-ic-constitution-checklist/), and use the [POSH complaint form](/toolkit/posh-complaint-form/).

<Callout type="warning" title="When the complaint is against a senior leader">
Make sure nobody on the committee reports to, or is close to, the person accused; anyone with a conflict should step aside. Consider interim steps to protect the complainant, such as changing reporting lines, with her agreement and the committee's advice. Brief the board or owners in confidence, without names unless they need them.
</Callout>

## Layoffs: lawful and humane

A layoff ends livelihoods. Do it rarely, plan it carefully, and treat every person with dignity. Start with the law, because the rules depend on who the employees are and how large the establishment is.

### Before: criteria and plan

- **Choose roles, not people.** Base selection on objective, documented criteria: roles no longer needed, a closed unit or location, or a fair and recorded method. For "workers", retrenchment within a category ordinarily follows "last come, first go" unless an agreement says otherwise, and you must record your reasons if you depart from it.
- **Never select on personal grounds.** Age, gender, pregnancy, health, medical leave, caste, religion, union activity or a personal grudge must play no part. Several of these are also protected by specific laws.
- **Budget beyond the minimum.** Where you can, add severance above the legal minimum, extend health cover for a period, and offer outplacement help.

### On the day: one-to-one, in person

Hold individual conversations, not a group announcement or an email. State the decision clearly, and hand over the details in writing: last working day, notice pay, retrenchment compensation if due, the full and final settlement date, insurance, and relieving and experience letters. The [communication chapter](/handbooks/hr-professional/communication-and-influence/) has a script, and the [full and final settlement calculator](/toolkit/full-and-final-settlement-calculator/) works out the dues.

### After: the people who stay

Within a day, the leadership team should explain to everyone what happened, why, and what it means for those who remain. Managers should then meet each team member individually. Watch engagement and resignations closely for the next three months; people who stay often feel guilt, fear or both.

## A social media storm

When a negative review or post about your company spreads, the instinct is to fight it. Don't.

- **Don't** delete, threaten, demand the post be taken down, post fake positive reviews or try to find out who wrote an anonymous review.
- **Do** reply within a day or two, calmly and briefly. Acknowledge the concern, say what you are doing about it only if that is true, and invite the person to talk privately.

> "Thank you for sharing your experience. We take this seriously and are working on [the specific area]. We would welcome the chance to discuss it with you directly. Please contact [HR contact]."

Then fix the underlying problem. If several reviews say the same thing, they are telling you something your surveys may have missed. Invite all employees, not only those you expect to be positive, to share honest reviews, and never offer rewards or apply pressure.

## When an employee is in distress

An employee may tell you, or a colleague may report, that someone is in crisis or talking about harming themselves. You are not expected to be a counsellor. You are expected to take it seriously and get help.

- **If there is immediate danger,** call 112, India's emergency number, and don't leave the person alone.
- **Listen calmly.** Ask directly and kindly how they are and whether they are thinking of harming themselves. Don't argue, judge or promise to keep it secret.
- **Connect them to help:** their family or emergency contact with their agreement, a doctor, your employee assistance programme if you have one, or the government's [Tele-MANAS](https://telemanas.mohfw.gov.in/) mental health helpline.
- **Keep it confidential.** Share only what others need to know to keep the person safe. Don't discuss it with the team.
- **Plan the return.** Leave, flexible hours or a lighter workload for a period, agreed with the person and their doctor.
- **Look after the people around them,** including yourself. These conversations are hard.

## Get ready before it happens

- A contact list: leadership, legal adviser, Internal Committee members, insurer, and local police and hospital numbers for each office.
- Drafted templates for the common crises, reviewed once a year.
- A constituted, trained Internal Committee in every office that needs one.
- Up-to-date statutory compliance, so a crisis doesn't uncover a second problem. Use the [HR compliance audit checklist](/toolkit/hr-compliance-audit-checklist/) and the [labour law compliance checklists](/handbooks/labour-law/compliance-checklists/).
- A habit of reviewing every crisis afterwards and updating this playbook.
