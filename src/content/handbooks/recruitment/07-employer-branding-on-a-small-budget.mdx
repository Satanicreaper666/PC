You don't need a big branding budget. Candidates judge you on what they can see: how your people talk about their work, how you answer reviews and how you treat applicants, especially the ones you turn down.

## Write an EVP your team recognises

Your employee value proposition (EVP) answers one question: why should a capable person join you rather than any of the other employers hiring for the same role? Fill in each line with something specific and true.

| Area | Question to answer | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pay | How do you pay compared with the market, and how is it reviewed? | “Pay ranges in every job post; reviewed every April” |
| Growth | What will they learn or become? | “Two people moved from individual roles to team lead in the last year” |
| Work | What is interesting about the work itself? | “You'll build the first version of the product, not maintain an old one” |
| Culture | How do you work day to day? | “Decisions written down; no meetings on Wednesday afternoons” |
| People | Who will they work with and learn from? | “A small team; you'll work directly with the founders” |
| Flexibility | Where and when do people work? | “Hybrid, three office days; core hours 11 to 4” |

Test the draft on five employees at different levels. If they don't recognise it, fix the company or fix the EVP before it goes on your careers page.

## Show the work, not slogans

Five things cost little more than time:

1. **A plain careers page.** List open roles with pay ranges, three honest reasons to join, how your hiring process works and how long each step takes, a privacy notice for applicants' data and an equal opportunity statement. Make it readable on a phone and with a screen reader.
2. **People talking about their own work.** Invite employees who want to post about what they're building or learning to do so, with simple guidance on what's confidential. Keep it voluntary; never make it a target or a condition.
3. **Replies to reviews.** Respond to reviews on sites such as Glassdoor and AmbitionBox, good and bad, using the approach below.
4. **A good experience for every candidate,** including the ones you reject.
5. **An alumni network.** People who left on good terms refer candidates and sometimes return.

## Make candidate experience your brand

Candidates remember how you made them feel long after they forget the job. The basics cost nothing.

### A rejection note that builds goodwill

> Hi [name],
>
> Thank you for the time you put into our process for [role]. We were impressed by [something specific, such as your analysis of the case study]. We've decided to go ahead with a candidate who has more experience in [specific requirement from the job description].
>
> If you're happy for us to, we'd like to keep your details for six months and get in touch if a suitable role opens. Just reply to let us know.
>
> [Name, role]

Keep the reason tied to the job's requirements. Don't compare the person with the candidate you chose, and don't mention anything personal. Keep the data only if they agree, for the period you stated.

<Callout type="warning" title="Lines you never cross">
Never try to identify a reviewer, comment on their performance or reveal anything about their employment. Never pressure or reward employees for posting reviews, and never write or buy reviews. If a review contains confidential information or a threat, use the site's reporting process.
</Callout>

## Collect data responsibly

Careers pages, application forms and talent pools all collect personal data. Publish a short privacy notice, keep only what you need and delete it when you said you would. From May 2027, when the main duties of the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 apply, notice and erasure become legal duties. The [screening chapter](/handbooks/recruitment/screening-and-shortlisting/#handle-candidate-data-with-care) explains what to collect at each stage.

Track a handful of simple signs: how many applicants came through referrals or your careers page, your offer acceptance rate, how quickly you reply to applicants and reviews, and a one-question survey of candidates after interviews (“How fairly were you treated?”).
