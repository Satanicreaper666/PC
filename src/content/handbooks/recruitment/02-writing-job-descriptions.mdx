A job description (JD) is your first filter. A clear one attracts people who can do the work and helps the rest decide quickly that it isn't for them. A vague one fills your inbox with applications you can't compare.

## Build the JD in six parts

Write the parts in this order. Each answers a question a good candidate is asking.

<Figure title="The six parts of a job description" caption="Read top to bottom: this is the order the parts appear in the post.">
  <Steps
    items={[
      { title: "Why the role matters", body: "Two or three lines on what the person will achieve. “You'll build our customer support team from scratch and take it to 500 tickets a day”, not “we are looking for a dynamic individual”." },
      { title: "What you'll do", body: "Five to seven bullets, each a real deliverable starting with a verb: build, own, run, ship, fix. Avoid “responsible for” and “duties include”." },
      { title: "What you need", body: "Three to five must-haves needed in the first months. Say what they should have done, not how many years they spent: “has managed a team of five” beats “5+ years”." },
      { title: "Nice to have", body: "Two or three extras, clearly labelled, so people who meet the must-haves still apply." },
      { title: "Pay, place and terms", body: "The pay range, location and work mode, employment type (permanent or fixed-term with an end date) and who the role reports to." },
      { title: "How to apply", body: "One clear step, what happens next and when you'll reply. A short note on why they're interested is a useful filter." }
    ]}
  />
</Figure>

<Callout type="tip" title="Three questions before you post">
Can you say what this person will have achieved in six months? How will you measure it? Why would a good candidate leave their current job for this one? If you can't answer all three, rewrite the JD.
</Callout>

## Keep it fair and lawful

A JD should describe the work, never the kind of person you picture doing it. Indian law prohibits discrimination in recruitment on several grounds, and the rest are simply not job-related.

<Callout type="law" title="What the law says about recruitment">
- **Sex and gender:** the Code on Wages, 2019 bars discrimination on the ground of sex while recruiting for the same or similar work, and in conditions of employment, except where a law prohibits or restricts women's employment in that work. It replaced the Equal Remuneration Act, 1976 from 21 November 2025, and the government says its gender protections include transgender persons.
- **Transgender persons:** the Transgender Persons (Protection of Rights) Act, 2019 says no establishment, public or private, may discriminate against a transgender person in any matter relating to employment, including recruitment.
- **Disability:** under the Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act, 2016, denying reasonable accommodation counts as discrimination, and every establishment must publish an equal opportunity policy. Establishments with 20 or more employees must also explain how candidates with disabilities are selected and name a liaison officer for their recruitment.
</Callout>

| Avoid | Why | Write instead |
| --- | --- | --- |
| “Male candidates only”, “female preferred” | Discrimination on the ground of sex in recruitment | Describe the work; the role is open to everyone |
| “Age below 30”, “young and energetic” | Age is not a skill and shuts out capable people | State the real demand: “travels 10 days a month” |
| “Unmarried candidates preferred” | Aimed mostly at women; evidence of sex discrimination | Leave it out |
| “Candidates from [community, religion or state] only” | Not job-related and invites bias | State the skill: “reads and writes Kannada fluently” |
| “Rockstar”, “ninja”, “aggressive” | Says nothing about the job and can put people off | Name the skill: “closes enterprise deals” |
| “Physically fit” for a desk job | Can exclude persons with disabilities for no reason | List the essential tasks and offer adjustments |
| “Only from top colleges” | Pedigree stands in for skill | “We assess everyone with a short exercise” |

If a role genuinely needs something, such as night shifts, frequent travel, lifting or a language, state the requirement itself and apply it to every applicant.

### Add an equal opportunity line

Close every JD with a short statement you mean, for example: “We welcome applicants of every gender, age, background and ability. If you need any adjustment to apply or interview, tell us and we'll arrange it.” Then make sure your [screening](/handbooks/recruitment/screening-and-shortlisting/) and [interviews](/handbooks/recruitment/structured-interviews/) live up to it.

## Match the JD to the level

| Level | Focus on | Length | Tone |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Intern or entry | What they'll learn, who will guide them, the project scope | 300 to 400 words | Friendly and clear |
| Mid-level | What they'll own, the team, how success is measured | 400 to 500 words | Direct and outcome-focused |
| Senior or lead | Scope, team size, decisions they'll make | 500 to 600 words | Peer to peer |
| Director or C-level | The business problem, reporting line, equity if any | 600 to 800 words | Confidential and specific |

## Check before you publish

- The title is what people would search for, not an internal grade.
- The first three lines say what the person will achieve.
- There are no more than five must-haves, and each passed the test above.
- The pay range, location, work mode and employment type are stated.
- Nothing refers to gender, age, marital status, religion, caste, appearance or family.
- There's an equal opportunity line and a way to ask for adjustments.
- The post says how you'll use applicants' data and when you'll reply (see [handling candidate data](/handbooks/recruitment/screening-and-shortlisting/#handle-candidate-data-with-care)).
