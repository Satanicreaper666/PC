An unstructured chat tells you whether you liked someone. A structured interview tells you whether they can do the job. When every candidate answers the same job-related questions and every answer is scored against the same guide, you compare like with like, you can explain your decision, and personal bias has less room to work.

## Design the interview before you meet anyone

## Ask for evidence with STAR

Behavioural questions work best when the answer covers four parts, known as STAR: situation, task, action and result. Teach interviewers to listen for each one and to probe for whatever is missing.

| Letter | Means | Listen for | Probe if you hear |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| S | Situation | Where, when and what was happening | “Sometimes at my previous company…” |
| T | Task | What they were personally responsible for | “We did…” (ask: what was your part?) |
| A | Action | The steps they took and the decisions they made | Vague generalities or passive voice |
| R | Result | What changed, with numbers where possible | “It went well”, with nothing specific |

<Callout type="tip" title="Two answers to the same question">
“Tell me about a time you handled a difficult client.”

**Weak (scores 1):** “We had an unhappy client. The team worked on it and resolved the issue, and the client was happy after that.” There is no situation, no personal action and no result.

**Strong (scores 5):** “Our largest client threatened to leave after we missed a delivery date. I called their head of operations the same day, agreed a revised plan with a service credit and set up a weekly status call. We kept the account, and they renewed for a larger contract six months later.”
</Callout>

## Score every answer the same way

Each interviewer scores each competency on the same five-point scale. The [interview evaluation form](/toolkit/interview-evaluation-form/) uses the same scale.

| Score | Meaning | What it sounds like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | No evidence | Couldn't answer, or the answer missed the point |
| 2 | Below the bar | Some relevant points, but gaps or little depth |
| 3 | Meets the bar | Covers the key points; would do the job competently |
| 4 | Above the bar | Specific and thoughtful; goes beyond the question |
| 5 | Exceptional | Clear, specific and insightful; exactly what you'd hope to hear |

### A question bank to start from

| Competency | Main question | Follow-up |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Problem-solving | “Tell me about the hardest problem you solved at work in the last year.” | “What other options did you consider, and why did you choose this one?” |
| Ownership | “Describe a time something went wrong on your watch. What did you do?” | “What would you do differently now?” |
| Teamwork | “Tell me about a time you disagreed with your manager. How did it end?” | “Did you convince them, or did you commit to their approach?” |
| Learning | “What have you taught yourself recently that helped you at work?” | “How did you apply it?” |
| Communication | “Explain [a concept from their field] to me as if I'm new to it.” | Listen for clarity, structure and examples |
| Leadership | “Describe a time you led a team through a difficult period.” | “How did you support the person who was struggling most?” |
| Customer focus | “Tell me about a time you went beyond what a customer or colleague expected.” | “What difference did it make to them?” |

<Callout type="warning" title="Define “culture fit” or drop it">
“Culture fit” scored on instinct usually means “like us”, which shuts out people with different backgrounds. If values matter, name the behaviours (for example “gives credit to others” or “raises problems early”), write a question for each and score them like any other competency.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Score this answer">
You ask: “Tell me about a time you improved a process.” The candidate says: “Onboarding took two weeks. I mapped every step, removed three duplicate approvals, wrote a day-one checklist and got it down to four days. Thirty new joiners went through the new process last quarter.” This scores 5: a specific situation, clear personal actions, a measured result and scale.
</Callout>

## Questions you must never ask

Ask only what helps you judge whether the person can do the job. Indian law bars discrimination in recruitment on several grounds, and the questions below either touch those grounds or have nothing to do with the work.

<Callout type="law" title="What the law says">
The Code on Wages, 2019 bars discrimination on the ground of sex while recruiting for the same or similar work, and the government says its gender protections include transgender persons. The Transgender Persons (Protection of Rights) Act, 2019 bars any establishment from discriminating against a transgender person in recruitment. Under the Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act, 2016, denying reasonable accommodation counts as discrimination.
</Callout>

| Don't ask | Why | If you need to know something, ask this instead |
| --- | --- | --- |
| “Are you married?” “Are you planning children?” | Asked almost only of women; rejecting on this basis is discrimination on the ground of sex | Ask everyone the same question about the real requirement: “The role needs travel two weeks a month. Can you do that?” |
| “Will you be able to manage work after marriage?” | The same problem, often aimed at women | Describe the hours and travel and ask if they work for the person |
| “How old are you?” | Age is not a skill | Confirm only any minimum age the law sets for the work |
| “What is your religion, caste or community?” “Where is your family from?” | Not job-related; invites bias | Nothing. It has no bearing on the job |
| “What does your father or husband do?” | Not job-related; often signals stereotypes | Nothing |
| “Are you transgender?” “What's your real name?” | Discrimination against transgender persons in recruitment is prohibited | Use the name and pronouns the candidate gives |
| “Do you have any illness or disability?” | Health is private, and disability discrimination includes denying reasonable accommodation | “Can you do these tasks [list them], with or without adjustments?” and “Do you need any adjustment for this interview?” |
| “Which party or union do you support?” | Not job-related | Nothing |

Two other questions are legal but waste time: “What's your biggest weakness?” and “Where do you see yourself in five years?” Both invite rehearsed answers.

### Make interviews accessible

Ask every candidate, when you invite them, whether they need any adjustment: an accessible room, a ground-floor venue, extra time for a test, a sign language interpreter, a screen-reader-friendly assignment or a video call instead of travel. Arrange it without fuss, and don't let the request influence the assessment.

Every extra round adds days and gives a good candidate another reason to accept a different offer. Most roles need two or three conversations and one work sample.

### Work samples

A short task close to the real work is often the most useful part of the process. Keep it fair:

- Set a realistic time limit, about two to three hours at most for take-home tasks, or pay for longer ones.
- Never ask for real work you will use, such as a full campaign plan or production code.
- Give everyone the same brief, the same time and the same scoring guide.

### Check references with consent

Before you make an offer, or as a condition of it, speak to at least two references. Ask the candidate first, and never contact a current employer without their explicit permission. Use the same short set of questions for each referee and record the answers in the [reference check report](/toolkit/reference-check-report/). Treat references as one input among many: weigh specific examples, not general praise or criticism.
