You posted a job and received 250 applications. Reading the first 20 and stopping is unfair to everyone else; reading every CV in full takes days. A simple system gets you to a fair shortlist in a few hours.

## Set knockout criteria first

Before anyone opens a CV, the hiring manager and recruiter agree two to four knockout criteria in writing. A knockout is something the person must have to do the job, and that you can see in an application.

| Good knockouts | Never use as knockouts |
| --- | --- |
| A licence or registration the law or the role requires (for example a driving licence for a driver) | Gender, age, marital status, religion, caste or community |
| The ability to work where the job is done, if it can't be done remotely | A photo or appearance |
| A core skill the role uses every day, shown by past work | The name of a college or past employer |
| The language the work is done in | A gap in employment, on its own |

<Callout type="tip" title="Ask for less on your application form">
Remove photo, date of birth, marital status, father's name and ID numbers from your application form. You don't need them to judge skills, they invite bias, and every extra field is more personal data you must protect. Collect identity details after an offer, when you need them.
</Callout>

Two habits keep the passes honest:

- **Record a one-line reason** for every rejection in passes 2 and 3, tied to the criteria (“no experience running paid campaigns”), not to impressions (“not a fit”).
- **Calibrate with a second reader.** Once per role, ask a colleague to re-screen ten CVs you rejected. If they would have kept several, your criteria or your reading need fixing.

### If you use software to screen

Applicant tracking systems and AI tools that rank CVs often learn from past hiring decisions, so they can repeat old biases at scale. Use them to sort and search, not to reject people automatically. Check regularly who the tool screens out, keep a person responsible for each decision, and be ready to explain why someone was rejected.

## Read CVs fairly

Many “red flags” in CV-reading folklore are really snap judgements. Read for evidence of the skills you need.

Strong signals are worth noting too: specific outcomes with numbers, growing responsibility over time, and an application written for your role rather than sent to everyone.

<Callout type="tip" title="Worked example">
A candidate has had three jobs in three years, but each move was to a bigger role and a larger company. That pattern suggests ambition more than restlessness. Don't reject on tenure alone: ask in the phone screen what they were looking for each time, and whether your role offers it.
</Callout>

## Run a 15-minute phone screen

The phone screen checks the basics both sides need before an interview. Use the same questions for every candidate for the role.

<Figure title="A 15-minute phone screen" caption="Times are minutes from the start of the call. The last two minutes, on next steps, matter most to the candidate." wide>

What to say:

1. “Thanks for applying. I have about 15 minutes. Is now a good time?”
2. “We're [company] and we do [one line]. This role is about [main outcome], and the range is [pay range].”
3. “Walk me through your current role in two minutes. What do you own?”
4. “Tell me about one thing you built or achieved that you're proud of. What was your part?”
5. “What are you looking for in pay, and what's your notice period? Is the joining date flexible?”
6. “What would you like to know about us?”
7. “Thanks. You'll hear from me by [date] either way.”

### The salary question

It is common in India to ask for current CTC. If you ask, use the answer to understand expectations, never to pay someone below the role's band. If a person earns ₹8 lakh and the role is worth ₹12 lakh, offer what the role is worth. Anchoring on past pay carries old pay gaps into your company, and the Code on Wages requires equal pay for the same or similar work regardless of gender. Sharing your range first, then asking for expectations, avoids most of the problem.

## Handle candidate data with care

CVs, phone numbers, salary details and ID documents are personal data. Treat them the way you'd want your own handled.

<Callout type="law" title="What the law says about candidate data">
- **Now:** the Information Technology (IT) Act's rules on sensitive personal data (2011) apply to items such as bank account details, health and medical records and biometrics. Before collecting them you need written consent, the person must know the purpose and who will receive the data, and you can't keep it longer than needed.
- **From May 2027:** the main duties of the Digital Personal Data Protection (DPDP) Act, 2023 apply under the phased timetable in the DPDP Rules, 2025. They include a clear notice whenever you ask for consent, erasure once the purpose is served (unless another law requires you to keep it), reasonable security safeguards and a valid contract with any vendor who processes data for you.
- **Not yet settled:** the Act lets employers use personal data “for the purposes of employment” without consent, but whether that covers job applicants who aren't yet employees is unclear. Until that is settled, give candidates a notice and take their consent for recruitment and background checks.
</Callout>

What to collect, and when:

| Stage | Collect | Don't collect yet |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Application | CV, contact details, answers to job-related questions | Photo, date of birth, marital status, family details, ID numbers, bank details |
| Interviews | Interview notes and scores tied to the criteria | Opinions on personal life, appearance or background |
| Offer and checks | Address, ID proof and education and employment records for verification, with consent | Health data, unless the job genuinely requires a fitness check |
| Joining | Bank details, provident fund details (Universal Account Number, UAN), nominations, emergency contact | Anything you have no use for |

Practical steps:

- **Publish a short privacy notice** on your careers page and application form: what you collect, why, who sees it, how long you keep it and how to ask for deletion.
- **Keep CVs in one place you control**, such as your applicant tracking system (ATS) or a restricted shared drive, not personal phones or chat groups.
- **Share only with the people deciding**, and only the parts they need.
- **Set a retention period** for unsuccessful candidates in your notice (for example, six months, or longer only if they agree to be considered for future roles), then delete.
- **Act on requests** to update or delete data promptly, and keep a note that you did.
