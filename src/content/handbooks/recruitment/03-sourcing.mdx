Posting a job on a portal, receiving 400 CVs and reading the first 20 isn't sourcing. Sourcing means knowing which channel works for which role, going where the right people are, and spending your money and time accordingly.

## Match the channel to the role

| Channel | Works well for | How it is usually paid for | Watch out for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Large job portals (Naukri, foundit, Indeed) | Entry and mid-level roles, high volume | Varies by portal: paid job slots, CV database access or sponsored listings, sometimes a free tier | Volume without fit; you need firm screening |
| LinkedIn (posts and direct outreach) | Experienced, specialist and senior roles, people not actively looking | Free and paid options, including paid job promotion and recruiter licences | Outreach takes time and must be personal |
| Employee referrals | All levels, especially hard-to-fill roles | A referral bonus you set | People from the same networks as your team |
| Fresher platforms and campuses (Internshala, college placement cells) | Interns and freshers | Free and paid options; campus visits | Limiting yourself to a few well-known colleges |
| Hyperlocal and frontline apps (such as Apna) | Field, delivery, retail and support roles | Free and paid options | Verify identity and keep middlemen out |
| Community groups (alumni, professional and local groups) | Local roles and niche skills | Free | Don't post candidates' personal details |
| Recruitment agencies | Senior, niche or confidential roles, or a city you don't know | A percentage of the hire's annual pay, usually after joining | Cost, and quality that varies by agency |

Pricing changes often, so check each platform's current plans before you commit, and start with the free options to learn which channels respond.

## Run a referral programme that stays fair

Referrals are often the quickest and cheapest way to reach good people, because your team knows the work and the culture. The catch is that people tend to refer people like themselves, so a team that hires mostly through referrals slowly narrows. Run referrals alongside open channels, and use the same criteria for everyone.

Rules that keep the programme fair and alive:

- **Publish the rules:** which roles qualify, the bonus amount by level, when it is paid and who is not eligible (for example, the hiring manager for their own team).
- **Keep referrers off the panel** for the person they referred.
- **Ask the referrer to check first** that the person is happy to be contacted, and only then share their details.
- **Close the loop every time.** A referrer who hears nothing won't refer again.
- **Extend it to alumni and advisers** if you like; they know how you work.
- **Compare referral hires with other channels** each quarter, including who they are, so you notice if your intake is narrowing.

## Use agencies on clear terms

Put these terms in the agency agreement before you share a JD:

1. **Fee basis:** the percentage, and exactly what it applies to: annual fixed pay or total cost to company (CTC), with or without variable pay and one-time bonuses.
2. **When the fee is due:** after the candidate joins, not when the offer is made, with payment terms in days.
3. **Replacement period:** a free replacement or refund if the hire leaves within an agreed period.
4. **Candidate ownership:** how long a candidate submitted by the agency counts as theirs, so you don't pay twice.
5. **No fees from candidates:** the agency must never charge candidates for the job.
6. **Data protection:** how the agency stores, shares and deletes candidates' data.

<Callout type="law" title="Agencies handle personal data on your behalf">
From May 2027, when the main duties of the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 apply, an organisation may use another firm to process personal data for it only under a valid contract (section 8(2)). Agencies, assessment vendors and background verification firms all fall in this group, so put data terms in writing now.
</Callout>

## Write outreach people answer

People who aren't looking for a job rarely reply to a generic message. Spend two minutes making each one specific to the person: what you noticed about their work, and why this role fits it.

> **Subject:** Your work on [specific project or post], and a role at [company]
>
> Hi [name],
>
> I read your [post, project or talk] on [topic], especially [one specific point]. We're [one line on the company] and looking for someone to [specific outcome].
>
> The role is [title], [location or remote], [pay range]. If you're curious, I'd be glad to share more. If the timing isn't right, I'd still like to stay in touch.
>
> [Your name, role, company email]

Send it from a company account, include the pay range, and take “no” for an answer.

## Track what each channel delivers

Applications are easy to count and tell you little. Track hires, cost and whether the people stayed, channel by channel.

Review the numbers every quarter and keep a simple table per channel: applications, shortlisted, interviewed, hired, total cost, cost per hire and how many hires are still with you after six months. Set your own channel mix from this data rather than a fixed rule. ## Protect candidates from fake job offers

Fraudsters copy real company names to collect “registration fees” from job seekers. Protect candidates and your reputation:

- State on your careers page and in every job post that you never charge candidates for applications, interviews, training or offers.
- Send offers and interview invitations only from your company domain, and list the official addresses on your careers page.
- Make sure agencies and vendors follow the same rule, in writing.
- If someone reports a fake offer in your name, warn candidates publicly and report it to the police.
