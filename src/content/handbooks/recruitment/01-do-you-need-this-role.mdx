The most expensive hire is the one you didn't need. Before anyone writes a job description, answer three questions: is the gap real, can it be closed without a new person, and can you afford this person for at least a year?

## Start with a one-sentence case

Ask the hiring manager to complete this sentence and put it in writing:

> “We need a **[role]** because **[specific gap]**, and we expect them to deliver **[measurable outcome]** within **[timeframe]**.”

If nobody can finish the sentence, you are not ready to hire. The sentence also becomes the opening of your [job description](/handbooks/recruitment/writing-job-descriptions/) and the first goal in the new hire's [30-60-90 day plan](/handbooks/recruitment/onboarding-day-1-to-90/).

<Callout type="tip" title="Worked example">
The sales team says it needs two more people because revenue has grown and the team hasn't. Before approving two posts, ask what work is not getting done. If reps spend hours updating spreadsheets, a customer relationship management (CRM) tool may free more time than a new hire would. If the real gap is enterprise deals nobody can close, one experienced seller may be worth more than two juniors.
</Callout>

## Run the headcount test

Work through the questions in order. Each “no” is a cheaper answer than a hire.

Questions that sharpen the test:

- What exactly is not getting done today, and what does that cost the business each month?
- Who is doing this work now, and what would they stop doing if the role existed?
- Is the gap about **capacity** (more of the same work) or **capability** (a skill nobody has)?
- What would we expect this person to have delivered after 90 days and after a year?

## Choose how to engage the work

The label in a contract doesn't decide what the relationship is: how the work is done does. Pick the form that matches the work, then document it honestly.

| Option | Use it when | Your main obligations | Main risk |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Permanent employee | Ongoing work that needs your direction and fits into a team | Appointment letter, wages, Employees' Provident Fund (EPF), Employees' State Insurance (ESI) and gratuity where they apply, leave, the Labour Codes | Slow and costly to undo if the hire is wrong |
| Fixed-term employee | Time-bound work: a project, a season, a launch | The same terms as comparable permanent staff, plus gratuity after one year | Repeated renewals for ongoing work |
| Contractor or freelancer | A defined piece of work the person controls and delivers | A clear contract for services; tax deducted at source (TDS) on payments where it applies | Treating them like staff turns the contract into employment |
| Staffing agency (third-party payroll) | Volume or short-notice hiring, especially frontline roles | Agency fees and a written agreement; check that wages and statutory dues are paid on time | Quality varies by agency |
| Intern | A genuine learning programme with a clear end date | Clear terms, stipend, supervision | Using “intern” as a label for a regular job |

<Callout type="law" title="Fixed-term employees under the Labour Codes">
A fixed-term employee must get the same hours, wages, allowances and benefits as a comparable permanent worker, with statutory benefits in proportion to service. Gratuity is payable after one year of service under the contract, instead of five. Sources: Industrial Relations Code, 2020 and Code on Social Security, 2020, in force from 21 November 2025.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning" title="Labels don't decide status">
If a “consultant” works your hours, reports to your manager, uses your laptop and does the same work as your employees, treat the arrangement as employment and take advice. The [contractor or employee chapter](/handbooks/tds/contractor-or-employee/) explains the tax side. If you engage workers through a contractor, the contract labour provisions of the Occupational Safety, Health and Working Conditions (OSH) Code may also apply; see the [labour law handbook](/handbooks/labour-law/contracts-and-fixed-term-employment/).
</Callout>

Check how your CTC is built before you add anything. Many Indian employers put employer PF, gratuity and insurance inside CTC; some don't. ESI applies only to employees earning up to ₹21,000 a month (₹25,000 for persons with disability), so it usually doesn't add to the cost of a ₹6 lakh hire. Use the [EPF](/toolkit/epf-calculator/) and [ESI](/toolkit/esi-calculator/) calculators for the statutory amounts.

### Can you sustain it for a year?

A hire is a commitment, not a trial. Confirm three things before you approve:

1. The pay fits a defined band for the role (use the [salary band designer](/toolkit/salary-band-designer/)), so you don't create a pay gap with people already doing similar work.
2. The budget covers at least 12 months of year-one cost, not just the first quarter.
3. Someone has time to manage and onboard the person. A new hire with no manager time rarely reaches full output.

<Callout type="note" title="Why the wrong hire costs more than a salary">
A wrong hire costs months of a role done badly, the manager's time spent correcting work, strain on the team and a second hiring round. Take time to define the role and decide carefully. If a hire isn't working, raise it early through honest probation feedback, as described in the [onboarding chapter](/handbooks/recruitment/onboarding-day-1-to-90/).
</Callout>
