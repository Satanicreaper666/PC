## How the calculation works

Section 392 asks the employer to deduct tax at source (TDS) when salary is paid, at the average rate of tax on the employee's estimated salary income for the tax year. Payroll systems do this by recalculating the whole year every month and spreading what is left over the remaining months. Each month's TDS therefore corrects for anything that changed: a raise, a bonus, a new joiner's previous salary or a late declaration.

## Slab rates for tax year 2026-27

Budget 2026-27 left personal income-tax rates and slabs unchanged. The new regime slabs are written into section 202(1) of the 2025 Act.

| Taxable income (new regime, default) | Rate |
| --- | --- |
| Up to ₹4,00,000 | Nil |
| ₹4,00,001 to ₹8,00,000 | 5% |
| ₹8,00,001 to ₹12,00,000 | 10% |
| ₹12,00,001 to ₹16,00,000 | 15% |
| ₹16,00,001 to ₹20,00,000 | 20% |
| ₹20,00,001 to ₹24,00,000 | 25% |
| Above ₹24,00,000 | 30% |

| Taxable income (old regime, employee below 60) | Rate |
| --- | --- |
| Up to ₹2,50,000 | Nil |
| ₹2,50,001 to ₹5,00,000 | 5% |
| ₹5,00,001 to ₹10,00,000 | 20% |
| Above ₹10,00,000 | 30% |

In the old regime the nil band is ₹3 lakh for residents aged 60 to 79 and ₹5 lakh for those aged 80 or more. The new regime has no separate bands by age.

### Surcharge and cess

Surcharge applies only when income is above ₹50 lakh: 10% above ₹50 lakh, 15% above ₹1 crore and 25% above ₹2 crore. In the old regime it rises to 37% above ₹5 crore; the new regime caps it at 25%. Health and education cess of 4% is then added to the tax plus any surcharge, in both regimes.

## The rebate and marginal relief

Section 156 gives resident individuals a rebate that cancels tax on lower incomes. In the new regime, if taxable income is ₹12 lakh or less, the rebate is the full tax, up to ₹60,000. In the old regime, the limit is ₹5 lakh and the rebate is up to ₹12,500. Non-residents don't get the rebate.

Income just over ₹12 lakh would otherwise jump from nil tax to more than ₹60,000. Marginal relief prevents that: the tax can't be more than the amount by which income exceeds ₹12 lakh. The relief stops mattering at about ₹12.7 lakh of taxable income, where the normal slab tax becomes the lower figure.

<Callout type="note" title="What ₹12.75 lakh really means">
The often-quoted "no tax up to ₹12.75 lakh" is ₹12 lakh of taxable income plus the ₹75,000 standard deduction. It applies to taxable salary, which is not the same as CTC: the employer's PF contribution and a gratuity provision are part of CTC but aren't taxed each month. Other income such as interest counts towards the ₹12 lakh too.
</Callout>

## A worked example

**Example.** Priya is a resident employee in Bengaluru on the new regime. Her taxable salary for tax year 2026-27 is ₹18,00,000, after leaving out the employer's PF contribution. She has no other income and nothing was deducted in earlier months.

| Step | Amount |
| --- | --- |
| Taxable salary | ₹18,00,000 |
| Less standard deduction | ₹75,000 |
| Taxable income | ₹17,25,000 |
| Tax at slab rates (no rebate: income is above ₹12 lakh) | ₹1,45,000 |
| Add 4% cess | ₹5,800 |
| Tax for the year | ₹1,50,800 |
| Monthly TDS (÷ 12) | ₹12,567 |

### Would the old regime have been better for Priya?

Suppose Priya's basic pay is ₹75,000 a month, her HRA is ₹30,000 a month and she pays ₹25,000 a month in rent. Her HRA exemption is the least of the HRA received (₹3,60,000), rent minus 10% of salary (₹3,00,000 − ₹90,000 = ₹2,10,000) and 50% of salary for Bengaluru (₹4,50,000), so ₹2,10,000. She also claims ₹1,50,000 of investments under the old section 80C, ₹25,000 of health insurance and ₹2,400 of professional tax (example figure; it depends on the state).

### When the old regime can win

The old regime has higher rates but allows more deductions, so it only wins when the deductions are large. Assuming salary is the only income and the employee is resident and below 60:

| Taxable salary | New regime tax with cess | Old regime is cheaper only if exemptions and deductions (beyond the ₹50,000 standard deduction) exceed about |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Up to ₹12.75 lakh | Nil | Never: the new regime already costs nothing |
| ₹15 lakh | ₹97,500 | ₹5.44 lakh |
| ₹18 lakh | ₹1,50,800 | ₹6.42 lakh |
| ₹20 lakh | ₹1,92,400 | ₹7.08 lakh |
| ₹25 lakh to ₹50 lakh | ₹3,19,800 at ₹25 lakh | ₹8 lakh |

Large HRA claims in expensive cities, home-loan interest on a self-occupied house (up to ₹2 lakh in the old regime) and full use of the ₹1.5 lakh investment deduction are what usually push someone past these figures.

<Callout type="tip">
Don't advise an employee which regime to choose. Show them both numbers, using their own declarations, and let them decide. Their final choice is made in their tax return, so a different choice at filing only changes their refund or the tax they pay then.
</Callout>

## Other income, a second job and perquisites

### Income the employee asks you to include

An employee can give you Form 122 with salary from another employer in the same year, other income (interest or rent, for example), tax already deducted or collected on that income, and any loss from house property. Section 392(4) says you must take these into account. It is optional for the employee, but it stops a large tax bill at filing time.

There is a limit: section 392(4)(b) lets you reduce salary TDS only for a loss from house property and for tax already deducted or collected elsewhere. Losses from shares or a business can't reduce salary TDS. In the new regime a house property loss can't be set against salary at all, so it doesn't reduce TDS either.

### Perquisites and the employer's option

Taxable perquisites, such as a company car used for personal travel, are added to salary at the values set by the Income-tax Rules, 2026 ([chapter 3](/handbooks/tds/ctc-design-and-perquisites/)). For non-monetary perquisites, section 392(2) lets the employer pay the tax itself instead of deducting it from the employee. That tax is treated as TDS. Decide this in writing before the year starts, because it changes the employee's take-home pay.

### Correcting the year as you go

Section 392(5)(c) lets you increase or reduce later deductions in the same tax year to correct an earlier excess or shortfall. Use it every month rather than waiting for March, when a large catch-up deduction can wipe out an employee's pay.
