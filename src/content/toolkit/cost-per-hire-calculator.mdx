Count people who actually joined, not offers made. An offer that is declined costs money but produces no hire, which is exactly why it belongs in the cost and not in the count.

## What to count

| Cost | Type | How to find it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Job boards, paid posts and ads | External | Invoices from finance for the period |
| Agency and search fees | External | Invoices; count fees for people who joined in the period |
| Referral bonuses | External | Payroll records of referral payouts |
| Assessments, background checks, medicals | External | Vendor invoices |
| Candidate travel, relocation, joining bonuses | External | Expense claims and payroll |
| Employer branding and hiring events | External | Careers site, campus drives, event costs |
| Recruiting team | Internal | Their CTC × share of time spent on hiring |
| Recruiting software | Internal | Annual subscription of the applicant tracking system and tools |
| Interviewers' time | Internal | Interview hours × an hourly cost (annual CTC ÷ about 2,000 hours) |

## Reading the result

Cost per hire is most useful as a trend in your own company. Compare it with last year, between hiring channels and between role families, such as sales and engineering.

A low cost per hire is not good on its own. If cheaper hiring brings people who leave within a year, the [attrition cost calculator](/toolkit/attrition-cost-calculator/) will show the bill arriving later. Read cost per hire next to [first-year attrition and quality of hire](/toolkit/hr-metrics-calculator/).

<Callout type="warning" title="Common mistakes">
  <ul>
    <li>Counting only agency fees, which hides what your own team costs.</li>
    <li>Dividing by offers made instead of people who joined.</li>
    <li>Mixing periods, such as a full year of costs against six months of joiners.</li>
    <li>Comparing your number with a figure from a blog without knowing what it counted.</li>
  </ul>
</Callout>
